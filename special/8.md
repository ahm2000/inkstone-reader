# Special Chapter 8: The Breathless Country

You did not need a high security clearance or a red desk telephone to feel what had just happened. From the brightest boulevards of Shanghai to the quietest county roads in Handong, everyday people stopped in their tracks and felt the entire country hold its breath.

Along the Bund in Shanghai, hundreds of late-night walkers, tourists, and tired office workers froze along the stone railing beside the Huangpu River.

"Hey, look at the river," a young delivery rider muttered, lowering his helmet visor as he pointed down at the dark water. "Not a single barge is moving. Even the sightseeing ferries shut off their music."

Beside him, a middle-aged taxi driver stepped out of his cab, leaving his door open as traffic behind him came to a complete standstill beneath a row of solid red lights. He tilted his head back toward the winter sky. High above the glowing towers of Lujiazui, dark, silent silhouettes hovered steadily against the low clouds.

"My radio just died," the taxi driver said softly, his voice carrying a mix of awe and unease. "Every channel, even the navigation map on my dashboard, just paused. What is going on tonight? Did some major exercise start?"

"That is no exercise," an old retired sailor standing near the promenade murmured, his eyes fixed on the sleek gray cutters blocking the river mouth. "Exercises have notices and loud horns. Look at how quiet it is. Whatever just happened, somebody at the very top is furious."

Hundreds of kilometers away in Yunxi County, the night was usually peaceful by 9:40 PM, save for the distant hum of long-haul freight trucks rolling along the provincial expressway outside town.

Inside a small noodle shop on Old Street, Old Wu paused with his bamboo ladle halfway over a steaming pot of broth. He frowned and tilted his head toward the open front door.

"Strange," Old Wu muttered to the two neighbors sitting at his front table. "Why did the expressway go completely quiet?"

Down the street, Wang Lei had just parked his silver delivery van outside Mingde Hardware after dropping off a late load of copper fittings. He stepped down from the cab, zipping up his dark blue jacket, and saw Acting Station Director Wei Kun walking briskly out of Old Street Police Station with a puzzled frown.

"Director Wei!" Wang Lei called out, gesturing toward the distant overpass where a glowing amber line of hazard lights stretched as far as the eye could see. "My dispatcher just texted me that every highway ramp into Jingzhou is sealed by unmarked black trucks. Did a fugitives sweep start?"

Wei Kun stared down at his silent police radio, then looked up at the dark sky. "Our station got no notice at all, Wang Lei. Keep your van parked tonight and head home early. Nobody is going anywhere until those ramps open."

Just two doors down from the hardware shop, the lights inside Chen Jianguo and Liu Meifang's courtyard were already off. Chen Xuan's parents slept soundly and safely in their warm rooms, completely unaware that six relaxed-looking neighbors strolling calmly along Old Street with warm tea flasks were keeping every alley sealed tighter than a fortress.

Far to the south, on a foggy fishing dock near the strait, a weathered ferryman shut off his diesel engine and stepped onto the wooden bow, staring out at a line of sleek gray cutters glowing white through the mist.

"Harbor control, this is Ferry Seven," he called into his crackly handheld radio. "Are we holding for a storm?"

The harbor master's voice came back thin and shaken. "No storm, Old Chen. Tie your rope to the piling and stay on the dock. Even the wind feels afraid to blow tonight."

And back in Handong's provincial capital of Jingzhou, inside the bright emergency operations hall, Party Secretary Sha Ruijin stood shoulder to shoulder with the Provincial Governor and Municipal Secretary Li Dakang. For once, there was no faction rivalry, no sharp debate, and no political pit-digging between the two top leaders of the province.

All three men simply stared in stunned silence at the giant digital transit board on the wall. Every expressway artery, every railway line, every river port, and every regional airport across Handong glowed solid red.

"Secretary Sha," the Provincial Governor said quietly, his voice unusually steady and grave. "I called the Transport Ministry. They told me they did not issue this lockdown."

Li Dakang wiped his forehead with a white handkerchief, his eyes wide. "First the black seal froze our customs desks ten minutes ago. Now the entire province just stopped moving without a single phone call from Beijing. Secretary Sha, who in this country has the power to stop the sky and the highways all at once?"

Sha Ruijin did not answer. He slowly folded his hands behind his back, thinking of the hidden benefactor who had once rescued their province's banks without asking for a single seat or title.

"Whoever it is," Sha Ruijin said softly, "we had better pray that the person who angered him is not standing inside Handong."
