# Chapter 80: The Quiet Watch Across the Street

When Chen Xuan carried the two red bags of apples and pears back into the sixth-floor apartment around noon, the living room was already loud with the warm clatter of lunch.

Wu Lanying had set a steaming platter of soy-braised river fish in the center of the folding table, flanked by stir-fried spinach, crisp pickled radish, and a clay pot of ginger rib soup. Chen Mingde sat at the head of the table with healthy color back in his cheeks, laughing as Chen Hao and Chen Yuan reached across each other for the crispiest piece of fish tail.

"Where did all this fruit come from?" Liu Meifang asked, wiping her hands on her cotton apron as she took the heavy bags from her son. "These apples are huge. Did you spend half your monthly pay at the corner stall?"

"Wang Lei dropped them off," Chen Xuan said easily, setting the bottle of dark vinegar beside the soy sauce. "He heard Second Uncle was back home and pedaled straight over on his delivery tricycle. He would not even let me pay him."

Chen Mingde paused with his chopsticks halfway to his bowl, his expression softening. "That boy Wang Lei has a good heart. Tell him to come upstairs for dinner this weekend. Your aunt will cook braised pork ribs for him."

"I already invited him," Chen Xuan replied with a quiet smile, pulling out a wooden stool beside his father, Chen Jianguo. "He said he has three more appliance runs before evening, so he will come by on Saturday."

Chen Jianguo ladled clear ginger soup into Chen Mingde's bowl and shook his head in mild wonder. "I still cannot get over how smooth everything went at the hospital this week. No waiting in hallways, no fighting for a specialist bed, and the head nurse even called this morning to check on your blood pressure."

"Times are getting better," Wu Lanying said cheerfully, nudging a piece of tender fish belly onto Chen Xuan's rice. "Eat more, Xuan. You have been running errands all morning and you look thinner than last month. That office job of yours must be wearing you down."

"It is just paperwork, Second Aunt," Chen Xuan said, lifting his bowl. "Nothing heavy. Mostly sitting at a desk and drinking tea."

Chen Hao grinned through a mouthful of rice. "Cousin Xuan always says that, yet he never looks tired. Maybe I should look for an simple office job after graduation too."

"Finish your homework first before talking about graduation," Chen Mingde teased, tapping the boy's knuckles lightly with the blunt end of his chopsticks. Laughter rolled around the small folding table, blending with the clink of porcelain spoons and the distant hum of a neighbor's television through the thin hallway wall.

For the next forty minutes, Chen Xuan simply ate, listened, and refilled bowls. He peeled an apple for his mother, listened to his father complain about the price of winter cabbages, and let the simple warmth of the sixth-floor apartment settle around him. Nothing in this room knew about frozen provincial accounts or silent power shifts in the capital, and he intended to keep it that way.

Once the dishes were stacked in the kitchen sink and the family settled onto the worn sofa with sliced pears, Chen Xuan poured himself a mug of warm green tea and stepped out onto the small enclosed balcony to open the sliding glass window.

Fresh midday air rushed in, carrying the faint scent of roasted chestnuts from the street corner and damp laundry drying on the neighbor's rack. Below him, Yunxi Street moved at its usual unhurried pace. An elderly woman pushed a folding grocery cart past the barbershop. Two schoolchildren raced each other toward the bus stop.

Chen Xuan rested one elbow on the painted iron rail and let his gaze drift across the narrow road.

Exactly a short distance from the courtyard gate, beside the shuttered steel door of an old hardware shop, two figures stood completely still in the band of shade beneath the plane trees. Both wore plain black tactical jackets, dark trousers, and smooth calm faces that left no skin exposed. There were no badges on their chests, no numbers on their sleeves, and no visible weapons.

A delivery rider rolled past within three meters of them without glancing twice. The two figures blended so naturally into the deep shadow of the awning that simple eyes slid right over them. Even their breathing seemed measured to the rhythm of the street.

Chen Xuan lifted his gaze slightly to the low concrete roof across the lane. Near the rusted water tank, a third figure in plain black gear crouched behind the parapet, scanning the east intersection for two seconds before vanishing cleanly from sight. The Black Guard held a quiet distance with silent precision, close enough to erase any threat before it reached the courtyard gate, yet far enough that no neighbor would ever feel watched.

In his trouser pocket, Chen Xuan's plain black phone vibrated once. He set his mug on the narrow ledge and opened the encrypted screen.

A single brief report from Number 1 sat at the top of the log.

"Young Master, the White Guard outer ring has pulled back three blocks as ordered. The Black Guard holds the inner quiet line around Yunxi without incident. Beijing and Handong began formal interrogations this afternoon. No outside unit has crossed the boundary."

Chen Xuan's thumb moved quickly across the glass.

"Good. Stay invisible. Do not disturb the street."

"Understood, Young Master," came the instant reply.

Chen Xuan locked the phone and slipped it back into his pocket. Down by the hardware shop, neither of the two black-clad figures looked up toward the sixth floor, yet the subtle shift in their posture told him the quiet order had already rippled through the perimeter.

"Xuan, do not stand in the draft too long," Liu Meifang called from the living room. "Your pear slices are going to turn brown if you do not eat them."

"Coming, Mom," Chen Xuan answered.

He took one last sip of tea, slid the balcony window halfway shut, and walked back into the bright, noisy living room where his family waited.
