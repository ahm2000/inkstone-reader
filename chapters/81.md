# Chapter 81: The Interrogation of Division Chief Zhao Dehan

Cold white light from recessed LED panels hummed steadily over the basement questioning room in Beijing. The terrazzo floor shone from a recent mop, carrying the sharp, sterile scent of plain bleach. Between the two bolted chairs stood a bare steel table holding a single paper cup of lukewarm water and one thick brown folder.

Across the table, Zhao Dehan sat rigid in his gray zippered jacket. Until yesterday, he had been the powerful Division Chief of the Project Approval Office at the National Energy Administration, a man whose signature decided whether billion-yuan coal mines and power plants lived or died. Today, his collar was damp with sweat despite the chilled air from the ceiling vents.

He folded his hands neatly on the steel surface, trying to summon the calm authority of a ministry veteran, though a faint twitch in his left thumb gave him away.

"Investigator Gao," Zhao Dehan said behind a thin, practiced smile. "I have served at the Energy Administration for thirty-one years. Everyone in the ministry knows how I live. I ride an old bicycle to work, eat plain noodles for lunch, and live in a sixty-square-meter staff apartment from the nineties. If there is a problem with a power project in Handong Province, my office will gladly cooperate. Locking a division chief in a basement over wild gossip from Jingzhou City is completely out of line."

Across from him sat Gao Zhengyi, a senior disciplinary investigator with the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection. Gao Zhengyi did not raise his voice. He did not even look up as he untied the cotton string on the brown folder.

"Plain noodles," Gao Zhengyi repeated evenly. "And a bicycle. You played the humble farmer's son very well, Zhao Dehan. You even sent three hundred yuan a month back to your old village so the neighbors would praise your thrift."

"That is simply who I am," Zhao Dehan insisted, his chin lifting slightly. "My father taught me never to forget my roots. You can check my salary card. There is barely forty thousand yuan on it."

Gao Zhengyi opened the folder and slid a glossy photograph across the cold metal table.

It showed a quiet two-story villa in a gated suburban compound outside Beijing. The second photo showed a living room where a false plaster wall had been pried open. Behind the drywall stood a solid wall of red banknotes, stacked floor to ceiling in tight plastic wraps. The third photo showed a wide wooden bed with its mattress lifted off, revealing a deep wooden box packed solid with cash.

"200 million yuan," Gao Zhengyi said quietly. "200 million and 395k yuan, to be exact. The bank clerks brought in sixteen counting machines from the nearby branch. Four of those machines burned out their motors before they finished counting your bedroom."

All color drained from Zhao Dehan's face. His folded hands separated, palms pressing flat against the steel table to stop them from shaking. For ten long seconds, the only sound in the room was the low hum of the ceiling lights.

"I never spent it," Zhao Dehan whispered, his voice cracking into a thin rasp. "Investigator Gao, I swear on my ancestors, I never spent a single fen of that money. Not one cent!"

"That does not make it legal, Zhao Dehan."

"I was scared!" Tears spilled suddenly down his sunken cheeks, wiping away thirty years of bureaucratic polish in an instant. "My family was poor when I was a child. So poor we could not afford salt. When they started bringing boxes of cash to that villa, I told myself I would just look at it. Just smell the paper and know I was never going to starve again. I did not dare buy a car. I did not dare wear a nice watch. I am just a farmer's son who lost his mind!"

Gao Zhengyi watched him without sympathy. He pulled a second sheaf of printed records from the folder and laid them neatly beside the photographs.

"Spare me the tears," Gao Zhengyi said. "You did not collect 200 million yuan by crying about salt. You collected it by stamping expedited permits for private coal mines, thermal plants, and land conversions across Handong Province. Now look at page four."

Zhao Dehan stared through blurred eyes at the stamped approval forms. Every single one bore his crisp blue signature.

"Handong Anti-Corruption Bureau Chief Hou Liangping sent us the seized ledgers from Jingzhou City this morning," Gao Zhengyi continued. "Your name appears beside every major energy transfer over the last four years. If you keep pretending you acted alone, you will carry the weight of an entire cross-provincial ring by yourself."

Zhao Dehan's shoulders slumped forward until his forehead nearly touched the steel table. A dry sob shook his chest.

"It was not my idea," he choked out. "I never had the nerve to ask for money like that on my own."

"Then tell me who built the bridge," Gao Zhengyi said, uncapping his black pen.

Zhao Dehan swallowed hard, wiping his nose on his sleeve. "Ding Yizhen. Former Vice Mayor of Jingzhou City. Four years ago, he came to Beijing for an energy forum and invited me to a private tea room. He knew my daughter was applying to study abroad. He did not hand me cash that first night. He just gave me a key to that suburban villa and told me a friend from Handong wanted to show gratitude."

"Who was the friend?" Gao Zhengyi asked.

"Zhao Ruilong," Zhao Dehan blurted out, the name tumbling out in a rush of panic. "Head of Shanshui Group. Every time Ding Yizhen brought a Handong energy file to my desk, Zhao Ruilong's people delivered another fifty-kilogram crate to the villa. They told me not to worry about inspections because Shanshui Group had full backing from the highest offices in Handong Province."

Gao Zhengyi's pen moved smoothly across the record sheet. "Did you keep track of those deliveries?"

Zhao Dehan nodded frantically. "Inside the false bottom of my old tea tin at the ministry office. Every date, every project code, and every Hong Kong routing number Ding Yizhen gave me in case I ever wanted to move the money out. Take it all. Just write down that I confessed everything."

Gao Zhengyi stood up, sliding the signed pages into the brown folder. The first brick in Handong's wall had just crumbled.
