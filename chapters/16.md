# Chapter 16: The Cold Bench

Across the lane from the station, the old tailor Yu Changshun was ironing a pair of trousers that were already flat.

A shadow filled his doorway.

"Yu Changshun."

The man standing there was as wide as the doorframe, with prominent front teeth. He was Niu Dali, who had been forced to run Hongrui's street crew ever since Yan Tiehu vanished off the ring road the night before.

Yu Changshun kept ironing. "I'm closed." The sign on his door said otherwise.

"Just stopping by to say good morning. Hongrui likes its neighbors to eat well." Niu Dali set a thick brown envelope on the cutting table, though his eyes kept darting nervously toward the street. "Twenty thousand. A little something for your granddaughter's schooling."

Twenty thousand yuan was four months of Yu Changshun's income.

"I didn't do anything to earn this."

"Exactly. That's what it's for. On Friday you saw nothing. Old eyes, old street, too much dust in the air."

He smiled and patted the head of a little girl sitting at the far end of the table.

Yu Changshun's nine-year-old granddaughter, Yu Ning, stayed with him on Saturdays while her mother worked.

Niu Dali looked down at her. "Pretty kid. Every Saturday at four you walk her down the river lane to her mother's workshop, right? Nine years old. Nobody on that lane ever looks out their window."

The iron stopped moving.

Niu Dali straightened and spoke in an almost kindly tone. "Friday you saw nothing. Keep seeing nothing, and she gets home safe every Saturday."

He had not said her name. He had not needed to.

He walked out.

Yu Changshun stood frozen until the heavy footsteps faded. His hands trembled so badly that the steam rising from the iron wobbled.

He picked up the envelope as if it might burn him, dropped it into the drawer beneath his spools of thread, and pushed it shut.

On Friday he had seen the truth right from this doorway. Now he could tell it, or he could keep his granddaughter safe. He had no idea how to do both.

Across the lane, Meng Xiaofang had watched the big man come and go from her bun stall. Her eyes met the old tailor's, and neither of them spoke.

* * *

The screen finally chimed and flashed number thirty-seven.

It had taken an hour and twenty minutes.

Chen Xuan stood and walked to the window. Wei Kun looked up from a stack of forms on his desk.

One of his cheeks was noticeably redder than the other. Cheng Biao had slapped him the night before for giving Chen Mingde a cup of water.

"Could you please tell me the charge against my uncle? And we need the written detention notice."

"Assault. Under review. No visits." Wei Kun's voice was stiff, and he kept his eyes fixed on the paper.

Chen Xuan noticed the faint swelling on his cheek, said nothing about it, and kept his voice polite.

"We'll mail the family notice. You're holding up the line," Wei Kun added quickly.

"The law requires the family to receive the notice within twenty-four hours. I know it isn't up to you, Officer Wei. I don't mind waiting."

Chen Xuan returned to the bench. Wei Kun picked up his desk phone with a damp palm.

"Director Cheng? The nephew is still sitting out here."

Ten minutes later, the steel door at the back of the lobby clicked open, and everyone stood up. The woman with the onions half-rose, weighed down by her sack.

Behind the glass, Wei Kun and his desk partner Zhong Hai stood at attention. The old records clerk, Number Thirty-four, eased himself off his stool. A small audio recorder was already running inside his sleeve.

Cheng Biao, the station director, strolled out with a toothpick tucked between his teeth.

Someone murmured, "That's Director Cheng. That poor family is done for."

Cheng Biao did not glance at Chen Jianguo. His eyes swept over Chen Xuan from his plain sneakers to his neat hair, full of lazy amusement.

"Back again, logistics clerk?"

Chen Xuan screwed the cap onto his thermos and stood up politely. "Director Cheng. We're just waiting on my uncle's detention notice."

"Give them their paper and clear my lobby, Wei Kun. And tell them the visiting room is closed this weekend for pipe repairs." Cheng Biao leaned close to Chen Jianguo's bruised cheek, dropping his voice. "Take your father home, kid. Building 2, sixth floor, no elevator, and the stairwell light has been out since spring. At his age, one bad fall means a year in a hospital bed. Nobody ever asks who happened to be on the stairs."

Chen Jianguo's fists clenched, his whole arm rigid with anger.

Chen Xuan placed a steady hand on his father's sleeve. His expression stayed mild, polite, and entirely unruffled.

"Thank you for the reminder about the stairs, Director Cheng," Chen Xuan said evenly. "We'll be careful. Also, the sign on the wall says Legal Aid Desk. Is a lawyer on duty today?"

"Want a lawyer?" Cheng Biao's lip curled. "Go hire a good one yourself. Three thousand yuan an hour. That's half a clerk's monthly pay."

Chen Xuan gave a small, polite nod. "I see. Thank you, Director Cheng. We'll take the notice and head home."

Cheng Biao let out a short, dismissive laugh. Just another timid little office clerk who folded the moment someone leaned on him. He turned without a second thought, and the steel door clanged shut behind him.

A moment later, a single stamped sheet slid out through the slot under the glass.

Wei Kun stared at the time box. He had written the real time there, 7:50 a.m. Saturday, even though Cheng Biao had ordered him to backdate it to Friday afternoon.

Would the nephew even read that far?
