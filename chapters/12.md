# Chapter 12: Three Options

Qingyan City, the quiet eastern courtyard. Saturday, 8:40 p.m.

"Chief, this is Twenty-three."

Chief An Zhiyuan sat up a little straighter at the sound, setting his tea aside. On the desk in front of him lay a clean sheet of paper with nothing on it yet but his own neat handwriting: *Option One. Option Two.*

"Go ahead, Gong Yi. And close your front door first, I can hear your customers."

"Already closed, Chief. Restocking sign's up." A pot clinked somewhere behind him; he'd clearly answered mid-shift, apron and all. "Two things worth your time, and one thing that just made me laugh out loud behind my own counter."

"Start with the laugh. I could use one."

"Fine. Two off-duty auxiliary officers came in for noodles an hour ago, and one of them was telling the other, loud enough for half the street, that duty officer Wei Kun snuck a cup of hot water through the cell bars to the old hardware man last night. His buddy said, 'You'll get written up for that,' and Wei Kun's friend said, 'Write me up, then. It was cold.' Then he dropped his voice the way you do when you're saying something true and dangerous: 'Director Cheng would've let the old man freeze and called it discipline. That's the difference between a cop and a tyrant with a badge.' Nobody at that table even knows who they were really talking about."

"Hot water," Chief An repeated, and something in his stern face eased for a second. "Keep that officer's name close. Now the other two."

"First, money. Yunxi County approved a six-hundred-forty-million-yuan relocation fund for Old Street. I pulled the public payment records and the business filings (nothing fancy, anyone with a library card could do it), and at least a hundred and ninety million of that never reached a single family. It went to three companies: Hongrui, a demolition outfit registered to Deputy Director Cheng Biao's brother-in-law, and Ankang Private Clinic."

Chief An's pen stopped over the page. "The clinic that wrote the injury note."

"The very same. Attorney Bai's assistant pulled the billing receipt through an ordinary records request. The driver was treated at 1:50 p.m., fifteen minutes before his own arrest was even logged at 2:05, and no police doctor ever laid eyes on him."

"Clean proof," Chief An murmured. "Go on."

"Second thing. The holding notices. I got my hands on two of the earlier families' copies, and both were backdated to the afternoon of the arrest, neat as you like, to hide an illegal overnight lockup. Only one notice in the whole stack tells the truth. The Boss's uncle's. Seven-fifty in the morning, in Wei Kun's own hand."

"So he's the one who wouldn't sign the lie."

"Looks that way, Chief. And the clock is running: Deputy Magistrate Lu controls the money, and the provincial inspection team lands next Wednesday. Every scrap of this came off public shelves. Nobody out there knows we so much as looked."

"Well done. Keep the apron on, and wait for my word."

Chief An clicked the line dark and sat looking at his two empty headings for a long moment.

With forty years behind him, he knew three different ways to end this before sunrise. One phone call to the right man in the provincial capital would turn Yunxi's police bureau inside out before breakfast. It would also be loud and clumsy, and it would bury a dozen ordinary shopkeepers and one honest beat cop along with the men who deserved it.

Above all, Chief An knew his Boss. Chen Xuan didn't want revenge. He wanted his uncle treated fairly, and he wanted it done by the book, because the book was supposed to work for people like his uncle too.

So instead of writing an essay, Chief An picked up his phone and typed something closer to what he'd say out loud if the Boss were sitting across the desk from him.

Yunxi County, Fuxing New Village, Building 2, Room 601. 9:20 p.m.

"Brother, if you stare at that phone any harder it's going to turn into a brick," Chen Hao said from the far end of the sofa, thumbs still flying over a mobile game.

"Just a work message," Chen Xuan said.

In the kitchen, his mother was scrubbing the same soup bowl for the third time, just to keep her hands busy. Chen Xuan scrolled through Chief An's note at the table, reading it the way he read a stack of shipping invoices: slowly, once through for the shape of it, then again for the parts that mattered.

The first part laid out everything Gong Yi had found, from the clinic slip timed fifteen minutes too early to the hundred and ninety million gone missing.

The second part was shorter, and read almost like Chief An talking: *Boss, here's how I see it. We can have our lawyer walk a full, proof-backed complaint into the city's anti-corruption office tonight, with copies to the prosecutor and the police chief. With inspectors landing Wednesday, that forces their hand inside hours, and your uncle's home by morning. Or we sit tight and hand all of it straight to the provincial team Wednesday, which means three more nights in that cell. Or I make one call tonight and have somebody's career ended before dawn, which is fastest and also the loudest. I'd go with the first one myself. One more thing: the duty officer who signed, Wei Kun, wrote the true time and brought your uncle hot water last night when nobody told him to.*

Chen Xuan didn't need five seconds.

With provincial inspectors four days out, Qingyan's city leaders wouldn't dare sit on proof this clean. Clean paperwork, aimed straight at that Wednesday deadline, would crack the cover-up open without anyone bending a single rule.

And that duty officer, a man with nothing to gain and every reason to just sign what he was told, had chosen to write one honest hour and carry a cup of hot water to an old man nobody important would ever thank him for helping. Something warm moved in Chen Xuan's chest at that. There was always somebody, somewhere, quietly doing the right thing when no one was watching.

He typed back with his thumb, the way he'd talk: *Let's go with the first one. Could you add Old Yu's eyewitness statement too? I'll send you a photo now. All the way by the book, no shortcuts, and nobody gets hurt over this.*

A second message followed before he'd even set the phone down: *Also, could someone send Second Uncle a hot meal and a blanket tonight, through the regular visitor window? He's fifty-two and that floor is concrete. And please make sure nobody takes it out on Officer Wei Kun afterward. He did right by a stranger when it would've been easier not to.*

He photographed Old Yu's handwritten page, sent it, and set the phone face-down on the coffee table.

"Why are you smiling like that?" Chen Hao squinted at him over his own screen.

"Am I smiling?"

"You look like you won the lottery."

"Must be Mom's soup."

Back in the quiet courtyard, Chief An read both replies twice, then let out a long breath.

Three ways to end a county's leadership before morning sat on that table, and what had the Boss actually spent half his reply on? A bowl of hot noodles. A blanket. One beat cop's safety.

Something in Chief An's throat went tight, and a rare, gentle smile crossed his lined face.

"Gong Yi," he said into the speaker, "cook up a big bowl of beef noodles, extra egg, and send a thick blanket over to the station's family window yourself, apron and all, strictly through the front door like anyone's relative would. And pass the word down: nobody lays a finger on Officer Wei Kun. Anyone who tries answers to me personally."

"Already boiling the broth, Chief," Gong Yi said, and Chief An could hear the smile in it.

"Attorney Bai. Your turn."

The study door opened. Bai Qingzhou, Number Eleven on the roster, stepped in wearing a neat charcoal suit and carrying a slim black folder, looking as pleased as a man walking into his favorite concert hall.

"Everything's ready, Chief," he said, tapping the folder. "Nineteen pages, in order: the clinic slip, the honest holding notice, the neighbor's signed statement, the street footage, and the fund trail. I'll file it myself at the night desk."

"Through the front door, Bai. Signed receipt, every rule followed, nothing that'll make a clerk raise an eyebrow."

Bai's eyes gleamed behind his silver-rimmed glasses. "Naturally, Chief. When the law itself is sharp enough to cut iron, why would anyone bother swinging a club?"
