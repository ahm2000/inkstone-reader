# Chapter 26: The Paper Wall

At the Yunxi County Labor Inspection Office service hall, Attorney Bai Qingzhou pulled a paper number from the machine by the door. It said eighty-one!

The red screen above Window Four said, "Now serving seventeen."

Bai looked at it for exactly one second. Then he sat in the blue plastic chair nearest the radiator and opened a thick book.

Thirty years ago he had learned that every government hall has the same red numbers and the same blue chairs. Nobody, not even a famous lawyer, gets a better chair!

Around him sat a grandmother with a cloth bag, a delivery rider, and two young men gripping a folded paper that said *unpaid wages*.

The Labor Inspection office is the county office that checks how workers are paid and insured. The hall smelled of tea, cold floor and worry.

"Eighty-one? He will be sitting here until lunch..."

"At least he brought a book..."

"With a coat like that, he must be somebody..."

"Then why is he in that chair?"

Bai turned a page and waited.

Hours passed.

At last, the screen chimed.

"Eighty-one!"

Behind Window Four sat Labor Inspection Officer Gu, a young woman with a tired face, two pens in her hair and a lanyard that had seen better years.

"Which matter?" she asked, without looking up.

"Five papers," said Bai, laying them on the counter one by one: the entrustment (a form that lets a lawyer act for her) signed by the Chens, the girl's temporary caretakers, his law license, the law firm's letter, his ID copy and a one-page written request.

"Lawyers usually forget at least two!" said Officer Gu, who had counted them twice.

"I forgot two for thirty years," said Bai. "Then I decided to stop."

"You are the first lawyer all month who did not ask me to hurry it along by calling someone's cousin," she said, laughing in spite of herself, and almost grudgingly impressed.

"The file room goes to lunch until two, so please come back this afternoon," she added with a sigh. "Sorry."

"Of course," said Bai, and he took his book back to the blue chair.

He was back in that chair at two sharp. Soon after, stamped pages slid out through the slot.

Bai read them in the corridor, standing up, from the first line to the last. The hall noise faded away.

Tang Dawei, forty-one, laborer. Employer: Tiancheng Demolition Company, owner Shen Jinbao, brother-in-law of Cheng Biao. No labor contract. No social insurance ever paid. Three months of wages never paid. A form labeled "voluntary leave," dated just after a fall from a scaffold at a site in Dongqiao Town: *Tang Dawei leaves his work for family reasons and gives up all claims.*

And one line more, from the Dongqiao clinic's emergency register: he had died of that fall, the company never reported it, and it paid a "silence sum," money to keep a family from complaining, to a middleman, the foreman's cousin, who still held every yuan of it.

Getting that money back through a claim could take weeks.

Nobody had ever told his daughter.

Bai closed his eyes for one slow breath. He had read thousands of files, and he still needed that breath.

Then he opened them and did the only thing he knew how to do, which was the lawful thing.

There were three doors, and he knocked on each. First, labor arbitration, where a panel settles pay and injury disputes between workers and bosses, for the unpaid wages. Second, work-injury compensation, the money the law says a company must pay for a worker hurt on the job, even a man it never insured. Third, a report to the County Discipline Commission, the anti-corruption watchdog already working in town, because the owner of Tiancheng was Cheng Biao's brother-in-law and held a thirty-eight-million-yuan Old Street contract.

Three filings, three stamped receipts, less than an hour, and nobody raised a voice.

"A wage complaint, an injury claim and a report to the Discipline Commission, all in one afternoon?" said Officer Gu, reading the last paper twice.

"Each paper goes to the office that owns it, Officer," said Bai. "I only carried them across the hall."

Officer Gu sat up straight. She had a duty now, in writing, with a deadline on it! For once, the paper in her hand was not someone else's problem.

"Officer Cai!" she called. "Get the car, we are going to Dongqiao Road!"

By late afternoon, the Tiancheng Demolition yard was mostly dust and old brick, with a metal gate and a white trailer for an office.

The two officers walked in with a clipboard and a plain notice. For the first time in years, nobody at Tiancheng could say they had not been asked!

"Scaffold permit, please," said Officer Gu, and nobody in the yard could produce one.

"Demolition license, please," said Officer Cai, and the one on the trailer wall had expired in March.

They asked for contracts for the men sitting on bricks in the sun, who looked up with wary eyes. There were none, because the men were paid in envelopes, and the envelopes had no names on them!

"They are really writing it all down..."

"Will we really be paid?"

"Quiet, let them finish..."

"Stop work and fix it," said Officer Gu, taping the notice to the gate, stamp side out. "Everyone gets paid what is owed, and the company will be called in!"

Half an hour later, a white car stopped at the gate. Shen Jinbao got out.

He was a round man in a clean hard hat that had never touched a brick. He read the notice once, then twice, with his lips moving.

Then he took the phone from his pocket and rang his brother-in-law.

Cheng Biao had been suspended ten days ago. He had switched his phone off the same afternoon.

*The number you have dialed is not available.*

"Pick up, Biao, pick up!" Shen Jinbao muttered, and he dialed again, and then again.

For years he had believed his brother-in-law was a wall. He had believed that paperwork was something other people did!

Now the wall was gone, and two officers with a clipboard had just walked in through his front gate.

Where was Cheng Biao? Who would answer for the form? Could a few sheets of paper really do this?

He thought of the scaffold, the form and the middleman. He understood that all three were now written down in the county's own files, with case numbers, and could not be made to disappear.

Silence.

He did not shout. He sat down on a pile of bricks with the phone in both hands and took off his hard hat.

Three filings, a stop-work notice, an expired license and a yard full of men who would finally be paid, and nobody had raised a voice!

That evening, back in his rented apartment in Qingyan, Chen Xuan was washing his scratched green thermos when his phone rang.

"Boss," said Bai Qingzhou, in his calm courtroom voice. "The first answer."

Bai told it plainly, in order, without softening a word. When he reached the one line, Chen Xuan turned off the tap.

The kitchen was very quiet. Chen Xuan stood there with the wet thermos in one hand.

He thought of a girl who folded her blanket into a perfect square. He thought of how she had said "my dad" and then said it again.

For a moment he could not say what he was feeling, only that it was heavy, and that it was about a girl who was only thirteen.

"Mm," he said at last. "Thank you, Attorney Bai. Please keep it lawful, every step."

"Every step, Boss." Bai paused. "Boss, who will tell the girl?"

"I will."

On the other end, Bai was silent for a long moment. When he spoke again, even his courtroom voice was not quite steady.
