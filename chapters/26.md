# Chapter 26: Sunday Dinner Without Second Uncle

By six o'clock on Sunday evening, the folding table in Building 2 of Fuxing New Village held three dishes and a ceramic pot of ginger and pork rib soup. Outside the sixth-floor window, dusk had settled over Yunxi County.

Liu Meifang set the pot onto a cork trivet, then brought an extra white porcelain bowl and bamboo chopsticks from the sideboard. She wiped the clean rim once and placed them at the empty corner facing the window.

"Second Brother likes sitting near the draft when he drinks hot soup," Liu Meifang said quietly. "Even if he is not back tonight, his seat stays ready."

Beside the table, Wu Lanying's knuckles turned white against a wooden stool. Two sleepless nights had left faint hollows under her eyes, and her mouth stayed pressed into a guarded line. When she felt her children watching, she forced her shoulders down and shaped a steady smile.

"You did not need to cook so much, Sister-in-law," Wu Lanying murmured. "We have been eating at your table since Friday."

"Do not talk nonsense," Liu Meifang replied, nudging her toward the bench. "Sit down before the broth cools. Chen Hao, Chen Yuan, pick up your bowls."

Fourteen-year-old Chen Hao sat down with his spine stiff as a board. He knew the silence around his father's hardware shop on Old Street was no ordinary delay. He glanced at his mother's tight jaw, then looked across the table at Chen Xuan, studying his older cousin's face for any crack in that calm.

"Brother Xuan," Chen Hao asked abruptly, his voice low, "is Dad really just helping the station check paperwork like Mom said?"

Wu Lanying's hand froze over the serving spoon. Chen Jianguo dropped his eyes sharply to his rice bowl.

Chen Xuan did not flinch. He placed braised greens into Chen Hao's bowl and met the boy's tense gaze evenly. "Some people on Old Street tried a dirty trick over the shop," Chen Xuan said calmly. "Your dad did nothing wrong. He is coming home tomorrow."

Chen Hao stared at him for two seconds before the rigid line of his shoulders eased. "All right," he muttered, lifting his chopsticks.

Beside him, younger Chen Yuan swung her legs under the bench and stared at the empty white bowl. "Mom, will Dad definitely come home tomorrow afternoon?" she asked earnestly. "My bicycle chain still clicks by the school gate. Dad promised he would oil it and tighten the rear nut on Monday."

Wu Lanying placed the tenderest rib into Chen Yuan's bowl. Her chopsticks trembled by a hair before she steadied her wrist. "Of course he will," she said softly. "Your dad never breaks a promise about your bicycle. Eat your greens too."

"Brother Xuan, can you fix bicycle chains?" Chen Yuan asked.

"Your dad is much better at it than I am," Chen Xuan answered with an easy smile, sliding half a cup of warm water beside Wu Lanying's elbow. "Let him handle it tomorrow."

Wu Lanying looked up, her eyes bright with unshed tears she refused to let fall in front of the children, and took a quiet sip to hide the tremor in her fingers.

At the head of the table, Chen Jianguo had barely touched his rice. His calloused hands lay flat beside his bowl. On Friday night, before Chen Xuan arrived in Yunxi County, Chen Jianguo had hurried to Old Street Station only to be brushed off by duty officers who refused to let him see his younger brother or leave a clean jacket.

He had obeyed Chen Xuan all weekend, staying away from the station. Now Monday morning was hours away.

Chen Jianguo cleared his throat softly so Chen Yuan would not hear the dread in his voice. "Chen Xuan," he said across the rising steam, "tomorrow is Monday. All the offices open at eight. I have been thinking all afternoon."

Liu Meifang froze with her chopsticks in midair. "Jianguo, let the children finish eating first."

"Let me finish, Meifang," Chen Jianguo murmured, his weathered face creased with the worn humility of fifty years avoiding trouble with anyone in a uniform. He looked at his son. "I listened to you all weekend. Still, your Second Uncle has been locked inside for more than forty-eight hours with no word."

"Dad," Chen Xuan said quietly, waiting.

Chen Jianguo swallowed hard, palms pressing the table edge. "First thing tomorrow, I should go down there myself. I can buy two cartons of good cigarettes, wait outside the gate, and beg the station chief or Deng Yongchang's people for a way out. Even if they want the hardware shop lease, we can sign it over. A shop is just bricks and iron. Your Second Uncle is flesh and blood."

Silence dropped over the table like a stone.

Wu Lanying's breath hitched. She set her chopsticks down, her chin trembling. "Big Brother, you cannot do that," she whispered past a tight throat. "You are over fifty. If you beg Deng Yongchang's men outside that gate, Mingde will never forgive himself."

"Pride does not unlock a cell door, Lanying," Chen Jianguo said hoarsely. "In Yunxi County, ordinary people do not win against Hongrui Real Estate or the men who dine with Deng Yongchang. If signing away a lease brings Mingde out whole, I will bow ten times and call it a bargain."

Wu Lanying lowered her gaze and blinked hard so no tear would fall. Chen Hao gripped his chopsticks until his knuckles whitened, his dark eyes burning with helpless anger.

Chen Xuan did not interrupt.

When the room fell quiet, he set his chopsticks neatly across his bowl, took the wooden-handled ladle, and stirred the ginger-radish soup once. He lifted a full ladle of hot broth with two slices of radish and poured it slowly into Chen Jianguo's bowl without spilling a drop.

Only then did Chen Xuan meet his father's worried eyes with a calm certainty heavier than iron.

"Tomorrow is Monday, Dad," Chen Xuan said evenly. "Second Uncle will walk out the front door with his name completely clean. Nobody in our family kneels to anyone."

For a full breath, nobody moved.

Chen Jianguo searched his son's face for youthful bravado. For years he had seen Chen Xuan as a quiet clerk working a modest desk job in Qingyan. Yet looking into those dark, composed eyes, Chen Jianguo felt the cold knot in his chest loosen all at once.

"Tell me the truth," Chen Jianguo asked barely above a whisper. "Are you only saying this to stop an old man from doing something foolish tomorrow?"

"I never lie to you, Dad," Chen Xuan replied steadily. "Stay home tomorrow, drink your tea, and wait. Nobody is signing away Second Uncle's shop, and nobody is buying cigarettes for Deng Yongchang's people."

Liu Meifang rested her hand over her husband's wrist. "Listen to Chen Xuan," she murmured. "When has he ever spoken like this without being sure?"

Chen Jianguo exhaled a long breath. Picking up his spoon, he saw his hand no longer shook. "All right," he said quietly. "I will stay home tomorrow morning. I will listen to you."

Across the table, Wu Lanying blinked rapidly, pretending the soup steam had drifted into her eyes, and took a slow sip. For the first time since Friday, the broth tasted warm all the way down.

After dinner, Chen Xuan washed the dishes beside Liu Meifang and helped Wu Lanying settle Chen Hao and Chen Yuan into the spare room. By eleven o'clock, the apartment had sunk into exhausted sleep.
