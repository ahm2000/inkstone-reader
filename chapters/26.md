# Chapter 26: Sunday Dinner Without Second Uncle

By six o'clock on Sunday evening, the fluorescent tube over the folding dining table in Building 2 of Fuxing New Village was humming softly, casting a warm yellow light across the narrow living room. Outside the sixth-floor window, a damp autumn dusk had already settled over Yunxi County, blurring the tiled roofs of the neighboring apartment blocks into quiet silhouettes of slate and charcoal.

From the tiny kitchen drifted the smell of ginger, white radish, and slow-simmered pork ribs. Steam fogged the lower glass panes, beading into thin rivulets that slid down toward the wooden sill.

Liu Meifang emerged carrying a wide ceramic pot with a folded towel wrapped around its handles. She set the pot carefully onto the faded cork trivet in the center of the table, then turned back to the sideboard. From the neat stack of everyday dishes, she took one extra white porcelain bowl and a pair of pale bamboo chopsticks.

She paused for half a second, wiped the rim of the bowl with a clean cotton cloth even though it was already spotless, and placed it at the empty corner of the table facing the window.

"Second Brother always likes sitting near the breeze when he drinks hot soup," Liu Meifang said quietly, smoothing the edge of the oilcloth beside the chopsticks. "Even if he is not back yet tonight, his place stays ready."

Beside the table, Wu Lanying's fingers tightened slightly around the back of a wooden stool. Her eyelids were still faintly swollen from two sleepless nights, and the corners of her mouth carried the tight lines of someone who had been holding her breath since Friday afternoon. Yet the moment she felt her two children looking up at her, she softened her face and managed a gentle, steady smile.

"Sit down, Chen Hao, Chen Yuan," Wu Lanying said, nudging her son and daughter toward the bench along the wall. "Eat while the ribs are hot. Your Aunt spent the whole afternoon skimming the broth."

Chen Hao, fourteen and old enough to understand that the sudden silence surrounding his father's hardware shop on Old Street was no ordinary delay, sat down without a word. He kept his shoulders straight, glancing once at Wu Lanying and then across the table at Chen Xuan, as if measuring the calm in his older cousin's face to decide whether he was allowed to stop worrying.

Younger Chen Yuan swung her legs beneath the bench and stared at the empty white porcelain bowl across from her. "Mom, will Dad come home tomorrow afternoon?" she asked in a clear, earnest voice. "The chain on my bicycle still clicks every time I pedal up the slope by the school gate. Dad promised he would oil it and tighten the rear nut on Monday."

Wu Lanying picked the tenderest piece of rib from the serving dish and placed it carefully into Chen Yuan's bowl. Her chopsticks trembled by a hair before she steadied them. "Of course he will," Wu Lanying replied softly. "Your dad never breaks a promise about your bicycle. Eat your vegetables too, or you will not have the strength to ride it."

Chen Xuan sat quietly on the outer side of the table in his plain gray sweater, looking no different from any mild office clerk home for the weekend. He poured half a cup of warm boiled water for Wu Lanying, then placed a generous spoonful of braised greens into Chen Hao's bowl.

"Eat properly, Chen Hao," Chen Xuan said easily. "You are still growing. Leave the worrying to the adults."

Chen Hao nodded once, his tight jaw loosening a fraction as he lifted his chopsticks.

At the head of the folding table, Chen Jianguo had barely touched his rice. His thick, calloused hands rested beside his bowl, his knuckles pale under the yellow light. On Friday night, before Chen Xuan had arrived in Yunxi County, Chen Jianguo had hurried down to Old Street Station only to be brushed off at the front desk by duty officers who refused to let him see his younger brother or even leave a clean jacket. Since then, a cold knot had sat heavy in his chest.

He had obeyed Chen Xuan all Saturday and Sunday, staying away from the police station and Old Street. Yet Sunday night was already here, and Monday morning was only hours away.

Chen Jianguo shifted on his stool, keeping his voice low so it would not frighten Chen Yuan. "Chen Xuan," he said across the rising steam of the soup pot. "Tomorrow is Monday. All the offices open at eight. I have been thinking about it all afternoon."

Liu Meifang paused with her chopsticks in midair, looking uneasily at her husband.

Chen Jianguo swallowed once, his weathered face creased with the anxious humility of a man who had spent fifty years trying to keep his family out of trouble with anyone wearing a uniform. "I know you told me not to go near Old Street Station again," he murmured. "I listened to you all weekend. Still, your Second Uncle has been inside for more than forty-eight hours. Maybe first thing tomorrow morning, I should go down there myself. I can buy two cartons of good cigarettes, wait outside the gate, and beg the station chief or Deng Yongchang's people to give us a way out. Even if they want the lease on the hardware shop, we can sign it over. A shop is just bricks and iron. Your Second Uncle is flesh and blood."

Wu Lanying lowered her gaze to her bowl, blinking hard so no tear would fall in front of her children. In a small county town like Yunxi, ordinary families did not win against Hongrui Real Estate or the men who dined with Deng Yongchang.

The hum of the fluorescent tube seemed to grow louder in the quiet room.

Chen Xuan did not interrupt his father. When Chen Jianguo finished, Chen Xuan simply set his chopsticks down neatly across the rim of his porcelain bowl. He reached across the table, took the wooden-handled ladle from the ceramic pot, and stirred the clear ginger-radish soup once so the warmth rose from the bottom.

He lifted a full ladle of hot broth and two translucent slices of radish, leaned forward, and poured them slowly into Chen Jianguo's soup bowl without spilling a drop.

Only then did Chen Xuan look up, meeting his father's worried eyes with a calm, unshakable certainty heavier than iron.

"Tomorrow is Monday, Dad," Chen Xuan said evenly. "Second Uncle will walk out the front door with his name completely clean. Nobody in our family kneels to anyone."

For a full breath, no one around the table moved.

Chen Jianguo stared at his son. For years he had thought of Chen Xuan as a quiet, dutiful clerk working a modest desk job in Qingyan. Yet looking into Chen Xuan's dark, composed eyes under the kitchen light, Chen Jianguo felt the anxious knot in his chest loosen all at once.

"All right," Chen Jianguo said quietly, picking up his spoon. His hand no longer shook. "I will stay home tomorrow morning. I will listen to you."

Across the table, Wu Lanying blinked rapidly, pretending the soup steam had drifted into her eyes, and took a slow sip. For the first time since Friday, the broth tasted warm all the way down.

After dinner, Chen Xuan washed the dishes beside Liu Meifang, wiped the oilcloth table clean, and helped Wu Lanying settle Chen Hao and Chen Yuan into the inner bedroom. By eleven o'clock, the apartment in Building 2 had sunk into exhausted rest, leaving only the slow tick of the hallway clock and the dark night beyond the glass.
