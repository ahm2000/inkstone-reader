# Chapter 26: The Paper Wall

Just past eight-thirty on Wednesday morning, in the service hall of the Yunxi County Labor Inspection Office, Attorney Bai Qingzhou pulled a paper number from the machine by the door. Eighty-one! The red screen above Window Four said: Now serving 17.

He looked at it for exactly one second, then sat in the blue plastic chair nearest the radiator and opened a thick book. Thirty years ago he had learned that every government hall in the country has the same red numbers and the same blue chairs. Nobody, not even a famous lawyer, gets a better chair!

Around him sat a grandmother with a cloth bag, a delivery rider, and two young men gripping a folded paper that said *unpaid wages*. The Labor Inspection office is the county office that checks how workers are paid and insured, so the hall smelled of tea, cold floor, and worry. Bai turned a page and waited.

At 11:10 the screen chimed eighty-one.

Behind Window Four sat Labor Inspection Officer Gu, a young woman with a tired face, two pens in her hair, and a lanyard that had seen better years. "Which matter?" she asked, without looking up.

Bai laid five papers on the counter, one by one: the entrustment signed by the girl's temporary caretaker, his law license, the law firm's letter, his ID copy, and a one-page written request.

Officer Gu counted them and blinked. "Lawyers usually forget at least two!"

"I forgot two for thirty years," said Bai. "Then I decided to stop."

She laughed in spite of herself. "You're the first lawyer all month who didn't ask me to hurry it along by calling someone's cousin," she added, almost grudgingly impressed, like patience was a trick she hadn't seen performed live before. Then she sighed. "The file room goes to lunch until two. Come back this afternoon. Sorry."

"Of course," said Bai, and he took his book back to the blue chair.

He was back in the blue chair at two sharp, and soon after, stamped pages slid out through the slot. Bai read them in the corridor, standing up, from the first line to the last, and the hall noise faded away.

Tang Dawei, forty-one, laborer. Employer: Tiancheng Demolition Company, owner Shen Jinbao, brother-in-law of Cheng Biao. No labor contract. No social insurance ever paid. Three months of wages never paid. A form labeled "voluntary leave," dated just after a fall from a scaffold at a site in Dongqiao Town: *Tang Dawei leaves his work for family reasons and gives up all claims.*

And one line more, from the Dongqiao clinic's emergency register: he had died of that fall, the company never reported it, and it paid a "silence sum," money to keep a family from complaining, to a middleman, the foreman's cousin, who still held every yuan of it. Getting that money back through a claim could take weeks. Nobody had ever told his daughter.

Bai closed his eyes for one slow breath. He had read thousands of files, and he still needed that breath.

Then he opened them and did the only thing he knew how to do: the lawful thing.

There were three doors, and he knocked on each. First, labor arbitration, where a panel settles pay and injury disputes between workers and bosses, for the unpaid wages. Second, work-injury compensation, the money the law says a company must pay for a worker hurt on the job, even a man it never insured. Third, a report to the County Discipline Commission, the anti-corruption watchdog already working in town, because the owner of Tiancheng was Cheng Biao's brother-in-law and held a thirty-eight-million-yuan Old Street contract.

Three filings, three stamped receipts, less than an hour, and nobody raised a voice.

At Window Four, Officer Gu read the wage complaint and sat up straight. She had a duty now, in writing, with a deadline on it! For once, the paper in her hand wasn't someone else's problem.

"Officer Cai!" she called. "Get the car. We're going to Dongqiao Road!"

By late afternoon, the Tiancheng Demolition yard on Dongqiao Road was mostly dust and old brick, with a metal gate and a white trailer for an office.

Officer Gu and Officer Cai walked in with a clipboard and a plain notice, and for the first time in years, nobody at Tiancheng could say they hadn't been asked! They asked for the scaffold permit. There wasn't one. They asked for the demolition license. It had expired in March. They asked for contracts for the men sitting on bricks in the sun, who looked up with wary eyes. There weren't any. The men were paid in envelopes, and the envelopes had no names on them!

"Stop work and fix it," said Officer Gu, taping the notice to the gate, stamp side out. "Everyone gets paid what's owed. The company will be called in!"

Half an hour later, a white car stopped at the gate, and Shen Jinbao got out.

He was a round man in a clean hard hat that had never touched a brick. He read the notice once, then twice. His lips moved. Then he took out his phone and called his brother-in-law's number.

Cheng Biao had been suspended ten days ago, and he had switched his phone off the same afternoon.

*The number you have dialed is not available.*

Shen Jinbao called again. Then again. For years he had believed his brother-in-law was a wall, and that paperwork was something other people did. Now the wall was gone, and two officers with a clipboard had just walked in through his front gate. He thought of the scaffold, the form, and the middleman, and he understood that all three sat in a county office in numbered folders.

He didn't shout. He sat down on a pile of bricks with the phone in both hands and took off his hard hat.

Three filings, a stop-work notice, an expired license, and a yard full of men who would finally be paid. And nobody had raised a voice!

That evening, back in his rented apartment in Qingyan, Chen Xuan was washing his scratched green thermos when his phone rang.

"Boss," said Bai Qingzhou, in his calm, courtroom voice. "The first answer."

He told it plainly, in order, without softening a word. When he reached the one line, Chen Xuan turned off the tap.

The kitchen was very quiet. Chen Xuan stood there with the wet thermos in one hand. He thought of a girl who folded her blanket into a perfect square. He thought of how she'd said "my dad" and then said it again. For a moment he couldn't have said what he was feeling, only that it was heavy, and that it was about a girl who was only thirteen.

"Mm," he said at last. "Thank you, Attorney Bai. Please keep it lawful. Every step."

"Every step, Boss." Bai paused. "Boss, who will tell the girl?"

"I will."
