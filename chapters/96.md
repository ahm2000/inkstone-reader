# Chapter 96: Lanterns on Old Street

Early winter arrived in Yunxi County with a crisp wind carrying the smell of roasted sweet potatoes down Old Street.

By four in the afternoon, the sky above the gray tile roofs had turned pale copper. Shopkeepers stepped outside with bamboo poles and hung red cloth lanterns beneath their eaves.

When the switches clicked on, a warm amber ribbon stretched from the east stone archway all the way to the old willow beside the river.

Six months ago, half the shutters along this lane had been pulled down before dusk. People had walked with collars turned up, watching for loan sharks and rude men collecting protection money.

Now the flagstones lay flat, and the street smelled of ginger soup, fresh timber, and sesame oil.

At the corner of the lane, the newly painted sign of Mingde Hardware shone under two bright bulbs.

Inside, a gleaming glass counter ran the length of the room. Wrenches, brass hinges, and copper valves sat in neat rows on blue felt trays, polished until they caught the lantern light.

Chen Mingde stood behind the counter in a clean navy jacket, tapping a calculator. Two lively apprentices from the neighboring village hurried between the shelves and the front door, calling out part numbers and laughing whenever they bumped shoulders.

"Watch the glass, you two," Chen Mingde called out with no real heat. "That counter cost more than my first motorbike."

"Yes, Master Chen!" the taller apprentice shouted back, grinning as he balanced a box of steel bolts on his hip. "We will treat it better than our own faces!"

From the courtyard doorway, Wu Lanying appeared carrying an enamel tray with four steaming bowls of red-date tea. Her cheeks were pink from the stove, and the deep worry lines between her brows had softened.

"Stop scolding the boys, Chen Mingde," Wu Lanying said, setting a bowl beside the register. "The new clinic contractor just ordered thirty sets of heavy door locks. Let them drink something hot first."

Chen Mingde picked up his bowl and blew across the red surface. His gaze drifted to the wooden plaque near the register, where a slip of red paper sat tucked safely behind the frame.

"It still feels like a dream," Chen Mingde murmured. "Last winter I was ready to sell the lease. Now we have two apprentices and a waiting list."

Wu Lanying patted his shoulder. "Good fortune comes to people who keep their hearts straight. And our nephew has the kindest luck in the province."

Chen Mingde chuckled. "Do not tell Chen Xuan that. He will just say he met helpful people and talk about pork-rib soup."

* * *

Three doors down, the steady hum of a sewing machine came to a gentle stop.

Yu Changshun leaned back from his workbench and squinted through thick reading glasses. On the table lay a heavy winter coat of deep burgundy wool, lined inside with soft cream fleece.

He snipped the last loose thread near the collar with brass shears and smoothed the lapel with quiet pride.

"Yu Ning," Yu Changshun called toward the inner curtain. "Come try this on before the light fades."

The beaded curtain rattled, and Yu Ning stepped out in a knit sweater, her hair in a neat ponytail. Her face brightened the instant she saw the burgundy wool.

"Grandpa, you finished it already?" Yu Ning hurried over and slid her arms into the sleeves as Yu Changshun held the coat open. "You promised you would not stay up past ten again."

"I slept at half past nine," Yu Changshun said, though the shadows under his eyes betrayed him. He fastened the horn buttons one by one. "Turn around. Let me see the back."

Yu Ning spun slowly in the small shop. The wool flared neatly around her knees, warm and tailored to the millimeter.

"It fits better than anything in Qingyan," Yu Ning said softly, her eyes shining as she pressed her palms into the fleece pockets. "Grandpa, you should have kept the best wool for your own coat."

"An old tailor does not freeze beside a charcoal brazier," Yu Changshun replied, brushing lint from her sleeve. "Next week the county middle school holds its winter assembly. I want my granddaughter walking through that gate warm from chin to ankle."

Outside, lanterns swayed in the breeze, casting red light across bolts of cloth stacked to the ceiling. The bullies who had once threatened to smash his sewing machine were gone, and orders from half the county filled his ledger.

* * *

Out on the flagstones, unhurried patrol boots sounded along the curb.

Director Wei Kun walked Old Street on foot every evening at five. His dark blue uniform coat was buttoned neatly, his cap straight, his hands clasped behind his back.

"Director Wei!" called the old woman roasting chestnuts beside the tea stall, holding out a steaming paper cone. "Take a bag! It is cold tonight!"

Wei Kun raised both hands in a laughing refusal. "Auntie, if I take free chestnuts, the discipline committee will have me sweeping the station courtyard until spring. Give me ten yuan worth, and not one extra."

"You are too stubborn, Director Wei!" She laughed, taking his ten-yuan note and sneaking three extra chestnuts into the cone anyway.

Down the block, Chen Mingde waved from behind his glass counter, and Yu Changshun nodded warmly from his doorstep. Even the children chasing each other beneath the lanterns slowed to shout hello.

When Wei Kun reached the arched doorway of Old Street Station, a thin man in a clean gray jacket stood outside under the lamp.

Wei Kun paused, recognizing the sharp cheekbones and quiet eyes at once.

"Zhong Hai," Wei Kun said calmly. "You are a long way from the hospital."

Zhong Hai straightened and bowed slightly. His hands were clean, and his eyes no longer held the desperate, cornered look of months ago.

"Director Wei," Zhong Hai said steadily. "I did not want to disturb your work. I just came to show you something."

He drew a laminated color photograph from his inner pocket and passed it to Wei Kun with both hands.

Under the doorway lantern, a small boy sat on a sunlit park bench in a bright yellow scarf. His cheeks were round and rosy. Little Zhong Tiantian held a red pinwheel, laughing so wide that his eyes squeezed into happy crescents.

"The doctors in Qingyan finished the second checkup yesterday," Zhong Hai said quietly, his throat bobbing. "His heart beat is strong and even. By spring, Zhong Tiantian can run with the other kids."

Wei Kun stared at the smiling boy for a long moment. "That is the best report I have read all year. How is the repair depot?"

"Good, Director Wei," Zhong Hai smiled genuinely. "Honest wages, regular hours, and I am home by six to cook for my son. I will spend my whole life repaying the mercy I was shown."

Wei Kun returned the photograph and clapped a firm hand on Zhong Hai's shoulder.

"You paid your debt the day you chose to tell the truth and stand straight," Wei Kun said. "Go home to Zhong Tiantian. Tell him Uncle Wei expects him to grow tall enough to beat me at arm wrestling."

Zhong Hai's eyes reddened. He nodded once, tucked the photograph over his heart, and turned down the lantern-lit lane.

Wei Kun stood on the station steps, watching him blend into the warm crowd beneath red lanterns guarding a street that had finally remembered how to smile.
