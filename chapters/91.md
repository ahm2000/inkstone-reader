# Chapter 91: Number One's Dilemma

Morning mist clung to the gray roof tiles of Qingyan's eastern courtyard, where ancient black pines arched over swept granite flagstones.

Beneath the veranda, Number One stood with an unmarked cream envelope resting lightly between his fingers.

Beside the carved stone rail, Number Seven waited in quiet silence, dressed in a plain dark windbreaker.

Number One had spent thirty years handling documents that could topple banks, redirect shipping fleets, or silence provincial factions overnight. Very few things on paper could make his pulse pause.

Yet as he unfolded the single cream sheet and read the four lines of steady dark ink, his breath slowed.

He recognized the penmanship immediately. Every senior strategist who had studied the republic's founding charters knew that exact slant of ink, upright and unyielding even at ninety years of age.

"It came through the sealed courier relay at dawn," Number Seven reported softly. "No digital trace, no escort vehicle, no official stamp. The courier waited outside the outer pine gate with his hat in his hand until I signed for it, then left without a word."

Number One read the four lines a second time.

There was no demand hidden beneath polite phrasing, and no veiled political test. It was simply the handwritten gratitude of a ninety-year-old man who had helped build the country from ruins, asking if he might pour a cup of tea for the man who had quietly saved it.

Yet Number One's brows drew together in genuine conflict.

"You are hesitating," Number Seven observed quietly.

"I am," Number One admitted, folding the cream letter back along its crisp crease. "You know how the Boss lives. He walked away from the highest thrones in the world because he was tired of marble halls, speeches, and people watching every breath he takes."

Number One looked out into the white mist coiling around the pine trunks.

"Last month, when provincial leaders hinted at an honorary economic medal, he told me to lose the invitation in a paper shredder. He dislikes political stages, cameras, and favors owed. He simply wants to finish his delivery sheets at five forty, catch the 6:10 bus, and eat dinner in peace."

"Then shall I draft a courteous refusal?" Number Seven asked calmly. "We can explain that the Boss is occupied with private matters and send a box of spring tea to the Western Hills in return. The elder will not press."

Number One looked down at the unmarked cream envelope in his palm.

He pictured a ninety-year-old man sitting by a window with a wool blanket across his knees, lifting an old fountain pen to write four humble lines by hand.

"No," Number One said slowly. "I have served the Boss long enough to know the difference between an ambitious official and an elder who earned every gray hair on his head. I cannot decide this for him. I have to call."

Number One stepped out onto the stone courtyard, pulled a plain matte-gray phone from his inner pocket, and pressed a single speed-dial key.

* * *

In Lanshui City, the twelfth floor of Hengxin Logistics buzzed with the cheerful clamor of the noon lunch break.

Wang Lei leaned over the partition, waving his chopsticks enthusiastically at Lin Dong.

"I am telling you, Lin Dong, the canteen chef puts extra star anise in the braised pork on Thursdays," Wang Lei insisted. "If we do not go down right now, Pan Xiulan from accounting will scoop out all the lean pieces."

"Then stop talking and walk," Lin Dong laughed, grabbing his meal card. "Chen Xuan, are you coming down with us?"

Chen Xuan stood up from his corner desk by the window, picking up his scratched green metal thermos.

"Go ahead first," Chen Xuan said with a mild, easy smile. "I need to take a quick call. Save me a seat near the fan if there is one."

"Got it, two pork buns and winter melon soup!" Wang Lei called over his shoulder as they hurried toward the elevators.

Chen Xuan walked calmly to the quiet stairwell landing at the end of the corridor, where a half-open window let in the cool river breeze.

Fifty meters below on the street, an ordinary delivery van sat idling softly beside a small magazine kiosk. No passerby glanced twice at the dark tinted glass, nor noticed the silent, featureless presence inside watching every rooftop and passing car within a fifty-meter ring.

Chen Xuan stepped into the sunlight by the stairwell railing and answered his vibrating phone.

"Number One," Chen Xuan said mildly, unscrewing the lid of his thermos to let the steam rise. "Did something happen with the southern ports?"

"No, Boss," Number One replied, his voice carrying an unusual note of careful deference. "The southern ports are stable. I am calling about a personal letter that arrived in Qingyan this morning."

"A letter?" Chen Xuan asked, watching a gray sparrow hop along the concrete sill.

"An unmarked cream envelope from the Western Hills," Number One said clearly. "Written by hand by the ninety-year-old founding Supreme Elder. There is no official seal and no political agenda. He learned what happened with Aegis Meridian and the four mercenaries in Lanshui City. He simply asked if you might spare half an hour for one quiet cup of tea at a lakeside pavilion, with no ceremony and no escort."

Number One paused for a heartbeat, then added honestly, "I know you dislike these gatherings, Boss. Say one word and I will send a respectful decline immediately."

On the stairwell landing, the breeze stirred the cuff of Chen Xuan's plain white shirt.

Chen Xuan did not answer right away. He looked out across the rooftops of Lanshui City, at the laundry lines fluttering on apartment balconies, the riverfront cranes, and the ordinary cars moving peacefully along the avenues below.

Three full seconds passed in gentle silence.

"He is ninety years old," Chen Xuan said softly, his tone mild and completely unhurried. "You do not make an elder wait."

On the other end of the line in Qingyan, Number One felt a quiet warmth settle in his chest.

"Saturday morning," Chen Xuan continued, taking a small sip of warm water from his green thermos. "Somewhere quiet near the water, not too far for an old man to travel. Tell them no motorcade, no police tape, and no banners."

"Understood, Boss," Number One said promptly. "I will have Number Seven arrange the old wooden pavilion on the eastern shore of Lanshui Lake. It is twenty minutes outside the city and completely secluded."

"Good," Chen Xuan said. Then he added with quiet practicality, "Make sure the wooden bench inside has a thick cushion for his knees, and tell them not to bring any rare auction tea. Plain green leaves from this spring are fine. Old people sleep better when the tea is light."

"I will see to every detail myself, Boss," Number One replied with quiet respect.

"Alright. I have to go before the canteen runs out of steamed buns."

The line clicked off softly.

Chen Xuan slipped the phone into his pocket, capped his scratched green thermos, and strolled calmly down the stairs toward the smell of braised pork and winter melon soup.

Hundreds of miles away beneath the misted pines of Qingyan, Number One lowered his phone slowly and turned to Number Seven.

"Clear Saturday morning," Number One said, a faint smile softening his stern face. "And find the softest cotton cushion in Lanshui City."
