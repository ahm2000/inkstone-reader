# Chapter 20: The Honest Line

Yunxi County Discipline Commission, third floor, Room 2. Monday, 2:00 p.m.

Wei Kun had buttoned his uniform wrong, and he didn't notice until he sat down.

The room was small and white, with a table, three chairs, and one window that looked at a wall. Across from him sat the provincial team's youngest investigator, Ms. Tong, about twenty-six, with a neat ponytail, a recorder, and a notebook. Beside her sat an older man who wrote everything down and never said a word.

Wei Kun was sure he was about to be punished. Here was why.

On paper he was only a deputy principal staff member, a title with a salary and no power, and the easiest name to cross out.

And something had gone very wrong. Chen Mingde had spent forty hours in his station's cell, and the detention paper for those hours hadn't even been written until hour eighteen. Wei Kun had been the duty officer on the sheet. Forty and eighteen. Those two numbers had drummed in his head for three days!

Would they take away his badge? Move him to a records room? Send him home with a note in his file that would follow him for twenty years?

At home, his wife was seven months pregnant. Their apartment loan took a big bite out of his pay every month, and the baby's crib was still in its box in the hallway.

In the corridor outside, he'd overheard two colleagues already dividing up his old shifts between them, not unkindly, just practically, the way people make room for an empty chair before it's even empty.

"Officer Wei," Ms. Tong said, and she slid a paper cup of water across the table. "Please start with Friday. Tell us what you saw, and what you did. Don't leave anything out."

The cup was warm. Wei Kun almost laughed. He had expected a harsh lamp and a loud voice, and they'd given him warm water!

So he started. And once he started, it all came out.

He told them about the two o'clock arrest, and the clinic note that was stamped before the arrest. He told them about the weeks of "talks" with shop owners, and how he'd typed the forms for every one of them. "I didn't write a false time before this," he said, staring at his hands. "But I typed what I was handed. That is my fault too."

Ms. Tong didn't say a word, and only nodded and wrote.

"Saturday morning, 7:40," Ms. Tong said gently. "Who told you to write Friday, 2:30 p.m., on the detention paper?"

"Director Cheng," Wei Kun said. "Cheng Biao. He stood in the duty room and said it out loud. 'Write Friday, 2:30, right after the arrest.'"

"And what did you write?"

"The true time. Saturday, 7:50 a.m." He swallowed. "I'd like to say I was brave. I wasn't. My hand shook so hard that the pen made a mark on the desk. I think I was just too scared to write a lie in my own handwriting."

For a long moment, nobody spoke.

Then Wei Kun reached into his jacket and took out a small black notebook, its corners worn soft. "In the second week, I started writing down what Director Cheng said out loud, with the date. I told myself it was so I wouldn't forget. But it was really in case. Seven pages. I don't know if it helps."

Ms. Tong looked at the older man, and the older man, for the first time, put down his pen!

"May we keep it?" she asked. "We'll give you a receipt."

"Yes. Please."

Wei Kun left the room at five. In the hallway, he realized something that made his ears go hot!

He had told them every ugly thing he'd done. He had not told them about the hot water.

Friday night, he'd carried a paper cup of hot water to Chen Mingde through the window, because the old man's hands were shaking with cold. Nobody had asked about it. Nobody knew. Should he go back and tell them?

No. Wei Kun shook his head. That was a small decent thing any officer should do, and a man who comes to be judged shouldn't use a good deed to bargain for a lighter sentence.

Yunxi County Discipline Commission, third floor, Room 2. Tuesday, 4:30 p.m.

By noon on Tuesday, the whole building had gone strangely quiet.

Word had run down the stairs: the Executive Deputy Magistrate had been taken from his office by Discipline Commission officers. Wei Kun had never once spoken to Lu Wenzhe, yet the news made his knees weak. It meant the team wasn't playing around!

Now he sat in the same chair, with the same warm cup of water, and he waited for the sentence.

"Officer Wei," Ms. Tong said, and she opened a folder. "The team has reviewed your statement and your notebook. We have no decision about you today."

He stared at her. "No decision?!"

"You are not being praised, and you are not being punished. Please remain available. Don't leave Yunxi, and answer your phone. A copy of your notebook stays in our file."

"That's all?"

"That's all, for now." She almost smiled. "Eat something, Officer Wei. You look like you haven't."

Wei Kun opened his mouth to answer and found he couldn't remember his last meal. He nodded, stood up too fast, and bowed to her twice by mistake.

Yunxi County Discipline Commission gate. Tuesday, 6:10 p.m.

He walked out with his legs feeling very strange. Not punished. Not praised. Only *wait*!

Was that good news or bad news? He couldn't tell!

And then he stopped on the front steps.

At the gate, under the yellow streetlamp, stood his wife. She had one hand on her belly and the other around a battered steel thermos, and she had taken two buses to be there.

"Why are you standing?! You should be sitting!" Wei Kun hurried down the steps. "It's cold, and the bus is far, and the doctor said..."

"I wanted to," she said, and held the thermos out to him. "Ginger tea. Drink it before it's cold."

He took the thermos. The warmth went straight through his hands and into his chest. He hadn't felt warm for three days!

"Was it a good day?" she asked, the way she asked every single night.

For two nights, he hadn't been able to answer. Tonight he opened his mouth, and the truth came out slowly, one piece at a time. "They asked me everything. I told them everything. They didn't say well done, and they didn't say shame on you. They said wait."

"That sounds fair to me!"

Wei Kun looked at her face, tired and round and patient under the lamp. He thought about the black notebook, and the honest line, and the pen that shook.

"I think it was a good day," he said. "Yes. I think it was."

She took his arm. The team might call again at any hour, and the baby was two months away. Yet for the first time in three days, Wei Kun walked home without being afraid of the phone in his pocket.
