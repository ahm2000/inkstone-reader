# Chapter 20: The Honest Line

In a quiet third-floor interview room at the Yunxi County Discipline Inspection Commission, Wei Kun had buttoned his uniform wrong, and he did not notice until he sat down.

The room was small and white, with a table, three chairs and one window that looked out at a wall.

Across from him sat the provincial team's youngest investigator, Ms. Tong, about twenty-six, with a neat ponytail, a recorder and a notebook.

Beside her sat an older man who wrote everything down and never said a word.

Wei Kun was sure he was about to be punished, and the reason was simple!

On paper, Wei Kun held the rank of deputy principal staff member, which gave him a salary and no power, so his was the easiest name in the station to cross out.

Worse, something had gone badly wrong!

Chen Mingde had spent forty hours in his station's cell, and the detention paper for those hours had not even been written until hour eighteen.

Wei Kun was the duty officer on that sheet.

Forty and eighteen, those two numbers had drummed in his head for three days!

Would they take his badge? Would they move him to a records room? Would they send him home with a note in his file that followed him for twenty years?

At home, his wife was seven months pregnant, the apartment loan took a big bite out of his pay every month, and the baby's crib was still in its box in the hallway!

In the corridor outside, he had already heard two colleagues dividing up his shifts, not unkindly, just practically, because they all expected him to be gone.

"I'll take his Tuesdays," one of them said.

"Then I get the weekends!" said the other. "Keep your voice down, he is right in there!"

"Name and post?" Ms. Tong asked.

"Wei Kun, deputy principal staff member."

"Years of service?"

"Twelve."

"Officer Wei," she said, and she slid a paper cup of water across the table. "Please start with Friday. Tell us what you saw and what you did, and leave nothing out."

The cup was warm.

Wei Kun almost laughed, because he had expected a harsh lamp and a loud voice, and they had given him warm water!

He started, and once he started, it all came out.

He told them about the two o'clock arrest, and the clinic note that was stamped before the arrest had even happened.

He told them about the weeks of "talks" with the shop owners, and how he had typed the forms for every one of them.

"I did not write a false time before this," he said, staring at his hands. "But I typed what I was handed, and that is my fault too."

Ms. Tong said nothing and only nodded and wrote.

"Saturday morning, seven-fifty," she said gently. "Who told you to write Friday, two-thirty in the afternoon, on the detention paper?"

"Director Cheng," Wei Kun said. "Cheng Biao. He stood in the duty room and said it out loud: 'Write Friday, two-thirty, right after the arrest.'"

"And what did you write?"

"The true time, Saturday, seven-fifty in the morning." He swallowed. "I would like to say I was brave, but I wasn't. My hand shook so hard that the pen left a mark on the desk, and I think I was only too scared to write a lie in my own handwriting."

Silence.

To put it simply, that was the whole matter: one false line would have made forty hours look like eighteen, and one honest line had spoiled it.

Then Wei Kun reached into his jacket and took out a small black notebook with its corners worn soft.

"In the second week, I began writing down what Director Cheng said out loud, with the date," he said. "I told myself it was so I would not forget, but it was really in case. Seven pages. I don't know if it helps."

"Seven pages," Ms. Tong said, and she looked at the older man.

For the first time, the older man put down his pen!

"May we keep it?" she asked. "We will give you a receipt."

"Yes. Please."

Wei Kun left the room at five o'clock, and in the hallway he realized something that made his ears go hot.

He had told them every ugly thing he had done, and he had not told them about the hot water!

On Friday night, he had carried a paper cup of hot water to Chen Mingde through the window, because the old man's hands were shaking with cold, and nobody had asked about it.

Should he go back and tell them?

No, Wei Kun decided, shaking his head, because that was a small decent thing any officer should do, and a man who comes to be judged should not use a good deed to bargain for a lighter sentence.

---

By the next afternoon, the whole building had gone strangely quiet.

Word had run down the stairs, and people said it in whispers.

"They took the Executive Deputy Magistrate out of his own office!"

"Discipline Commission officers, in daylight!"

Wei Kun had never spoken to Lu Wenzhe even once, yet the news made his knees weak, because it meant the team was not playing around!

Now he sat in the same chair with the same warm cup of water, and he waited for the sentence.

"Officer Wei," Ms. Tong said, and she opened a folder. "The team has reviewed your statement and your notebook, and we have no decision about you today."

"No decision?!"

"You are not being praised, and you are not being punished. Please remain available, do not leave Yunxi, and answer your phone. A copy of your notebook stays in our file."

"That's all?"

"That's all, for now." She almost smiled. "Eat something, Officer Wei. You look as if you haven't."

Wei Kun opened his mouth to answer and found he could not remember his last meal, so he nodded, stood up too fast and bowed to her twice by mistake.

---

Outside the Commission gate, Wei Kun walked out on legs that felt very strange.

Not punished, not praised, only this.

Wait.

Was that good news or bad news? He could not tell, and then he stopped on the front steps.

At the gate, under the yellow streetlamp, stood his wife, with one hand on her belly and the other around a battered steel thermos.

She had taken two buses to be there.

"Why are you standing? You should be sitting," Wei Kun said, hurrying down the steps. "It's cold, and the bus is far, and the doctor said..."

"I wanted to," she said, and she held the thermos out to him. "Ginger tea. Drink it before it is cold."

He took the thermos, and the warmth went straight through his hands and into his chest.

He had not felt warm for three days.

"Was it a good day?" she asked, the way she asked every single night.

For two nights he had not been able to answer, but tonight he opened his mouth, and the truth came out slowly, one piece at a time.

"They asked me everything, and I told them everything. They did not say well done, and they did not say shame on you. They said wait."

"That sounds fair to me," she said.

Wei Kun looked at her face, tired and round and patient under the lamp, and he thought about the black notebook, and the honest line, and the pen that shook.

"I think it was a good day," he said. "Yes. I think it was."

She took his arm.

The team might call again at any hour, and the baby was two months away, yet for the first time in three days, Wei Kun walked home without being afraid of the phone in his pocket.
