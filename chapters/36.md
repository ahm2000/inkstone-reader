# Chapter 36: Employee of the Quarter

On Friday afternoon in the Operations floor break room, the ballot box was a shoebox, wrapped in red paper by someone from HR, with a slot cut in the lid, and it sat on top of the filing cabinet like a very small, very serious mailbox.

Lu Qinghe pushed her cleaning cart up beside it and began to wipe the side of the cabinet, very slowly.

She was Detail Number Fourteen, with twenty-two years of service, and she had never been so nervous in her life. The reason was simple: the danger this time was a shoebox, and her Boss's name was about to be written on slips of paper and dropped inside it!

At breakfast, Chief An's voice had come through her earpiece, calm and exact.

"Number Fourteen, this is the stay-ordinary order: not one slip is touched, added, removed, or nudged, because if the Boss wins he wins and if he loses he loses, and your only job is that the box is never left alone."

"Understood, Chief."

To put it simply, the stay-ordinary order meant that the Detail never change a result, not even to help the Boss.

"And if someone stuffs it against him, you tell me who, because we do not stuff it back," Chief An added.

Stopping a cheat was easy, because you simply stopped him, but letting a fair vote happen with her hands in her pockets was the hardest order she had ever been given. If the Boss lost, could she keep her face still?

A little later, a manager from Sales wandered in for coffee, and Lu Qinghe stepped in front of the shoebox with her mop.

"Wet floor," she said, and although the floor was perfectly dry, the manager left anyway.

---

Out in the open office, Sister Pan clapped her hands.

"Silence, everybody!" Sister Pan had dragged a chair to the whiteboard and drawn two columns, and the shoebox now sat on her desk with Lu Qinghe's cart parked one step behind it. "Thirty-four slips, thirty-four colleagues. I read, you watch, nobody cries. Lunch bets close at the last slip!"

Chen Xuan sat at his desk with his thermos, thinking about the six-ten bus, and he had not looked up once.

"Put your money where your mouth is, Pengfei!" someone called from the back, and nobody owned up to it afterward.

The room laughed the particular laugh of people who are taking sides without admitting it.

"Zhang, Zhang, Chen, Zhang," Sister Pan read out, making her marks with a red marker, "Zhang, Chen, Zhang, Zhang."

By the ninth slip, Zhang Pengfei was clearly ahead, and he sat up very straight and tried hard to look as if he did not care.

Would the quiet one lose to the loud one, the way it had always gone on this floor?

Then the tide turned.

"Chen, Chen, Chen, Zhang, Chen," Sister Pan read, and her marker squeaked faster. "Chen, Chen!"

At fifteen slips, then twenty, then twenty-eight, the whole floor stopped breathing, and Hu Dewei stopped tugging his tie, which was a historic event in itself.

"Thirty-three slips, sixteen for Zhang, sixteen for Chen, and one for Lin Dong, who voted for himself and has told me so three times," said Sister Pan, and her voice cracked.

"It was a matter of principle!" said Lin Dong.

"Last slip," she said, and she unfolded it and read it slowly.

"Chen Xuan."

The room exploded!

"Seventeen to sixteen!"

"One vote, just one!"

"Sister Pan, count again!"

"I counted twice, I am not a child!" Sister Pan grabbed the pile. "Wait. Some of these have writing on them. Listen."

She held up a slip and read it in a voice that had suddenly gone gentle. "*For the ledger errors he fixed in March and never told anybody.*" She held up another. "*For the customs codes. Eleven times this year. I only found out from the edit history.*" And a third. "*For the fourteen thousand yuan, and for the yellow notes.*"

"Who wrote that last one?" a clerk asked softly.

"Nobody signs a thank-you," said Sister Pan. "Everyone knows the yellow notes, the small notes he left on a desk beside every line he had fixed."

"He fixed my sheets too, and I never knew who it was," the girl from accounting said first, softly.

"Mine," said a clerk by the window.

"Mine, last winter," said another.

One by one, the voices came from every corner of the floor.

Chen Xuan sat very still with his hand on his thermos. He had never counted how many he had fixed, and he had only wanted to go home on time, and a wrong number in someone else's sheet always came back to him later as extra work.

"Hmm?" he said at last. "They were small mistakes, typos mostly."

"Typos?" Sister Pan cried. "One of them would have cost somebody a month's bonus!"

Nobody had known, and nobody had ever said thank you, because nobody had known whom to thank. In one afternoon, thirty-four slips had said it for them.

At the back of the room, Lu Qinghe stood beside the filing cabinet with her gray cloth and did not look at anyone. Her eyes were suddenly very bright, and she wiped the clean cabinet for a long time.

Seventeen, sixteen, and one, said the whiteboard, and that evening Sister Pan paid out the lunch bets from memory, because in all the years of her notebook, it was the first time a bet nobody expected had won.

Then there was Zhang Pengfei.

He stood up slowly, and every head turned, and everybody expected a slammed chair, a cold remark, or a pale face walking out toward the stairs. Was he going to make a scene?

Instead, Zhang Pengfei crossed the floor to Chen Xuan's desk and held out his hand.

"Congratulations," he said, a little hoarsely, "and next quarter I want that vote back."

"Oh, thanks," said Chen Xuan, shaking it, "and that's fair."

"I looked at the edit history on my own shared folder," Zhang Pengfei said, so quietly that only Chen Xuan could hear. "You fixed it in two minutes, the day I broke it, and you never said a word. I am not stupid, Supervisor Chen. I am only second."

Chen Xuan did not answer, and he only nodded, as if a small, friendly thing had been handed back to him.

What nobody knew was that on his own slip, in a neat hand, he had written Zhang Pengfei's name. Zhang worked the latest hours on the floor, and nobody ever thanked him either. It was a secret ballot, after all.

"Attention, everyone!" Hu Dewei hurried forward with a printed notice, tugging at his tie again. "The prize, from General Manager Sun, is lunch with our visitor from Haiyue Hospitality, on Monday at noon!"

"Lunch?" Chen Xuan asked, "and is it a free one?"

"Free, and a very good one!"

"Mm," said Chen Xuan, picking up his thermos, "then I will be there."

The clock said four fifty-five exactly. He tucked his canvas bag under his arm and walked to the door with the whole floor watching his back, as it had on that first Friday and on every Friday since.

Outside, the elevator was already waiting with its doors open.

"Monday at noon!" Hu Dewei called after him, and his flowerpot mug shook so hard that the tea made little rings. Then, much more quietly, to the closing doors: "You will remember, won't you, Supervisor Chen? Monday at noon?"
