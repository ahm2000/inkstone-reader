# Chapter 36: Employee of the Quarter

Qingyan City, Hengxin Logistics, Operations floor break room. Friday, 3:00 p.m.

The ballot box was a shoebox. Someone from HR had wrapped it in red paper, cut a slot in the lid, and set it on top of the filing cabinet, where it looked like a very small, very serious mailbox.

Lu Qinghe pushed her cleaning cart up beside it and began, very slowly, to wipe the side of the cabinet.

She was Detail Number Fourteen, with twenty-two years of service, and she had never been this nervous in her life. Why? Because this time the danger was a shoebox, and her Boss's name was going to be written on slips of paper and dropped inside it!

At breakfast, Chief An's voice had come through her earpiece, calm and exact. "Number Fourteen, this is the stay-ordinary order. Not one slip is touched, added, removed, or nudged. If the Boss wins, he wins. If he loses, he loses. Your only job is that the box is never left alone."

"Understood, Chief." The stay-ordinary order meant the Detail never change a result, not even to help the Boss.

"And if someone stuffs it against him, you tell me who. We do not stuff it back."

Stopping a cheat was easy. You simply stopped him. Letting a fair vote happen, with her hands in her pockets, was the hardest order she had ever received!

At 3:40, a manager from Sales wandered into the break room for coffee. Lu Qinghe stepped in front of the shoebox with her mop and said, "Wet floor."

The floor was perfectly dry. The manager left anyway.

Same floor, the open office. Friday, 4:20 p.m.

"Silence, everybody!" Sister Pan had dragged a chair to the whiteboard and drawn two columns. "Thirty-four slips, thirty-four colleagues. I read, you watch, nobody cries. Lunch bets close at the last slip!"

Chen Xuan sat at his desk with his thermos. He was thinking about the 6:10 bus and had not looked up once.

"Zhang. Zhang. Chen. Zhang." Sister Pan made marks with a red marker. "Zhang. Chen. Zhang. Zhang."

By the ninth slip Zhang Pengfei led six to three, and he sat up straight, trying very hard to look as if he didn't care.

Then the tide turned.

"Chen. Chen. Chen. Zhang. Chen." Sister Pan's marker squeaked faster. "Chen! Chen!"

Fifteen slips. Twenty. Twenty-eight. The whole floor had stopped breathing, and Hu Dewei had stopped tugging his tie, which in itself was a historic event.

"Thirty-three slips," said Sister Pan, her voice cracking. "Sixteen for Zhang. Sixteen for Chen. One for Lin Dong, who voted for himself and has already told me so three times."

"It was a matter of principle!" said Lin Dong.

"Last slip!"

She unfolded it and read it slowly.

"Chen Xuan."

The room exploded!

"Seventeen to sixteen!"

"One vote! One!"

"Sister Pan, check again!"

"I checked twice! I'm not a child!" Sister Pan grabbed the pile. "Wait. Some of these have writing on them. Listen!"

She held up a slip and read aloud, her voice suddenly gentler. "*For the ledger errors he fixed in March and never told anybody.*" She held up another. "*For the customs codes. Eleven times this year. I only found out from the edit history.*" And a third: "*For the fourteen thousand yuan, and for the yellow notes.*"

Nobody joked this time. Everyone knew the yellow notes: the small notes he left on desks beside the lines he had fixed.

The girl from accounting spoke first, softly. "He fixed my sheets too. I never knew who it was."

"Mine," said a clerk by the window.

"Mine, last winter," said another.

One by one, the voices came from every corner of the floor. Chen Xuan sat very still with his hand on his thermos. He had never counted how many he had fixed. He had only wanted to go home on time, and a wrong number in someone else's sheet always came back to him later as extra work.

"Hmm?" he said finally. "They were small mistakes. Typos mostly."

"Typos!" Sister Pan cried. "One of them would have cost somebody a month's bonus!"

Nobody had known. Nobody had ever said thank you, because nobody had known whom to thank. In one afternoon, thirty-four slips had said it for them.

At the back of the room, Lu Qinghe stood by the filing cabinet with her gray cloth, and she did not look at anyone. Her eyes were suddenly very bright. She wiped the already clean cabinet for a long time.

Seventeen, sixteen, and one, said the whiteboard. Sister Pan paid out eleven lunches from memory that evening, because for the first time since she started her notebook, the bet that Chen Xuan would not place had lost.

Then there was Zhang Pengfei.

He stood up slowly. Every head turned. Everybody expected a slammed chair, a cold remark, or a pale face walking out toward the stairs.

Instead, Zhang Pengfei walked across the floor to Chen Xuan's desk and held out his hand.

"Congratulations," he said. His voice was a little hoarse. "One vote. Next quarter I want that vote back."

"Oh. Thanks." Chen Xuan shook it. "That's fair."

"I looked at the edit history on my own shared folder," Zhang Pengfei said, so quietly that only Chen Xuan could hear. "You fixed it in two minutes, the day I broke it. You never said a word. I'm not stupid, Supervisor Chen. I'm only second."

Chen Xuan did not answer. He only nodded, as if a small, friendly thing had been returned to him.

He had not mentioned that on his own slip, in a neat hand, he had written Zhang Pengfei's name, because Zhang worked the latest on the floor and nobody ever thanked him either. It was a secret ballot, after all.

"Attention, everyone!" Hu Dewei hurried forward with a printed notice, tugging at his tie again. "The prize, from General Manager Sun, is lunch with our visitor from Haiyue Hospitality! Monday at noon!"

"Lunch?" Chen Xuan asked. "A free one?"

"Free! A very good one!"

"Mm," said Chen Xuan, picking up his thermos. "Then I'll be there."

The clock said 4:55 exactly. He tucked his canvas bag under his arm and walked toward the door with the whole floor watching his back, as it had on that first Friday and on every Friday since.

Outside, the elevator was already waiting with its doors open.

"Monday at noon!" Hu Dewei called after him, and his flowerpot mug trembled so hard that the tea made little rings.
