# Chapter 19: The River Barge at Pier Four

By nine o'clock on Saturday night, thick mist hung low over the Yunxi River, hiding the far bank. Three hundred meters east of the shut-down cement plant, Pier Four jutted out into the water behind a padlocked gate marked closed and unsafe.

Half a kilometer upriver, inside the top floor of an empty grain tower, two still figures in dark rain coats watched the dock through black night scopes. Number 9 turned the focus ring on the long-range scope while Number 19 worked a tough laptop under a dark screen cover, watching river traffic records and freight lists.

"Movement under the south floodgate," Number 9 said quietly, his voice barely carrying three feet. "Lights turned down to dim red. Ship tracking signal is switched off. It is a forty-eight-meter flat-bottom steel cargo barge."

Number 19 linked the scope view to the laptop screen. "No boat is listed to dock within four kilometers of Yunxi County tonight. Wait. Checking the Lanshui river patrol log. A sand-digging permit was stamped at six o'clock for an unnamed work barge hauling river gravel downriver to the Lanshui bridge site."

"Listen to the engine," Number 9 murmured, shifting a small listening mic slightly. "Underwater muffler pipe. No normal gravel crew pays to quiet a diesel engine below the waterline."

"And no normal gravel crew loads up at a closed dock in the dark," Number 19 muttered, zooming in on the screen. "How many men on deck?"

"Four men in dark raincoats," Number 9 answered, watching them untie thick dock ropes without a single shouted word or deck flashlight. "Clean, trained movements. Not local boat hands."

Through the mist, the heavy black hull glided out from beneath Pier Four's concrete overhang where the old floodgate met the river. Number 9 followed the boat as it eased into the main river lane, zooming in tightly on the white water-depth numbers painted near the front.

Number 9's hand froze on the focus ring. "That is not river sand."

"Check how deep it sits," Number 19 said right away, fingers ready over the keys.

"Only fifty-five centimeters of hull is left above the water," Number 9 read off the distance meter. "The cargo hold is only two-thirds full, piled with wet gray gravel. Yet the boat is carrying almost nine hundred tons. Wet river gravel at that size cannot weigh more than four hundred and twenty tons."

Number 19 sat up straight. "Double the normal weight. Turning on the heat camera now."

A narrow lens beside Number 9's scope swept across the open cargo hold. While river gravel cooled down evenly in the evening fog, heavy metal boxes held indoor warmth much longer. Across the laptop screen, the heat picture popped into sharp color.

Beneath a thin, raked layer of damp gravel barely forty centimeters deep sat forty-two square boxes arranged in six neat rows. Their edges were razor-straight, their insides evenly packed, and every box showed the heavy shape of thick steel lined with lead.

"Check the box size," Number 9 ordered evenly.

Number 19 ran the exact size against customs and factory shipping lists in White Guard's regional files. Four seconds later, the screen flashed bright red.

Number 19's voice dropped into cold focus. "The box size matches to the millimeter. Standard heavy-duty transport boxes, Type Seven-B. Used for high-grade rare-earth metals and precision military metal parts."

Number 9 kept the scope locked on the back of the barge as it moved toward the east bend. "Yunxi County has no rare-earth plant and no military factory. Where did those boxes come from?"

"Pulling highway weight-station records and toll camera photos from the last three days," Number 19 replied, typing fast. "Here. Three covered heavy trucks carrying provincial farm permits left the mountain highway at Yunxi West between midnight and three o'clock Friday morning. Recorded weight at the mountain station: thirty-eight tons each. By dawn, when they drove north, they were empty."

"Trace the license plates back to their start," Number 9 ordered. "Skip the county files."

Number 19 linked the plate records three hundred kilometers inland across provincial truck logs and train yard shipping lists that no county official could touch. One line locked into place. Number 19 stopped typing and stared at the screen for two full seconds.

"Speak," Number 9 said quietly.

"Source confirmed," Number 19 reported, every bit of calm replaced by sharp tension. "The load came from a side depot tied to the Seventh Inland Military-Industrial Materials Plant. Official records list two batches of refined rare-earth metals and forty-eight precision military metal casings as low-grade factory scrap meant for local recycling."

For a long moment, neither man spoke.

"Military-grade rare-earth metals and precision metal casings," Number 9 repeated softly. "Written off as scrap inland, trucked into a small county under farm permits, hidden inside an old 1970s air-raid tunnel under Old Street, and floated out of a closed dock under forty centimeters of river gravel."

"It gets worse," Number 19 said, tapping the right screen where port shipping lists were popping up. "I matched this barge's engine sound against river-lock recordings. This exact boat has made eleven nighttime runs down the Yunxi River in fourteen months. Every single time, it unloads at an unlisted dock outside Lanshui into locked cold seafood containers headed for Pier Twelve at Qingyan Port."

Number 9's eyes narrowed behind the scope. "Pier Twelve is a sealed international shipping dock. Once a container clears that gate with a pre-approved customs stamp, it goes straight onto an overseas cargo ship with zero physical checks."

"Which means," Number 19 finished evenly, "whoever backs Hongrui Real Estate is not building riverside apartments. They are building a permanent underground storage room to smuggle military materials overseas."

Down on the river, the barge turned the wooded bend and slipped out of sight. Number 9 checked that the silent sound tag had passed tracking on to the downriver watch outside Lanshui.

Number 19 held a gloved hand over the radio. "Do we stop them before the Lanshui lock? Four men on an unarmed barge. Our arrest team could board them in twenty minutes."

"No," Number 9 answered without a single second of doubt. "Grabbing four boatmen on a foggy river only warns Qingyan Port and gives whoever runs Pier Twelve time to burn the shipping lists and cut the inland trail. Watch and gather proof until Number 1 gives the word."

"Understood," Number 19 said, already packing the heat photos, weight math, highway logs, and Qingyan Port papers into a locked file stamped with a top-level red alert. "File sent to the Lanshui safehouse. Number 1 will have it in three seconds."

"Stay on the river watch until the next shift checks in," Number 9 said, his eyes returning to the dark water below. "Tonight just stopped being a county case."

Far across the misty county, in the warm light of a sixth-floor kitchen at Fuxing New Village, Chen Xuan was still washing dinner bowls beside his mother. He had no idea that the quiet stone he dropped to clear his uncle's name had cracked open a fault line reaching all the way to the sea.
