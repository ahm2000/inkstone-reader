# Chapter 14: The Empty Chair

By three in the morning, the last customer had gone from Gong's Noodles.

Number Twenty-three stood beside the window table and looked down at the fourth chair. A fresh bowl of noodles had been set there at midnight, and it had long since gone cold.

He cleared it away, washed the bowl, and set a clean one in its place.

"Same tomorrow?" Number Sixty-eight asked from the doorway.

"The same."

"Three years, and he has never once sat in that chair. He doesn't even know this shop is ours."

"He will." Number Twenty-three lined up the chopsticks until they lay perfectly parallel. "An empty chair is a promise. When the Boss finally walks in and sits down, I want the bowl to be hot."

Number Sixty-eight smiled.

On the wall behind the counter, a small monitor displayed a single green dot circled by a ring, marking the Boss's position. It was not at Fuxing New Village.

It was on the corner of Old Street, right beside a crooked shop shutter.

"He's awake?" Number Sixty-eight whispered.

Number Fifty-six slipped in through the back door with his helmet tucked under his arm, fresh off watching the lane.

"He brought a screwdriver. Couldn't sleep, I guess. He's tightening the shutter back onto its bolt and humming to himself. Yao Xiaolong was sitting in the parked van looking jumpy as a cat ever since Yan Tiehu disappeared. He shouted out the window, 'That wall is gravel on Monday, clerk. Save your screws.' The Boss didn't even blink. He just said, 'Evening. It hangs better straight.' Then he went right back to lining up the lock."

"Does he look angry?"

"He looks like a man who wants his uncle's shutter straight before he goes back to bed."

They watched the green dot in silence. Outside the ten-kilometer line, every regular Black Pine Guard post stood dismissed and motionless under Zero Guard watch, while inside the unseen dome the Zero Guard held the night in absolute stillness. Somewhere across the county, Yan Tiehu was still chained in a pitch-black cellar, and Dong Haisheng was still staring at a dead phone in a cold garage.

Beneath the green dot on the screen, a single line of text glowed.

*8:30 a.m.: goes to Old Street Station.*

Number Twenty-three shook his head quietly. "A bench. I wonder what that station will do once it finds out who is sitting on it."

* * *

Near midnight, in the duty room of Old Street Police Station, duty officer Wei Kun's hands were still warm.

He sat at the desk and stared at them as if they belonged to somebody else.

An old man in a holding cell had taken a paper cup through the bars with both hands and said, "Thank you, young man."

Nobody in this building had thanked Wei Kun for anything in three years.

So why did he feel like a thief?

"Officer Wei!"

Lei Bo leaned through the doorway, cracking sunflower seeds between his teeth and grinning. "Caught you on the hallway monitor. Bringing room service to the guest, eh?"

"He was cold." Wei Kun kept his voice low. "It was just a cup of water."

"Sure, sure." Lei Bo shut the door and perched on the edge of the desk. "Only Director Cheng said no comforts for the Old Street holdouts. Cold nights make for quick signatures. You know the drill."

"I know."

"I'd hate for him to hear about it." Lei Bo rubbed his thumb across his fingertips. "Silence is cheap, Wei Kun. A carton of good cigarettes, eight hundred yuan, and my memory goes foggy. Or it stays sharp, and I mention it to Director Cheng before breakfast. Your wife is seven months along, isn't she? One salary, a mortgage, a baby on the way. A bad review costs a lot more than cigarettes."

"I have a mortgage."

"Everybody has bills. My cousin paid two hundred thousand for a seat on the traffic team, and he's thrilled with it." Inside the station, a seat meant a comfortable post that money could buy. "Me, I'm still saving up. Or I could skip the saving and do Director Cheng a favor, like tipping him off about a soft-hearted officer." Lei Bo smiled. "Think it over."

Two hundred thousand yuan was forty-three months of Wei Kun's salary.

Then slow, heavy boots sounded in the corridor, and Lei Bo's smile vanished as if wiped clean.

Cheng Biao, the deputy chief who ran the station, stepped in with a toothpick clamped between his teeth and liquor on his breath.

He had come straight from the banquet at Fuyuan Garden, where Fan Weiguo, the County Party Secretary, had warned him in front of the whole table. Fan Weiguo never shouted. He had once told Cheng Biao, "I keep a list, and a chair is easy to replace."

Then, less than an hour later, Yan Tiehu had vanished off the ring road without a trace, and Director Mai Zhenhua had issued a panicked county-wide freeze keeping every police unit locked inside its station without explanation. Cheng Biao's nerves were shredded, and he had been looking for someone to take it out on ever since.

"Director Cheng!" Lei Bo jumped up. "You came by in person! I was just about to report a serious breach of discipline!"

"Is that so?"

"Yes, Director! Wei Kun carried hot water to the Old Street guest! I told him not to!"

Wei Kun rose to his feet and kept his hands at his sides. "Director Cheng."

"I hear somebody is running a hotel in my holding cell."

"Director, it was only boiled water. The man is fifty-two, and the floor is bare concrete."

"Did I ask how old he was?"

"No, Director."

"Then why are you still talking?"

Silence fell over the room.

Lei Bo chimed in, "He's always soft, Director. Last month he gave a drunk his own steamed bun."

"Lei Bo."

"Yes, Director?"

"Did you actually tell him no, or did you just think it?"

Lei Bo's mouth opened and shut. "In my heart, Director." His ears turned bright red.

Cheng Biao stepped close to Wei Kun, rolling the toothpick to the corner of his mouth.

Then his hand swung up, and cracked across Wei Kun's face.

Wei Kun's head snapped to the side. His uniform cap flew off and rolled across the tiles.

His cheek stung, and something far worse burned behind it. He was thirty-six years old, with twelve years on the force, and he had just been slapped in front of a kid who smirked at everything.

Shame crept hot up his neck. He did not lift a hand to his face.

Cheng Biao spoke quietly. "Kindness costs money, Wei Kun. Who is paying for yours? You're a deputy principal staff member. That fancy title has nothing underneath it."

"Yes, Director."

"I can have you guarding the county landfill gate by Monday, and nobody will ask a single question. Do you know how many men would pay for your seat?"

"No, Director."

"More than you could ever scrape together. Your wife is due in the spring, I hear. Booked at the county maternity hospital. Their vice president has dinner with me twice a month, and good beds go to families with clean files." Cheng Biao pulled out the toothpick and studied its tip. "Yours will have a note in it by Monday."

Wei Kun's mouth went dry.

"Tomorrow morning the nephew comes back to my lobby. At 7:40 you bring me that detention notice, the one with the blank time box. You will write the hour I tell you to write. Understood?"

"Understood, Director."

"Clean yourself up."

Cheng Biao walked out, and Lei Bo trotted after him, chuckling at nothing.

The door clicked shut.

Wei Kun did not move. A moment later the door eased open a crack, and a thin boy slipped inside.

It was Zhou Pingan, the nineteen-year-old auxiliary whose cap Cheng Biao had knocked off only hours earlier.

He picked up Wei Kun's cap, brushed the dust from it on his sleeve, and offered it back with both hands.

He didn't say a word. His eyes were bright and wet, yet his back stayed straight. Then he hurried back out.

Wei Kun stared down at the cap for a long time.

His phone buzzed in his pocket, and he knew who it was before he even looked.

"Wei Kun? You're still up?"

His wife, Su Min, sounded soft and half-asleep. Her voice warmed the cold room like a lamp.

"The baby kicked twice just now. I swear she recognizes your voice. Was it a good day?"

Every night she asked, and every night he gave the same answer.

He caught sight of the red mark on his cheek reflected in the dark window glass. "A good day," he said.

"You're a terrible liar, you know." She laughed softly. "Did you eat?"

"I ate."

"The mortgage comes due on the fifth, twenty-eight hundred. I moved it out of the baby fund. There's still six thousand one hundred left, so don't stress about it."

"I'll stress anyway."

"Wei Kun." Her tone grew quiet. "Whatever is going on there, don't play the hero. Be a father. Come home to us."

His throat tightened. "I will."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

After she hung up, Wei Kun sat completely still, holding a cap he could not bring himself to put back on.
