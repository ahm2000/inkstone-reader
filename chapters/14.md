# Chapter 14: Just an Old Float

The water lay flat and green. Nothing moved except the hat brim on Chen Xuan's sleeping face.

"Chen Xuan!" said Grandfather Chen Fugen, thumping the bank with his rod. "Your uncle sat in a cell for forty hours, and you come to my reservoir to nap?"

"He came out at 6:20, Grandpa."

"Luck! Pure luck! And you slept through the luck!"

He flicked the straw hat off his grandson's head with the tip of the rod, and Chen Xuan caught it without opening his eyes.

"Mm."

Chen Fugen was seventy-eight, tall as a door, with a voice built for shouting across fields.

Beside him sat a thin old man in a gray cap and a canvas vest with too many pockets. His back was as straight as a ruler.

He was Old Zhao, seventy-six, and he had fished on this bank for three summers.

The village thought he had been a clerk in some provincial office. Nobody asked, because it is rude to ask an old clerk about his pension.

"Morning, Grandpa Zhao," said Chen Xuan, tipping the hat up an inch.

"It's eleven," said Old Zhao, without turning.

"Then it's a late morning."

"A man held forty hours for a fight he never had?" Old Zhao went on.

"A bad director held him," said Chen Fugen. "A good director let him out. And one honest young policeman gave him hot water through the bars. Wei something."

"Wei Kun," said Chen Xuan.

"Wei Kun," Old Zhao repeated, slowly, twice, like a man writing a name in a book.

Then he looked at Chen Xuan's line.

"Your hook has no worm on it."

"No."

"Why not?"

"If a fish bites, it'll wake me up."

"Lazy!" Old Zhao snapped. "Fifty years I worked. I never met a young man like you!"

"Mm," said Chen Xuan. "Lucky you."

Old Zhao glared. The corner of his mouth moved, and he did not let it.

"I need worms, and I forgot my wallet. Lend me ten yuan."

Chen Xuan gave him a bill without opening his eyes.

"Bring change."

"I'll bring worms," Old Zhao said, and stomped off.

At the bait stall, he argued over a box of worms.

"Eight yuan," said Old Zhao.

"Ten for the fat ones, Grandpa."

"Eight."

Old Zhao once signed budgets of forty billion yuan. Now he fought over two yuan like a man defending a province.

"City people," the owner muttered. "All misers."

* * *

Thirty yards behind the fishermen, a plain man in a gray jacket sat under a willow with a newspaper.

Number Nine, Mo Lin, had been there since dawn. Nobody in the village had noticed him.

Then he noticed a man selling cabbages.

The man wore a farmer's straw hat and had the straightest back in the village. His cabbages had no wilted leaves.

On the water, two men in a small boat were fishing with rods that had never once been cast.

Nine understood. The cabbage man understood in the same second. Neither moved.

"Nice weather," said the cabbage man at last, in a farmer's voice.

"For fishing," said Nine.

Slowly, below the paper, Nine opened his palm. The little black block, with its pine branch and number, lay there for one breath.

The cabbage man took a green stone tablet from his belt, no bigger than a domino, and showed it just as slowly. It had one character on its face.

Nine had read about it in a file. It was the token of a retired top leader's guard.

"Colonel Ren Gang," the cabbage man said quietly. "Forty men. Eleven years with the old gentleman. Before that, special forces."

"Nine."

"Only Nine?"

"Only Nine."

Ren looked at the badge again. His sixty-year-old back grew a little straighter, which Nine had not thought possible.

Nine felt a cold drop of sweat run down his back. He guessed Ren felt one too.

Both men looked at the sleeping boy and the old man scolding him.

"My man must not know I'm here," Ren said.

"Mine doesn't know I'm here either."

"He thinks he's alone?"

"He thinks he's eating a bun."

Ren's moustache twitched.

"My old man thinks he's a clerk on holiday."

"Your boat hasn't cast once," Nine murmured.

"Your bakery van is parked in a cabbage field."

They did not shake hands. They sat side by side like strangers, two professionals, while their principals chatted thirty yards away like two grandfathers at a bus stop.

* * *

Toward evening, Old Zhao pressed something into Chen Xuan's palm. "Take this."

It was a slim float carved from yellow boxwood, worn smooth by many hands. A tiny square seal was cut into its base.

"That plastic thing of yours is crooked. No wonder your line plays dead. Try this one."

"Oh, nice," said Chen Xuan, turning it over. "Looks old."

"Just an old float. Keep it, or you'll blame my reservoir for having no fish."

"Thanks, Grandpa Zhao. If it catches nothing, I'll blame the float."

He dropped it into his canvas bag beside the green thermos. Then he took out a glass jar with a red cloth lid and set it in the old man's hands.

"Grandma's pickled eggs. And bring the grandkids next Saturday. The bait's on me."

Old Zhao looked at the jar for a long moment.

"Hm. Eggs," he said gruffly. He held it in both hands as if it might break, and would not let his aide take it.

Chen Xuan took his grandfather's heavy tackle box without being asked.

"Watch the step, Grandpa. Your knee will blame me tomorrow."

"My knee is fine," Chen Fugen growled, and leaned on his grandson's arm all the way up the path.

"What a nice retired clerk," Chen Xuan told him. "He borrowed ten yuan and gave me a float. I owe him more than eggs."

"A clerk who eats three buns," Chen Fugen grumbled, pleased.

Behind the willows, Colonel Ren caught the old man's aide, a young man named Liang, before Old Zhao reached his car.

"A bakery van in a cabbage field," Ren said sourly. "Liang, you walked this bank at dawn. How did you miss it?"

"You said the village was yours, Colonel. I only counted the boats."

"Then you counted two boats and no vans. Count better tomorrow."

Liang bowed his head. Ren lowered his voice.

"The boy's guards are Black Pine," he murmured. "A man called Nine, one of thirty in the whole country. I don't know the boy. But whoever commands those men sits above a province."

Liang's face went the color of rice paper.

"A great family?"

"Greater."

Over the next hill, Nine spoke into a phone no bigger than a matchbox.

"Chief. The old man beside the Boss is Zhao Dehai. Retired Governor of Lanshui. Seventy-six. His guard is Colonel Ren, forty men, a green jade tally. The old man borrowed ten yuan from the Boss this morning. This afternoon he gave him his float."

There was a long silence in the quiet courtyard in Qingyan.

Then Chief An laughed, short and cracked.

"Does the Boss know who he's been feeding buns?"

"He said, sir, 'a nice retired clerk.'"

In the back of a black car, Old Zhao watched the road with his hands on his knees. The jar sat in his lap. Nobody had packed him a gift in years.

An honest boy from a great family, he thought, and he has no idea how big he is.

"Liang. Your phone."

He dialed one number from memory.

"Zhengyi. Old Zhao."

In a house in the provincial capital, Vice Director Gao Zhengyi dropped the remote and jumped up. His free hand flew to his tie.

"Governor! Yes, Governor!"

"Your team is due at Yunxi on Wednesday. Bring it Monday, at nine."

"Monday, Governor? We're not ready."

"The water looks calm to me. I don't trust calm. There's a duty officer at Old Street named Wei Kun. If he's what I hear, shake his hand."

"Yes, Governor."

The line clicked.

Gao stood in his living room with the dead phone at his ear, bowing a little to it.

"Who was that?" his wife called from the kitchen.

"My old teacher," said Gao, still pulling at his tie.

Then he asked the empty room a question no teacher had taught him.

Why would a retired Governor, who had never asked him for anything, ask him to shake a policeman's hand?
