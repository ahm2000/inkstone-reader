# Chapter 14: Just an Old Float

The water lay flat and green. Nothing moved except the hat on Chen Xuan's sleeping face.

His grandfather, Chen Fugen, thumped the bank with his fishing rod. "Little Xuan! Your Second Uncle sat in a cell for forty hours. And you come to my reservoir for a nap? Look at you, so thin! Don't they feed you?"

"He came out at 6:20 this morning, Grandpa." Chen Xuan's voice went soft. "I couldn't sleep last night. I kept my phone on my chest. That's why I'm so tired."

"Luck! It was pure luck! And you slept through the luck!"

Chen Fugen flicked the straw hat off his grandson's head with the rod. Chen Xuan caught it without opening his eyes.

"Don't be mad, Grandpa. I'm just so glad he's home."

Beside him sat a thin old man in a gray cap and a vest with too many pockets. His back was straight as a ruler.

His name was Zhao Dehai, and the village called him Old Zhao. He had fished on this bank for three summers.

The village thought he had once been a clerk in some government office. Nobody asked more.

Chen Xuan tipped the hat up an inch. "Morning, Grandpa Zhao."

"It's eleven." Zhao Dehai did not turn.

"Then it's a late morning. Those are the best kind."

"Forty hours in a cell for a fight he never had," said Zhao Dehai.

"A bad director held him. A good director let him out." Chen Fugen scratched his head. "And one honest young policeman gave him hot water through the bars. Wei something."

"Wei Kun." Chen Xuan's eyes were wet. He looked at the water so nobody would see. "He didn't even know us. He just gave a stranger a cup of water."

Zhao Dehai said the name twice, slowly, like a man writing it in a book. "Wei Kun."

Then he looked at Chen Xuan's line.

"Your hook has no worm on it."

"No."

"Why not?"

"If a fish bites, it will wake me up. I don't want to be woken up."

"Lazy!" Zhao Dehai snapped. "I worked fifty years. I never met a young man like you!"

Chen Xuan grinned. "Lucky you. Now you have."

Zhao Dehai glared. The corner of his mouth moved, and he did not let it.

"I need worms, and I forgot my wallet. Lend me ten yuan."

Chen Xuan gave him a bill without opening his eyes.

"Bring change, Grandpa Zhao."

"I'll bring worms." Zhao Dehai walked off, stamping his feet.

At the bait stall he held up two fingers. "Eight yuan." The seller said ten. "Eight."

Zhao Dehai, the Retired Governor of Lanshui, had once signed budgets of forty billion yuan. Now he fought over two yuan like a man guarding a whole province.

The stall owner muttered, "City people. All misers."

* * *

Thirty meters behind the fishermen, a plain man in a gray jacket sat under a willow tree with a newspaper.

His name was Mo Lin. He was a guard of the Detail, the secret team that watched over Chen Xuan, and his code name was Number Nine. He had been there since dawn.

Then he noticed a man selling cabbages.

The man wore a farmer's straw hat and had the straightest back in the village. His cabbages had no old leaves.

On the water, two men in a small boat were fishing with rods that had never once been thrown out.

Mo Lin understood. A bodyguard knows another bodyguard. The cabbage man understood in the same second. Neither moved.

The cabbage man spoke in a farmer's voice. "Nice weather."

"For fishing," said Mo Lin.

Slowly, below the newspaper, Mo Lin opened his hand. A little black block with a pine branch lay there for one breath. It was the sign of the Black Pine, the Detail's highest order.

The cabbage man took a green stone token from his belt. It was no bigger than a domino. He showed it just as slowly. It was the token of a retired top leader's personal guard.

"Colonel Ren Gang," he said, very low. "Forty men. Eleven years with the old gentleman."

"Mo Lin. Number Nine."

"Black Pine." Ren Gang looked at the badge again. His back grew even straighter. "There are only thirty of you in the country."

"My man must not know I'm here," said Ren Gang.

"Mine doesn't know I'm here either."

"He thinks he's alone?"

"He thinks he's eating a bun."

Ren Gang's moustache twitched.

"My old man thinks he's a clerk on holiday."

Mo Lin glanced at the water. "Your boat hasn't thrown out a line all day."

"Your bakery van is parked in a cabbage field."

They did not shake hands. They sat like strangers, two professionals. The two men they guarded chatted like grandfathers at a bus stop.

* * *

Near five o'clock, Zhao Dehai pressed something into Chen Xuan's hand. "Take this."

It was a slim fishing float of yellow wood, worn smooth by many hands. A tiny square seal was cut into its base.

"That plastic thing of yours is crooked. No wonder your line plays dead. Try this one."

Chen Xuan turned it over. "Oh, nice. It looks old."

"Just an old float. Keep it, or you'll blame my reservoir for having no fish."

"Thanks, Grandpa Zhao. If it catches nothing, I'll blame the float."

He dropped it into his canvas bag beside the green thermos. Then he took out a glass jar with a red cloth lid and set it in the old man's hands.

"My grandma's pickled eggs. And bring your grandkids next Saturday. I'll buy the bait."

Zhao Dehai looked at the jar for a long moment.

"Hm. Eggs." His voice was rough. He held the jar in both hands as if it might break. He would not let his young aide, Liang Yuhang, carry it.

Chen Fugen grabbed the heavy tackle box before his grandson could touch it.

"Too thin to carry this, my Xuan."

"Okay, Grandpa." Chen Xuan sighed happily and let himself be pulled up the path by the sleeve. "He's a nice old clerk. He borrowed ten yuan and gave me a float."

"A clerk who eats three buns." Chen Fugen grumbled, but he looked pleased.

Behind the willows, Ren Gang caught Liang Yuhang before Zhao Dehai reached his car.

"Liang, you walked this bank at dawn and missed a bakery van in a cabbage field."

"I only counted the boats, Colonel."

"Did you see the float? The seal on it is the old man's own mark. In eleven years he has given it to no one. Today he gave it away for ten yuan of worms."

Liang Yuhang blinked. "To a boy with no worm on his hook?"

"To a boy whose guards wear the Black Pine. One is called Nine. There are only thirty like him in the whole country. I don't know the boy. But whoever gives orders to those men stands above a province."

Liang Yuhang's face went white as paper.

"A great family?"

"Greater."

Over the next hill, Mo Lin spoke into a phone no bigger than a matchbox.

"Chief. The old man beside the Boss is Zhao Dehai, Retired Governor of Lanshui. His guard is Colonel Ren Gang, forty men. This afternoon the old man gave the Boss a float with his own seal. Everyone in Lanshui knows that seal. Whoever holds it is his friend."

There was a long silence in the quiet courtyard in Qingyan.

Then An Zhiyuan, Chief of the Detail, gave a short, dry laugh.

"Does the Boss know who he's been feeding buns?"

"He said, sir, 'a nice retired clerk.'"

In the back of a black car, Zhao Dehai watched the road with his hands on his knees. The jar sat in his lap. Nobody had given him a gift in years.

A lazy, honest boy, he thought. He never once asked who I was. I like how calm he is.

"Liang. Your phone."

He dialed one number from memory.

"Zhengyi. Old Zhao."

In a house in the provincial capital, Gao Zhengyi, a Provincial Vice Director, dropped the remote and jumped up. His hand flew to his tie.

"Governor! Yes, Governor!"

"Your team is due at Yunxi on Wednesday. Bring it tomorrow, Monday, at nine."

"Tomorrow, Governor? We're not ready."

"Good. A man who has time to get ready also has time to hide things. The water looks calm to me. I don't trust calm. And there's a duty officer at Old Street named Wei Kun. If he's what I hear, shake his hand."

"Yes, Governor."

The line clicked.

"Who was that?" his wife called.

"My old teacher." Gao Zhengyi was still pulling at his tie.

Why would a retired Governor ask him to shake a policeman's hand? The old man had never asked him for anything before.
