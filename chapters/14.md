# Chapter 14: Just an Old Float

The reservoir water lay flat and green. Nothing stirred on the bank except the straw hat resting over Chen Xuan's sleeping face.

His grandfather, Chen Fugen, thumped the ground with his fishing rod. "Chen Xuan! Your Second Uncle spent forty hours in a cell, and you come to my reservoir to nap? Look how thin you are! Don't they feed you at work?"

"He came out at 6:20 this morning, Grandpa." Chen Xuan's voice was soft and drowsy. "I couldn't sleep all night. I just kept my phone on my chest waiting for news. That's why I'm worn out."

"Pure luck! It was sheer luck, and here you are sleeping through it!"

Chen Fugen flicked the straw hat off his grandson's face with the tip of his rod. Chen Xuan caught it smoothly without opening his eyes.

"Don't be mad, Grandpa. I'm just relieved he's home."

Beside them sat a lean old man in a gray cap and a fishing vest with too many pockets. His back was straight as a ruler.

His name was Zhao Dehai, a quiet retiree who had fished along this bank for three summers.

The villagers assumed he had once been a clerk in some county office, and nobody ever thought to ask more.

Chen Xuan tipped his hat up an inch. "Morning, Mr. Zhao Dehai."

"It's eleven o'clock." Zhao Dehai did not turn his head.

"Then it's a late morning. Those are the best kind."

"Forty hours in a holding cell for a fight he never started," Zhao Dehai remarked.

"A corrupt director locked him up, and an honest director let him out." Chen Fugen scratched his head. "Plus, one decent young policeman passed him hot water through the bars. Officer Wei something."

"Wei Kun." Chen Xuan's gaze softened as he looked out over the water. "He didn't even know us. He just gave a stranger a cup of hot water."

Zhao Dehai repeated the name twice, slowly, like a man committing it to memory. "Wei Kun."

Then he glanced at Chen Xuan's fishing line.

"Your hook has no worm on it."

"No."

"Why not?"

"If a fish bites, it'll wake me up. I don't want to be woken up."

"Lazy!" Zhao Dehai snapped. "I worked for fifty years, and I've never met a young man like you!"

Chen Xuan grinned. "Lucky you. Now you have."

Zhao Dehai glared. The corner of his mouth twitched, though he refused to let it turn into a smile.

"I need worms, and I forgot my wallet. Lend me ten yuan."

Chen Xuan handed him a bill without opening his eyes.

"Bring back the change, Mr. Zhao Dehai."

"I'll bring worms." Zhao Dehai marched off, his steps brisk and firm.

At the bait stall, he held up two fingers. "Eight yuan." The vendor asked for ten. "Eight."

Zhao Dehai, the Retired Governor of Lanshui Province, had once signed off on budgets of forty billion yuan. Now he haggled over two yuan as fiercely as if he were guarding an entire province.

The stall owner muttered under his breath, "City folks. Every last one is a miser."

* * *

Thirty meters behind the fishermen, an unremarkable man in a gray jacket sat under a willow tree reading a newspaper.

His name was Mo Lin. He was a guard of the Detail, the secret unit assigned to watch over Chen Xuan, and his code name was Number Nine. He had been in position since dawn.

Then his eyes caught a man selling cabbages nearby.

The vendor wore a farmer's straw hat, yet he had the straightest posture in the village, and his cabbages did not have a single wilted leaf.

Out on the water, two men sat in a small wooden boat holding fishing rods whose lines had never once been cast.

Mo Lin understood instantly. A bodyguard always recognizes another bodyguard. The cabbage vendor understood in the very same second. Neither of them shifted.

The vendor spoke in a rough rural drawl. "Nice weather."

"Good for fishing," Mo Lin replied.

Slowly, beneath the fold of his newspaper, Mo Lin opened his palm. A small black plaque carved with a pine branch rested there for a single breath. It was the emblem of the Black Pine, the Detail's highest tier.

The cabbage vendor drew a green stone token from his belt, no larger than a domino, and showed it just as slowly. It was the mark of a retired senior leader's personal guard.

"Colonel Ren Gang," he murmured. "Forty men. Eleven years with the old gentleman."

"Mo Lin. Number Nine."

"Black Pine." Ren Gang glanced at the emblem again, his back straightening even further. "There are only thirty of you in the whole country."

"My principal must not know I'm here," Ren Gang said quietly.

"Mine doesn't know I'm here either."

"He thinks he's alone?"

"He thinks he's eating a steamed bun."

Ren Gang's moustache twitched.

"My old gentleman thinks he's a retired clerk on holiday."

Mo Lin glanced out at the reservoir. "Your boat hasn't cast a single line all day."

"Your bakery van is parked in the middle of a cabbage field."

They did not shake hands. They remained seated like strangers, two professionals doing their jobs while the two men they guarded chatted like neighbors at a bus stop.

* * *

Near five o'clock, Zhao Dehai pressed a small object into Chen Xuan's hand. "Take this."

It was a slender fishing float carved from yellow wood, worn smooth by years of handling. A tiny square seal was engraved into its base.

"That crooked plastic thing of yours is useless. No wonder your line never moves. Try this one."

Chen Xuan turned it over in his fingers. "That's nice. Looks old."

"Just an old float. Keep it, or you'll blame my reservoir for having no fish."

"Thanks, Mr. Zhao Dehai. If I still catch nothing, I'll blame the float."

He tucked it into his canvas bag beside his green thermos, then pulled out a glass jar topped with a red cloth lid and set it in the old man's hands.

"My grandma's pickled eggs. Bring your grandkids next Saturday, and I'll buy the bait."

Zhao Dehai stared down at the jar for a long moment.

"Hm. Eggs." His voice turned gruff. He cradled the jar in both hands as though it were fragile porcelain, refusing to let his young aide, Liang Yuhang, carry it.

Chen Fugen snatched up the heavy tackle box before his grandson could reach for it.

"You're too thin to carry this, Chen Xuan."

"Alright, Grandpa." Chen Xuan sighed happily and let his grandfather tug him up the path by the sleeve. "He's a nice retired clerk. Borrowed ten yuan and gave me a float."

"A clerk who eats three buns," Chen Fugen grumbled, though he looked pleased.

Behind the willows, Ren Gang caught Liang Yuhang before Zhao Dehai reached the car.

"Liang Yuhang, you swept this bank at dawn and somehow missed a bakery van parked in a cabbage field."

"I only counted the boats, Colonel."

"Did you see that float? The seal on its base is the old gentleman's personal mark. In eleven years, he has never given it to anyone. Today he traded it for ten yuan's worth of worms."

Liang Yuhang blinked in disbelief. "To a young man with no worm on his hook?"

"To a young man whose guards carry the Black Pine. One of them is Number Nine. There are only thirty like him in the entire country. I don't know who that boy is, but anyone who commands those men stands far above a province."

The color drained from Liang Yuhang's face.

"A powerful family?"

"Higher than that."

Over the next ridge, Mo Lin spoke into a phone no larger than a matchbox.

"Chief. The old man sitting beside the Boss is Zhao Dehai, Retired Governor of Lanshui. His detail is led by Colonel Ren Gang, forty men strong. This afternoon, Zhao Dehai gave the Boss a fishing float bearing his personal seal. Everyone in Lanshui recognizes that seal. Whoever holds it is counted as his friend."

A long silence hung over the quiet courtyard in Qingyan.

Then An Zhiyuan, Chief of the Detail, let out a short, dry laugh.

"Does the Boss have any idea who he's been feeding buns to?"

"He called him, sir, 'a nice retired clerk.'"

In the back seat of a black sedan, Zhao Dehai watched the road roll past with his hands resting on his knees. The jar of pickled eggs sat securely in his lap. Nobody had given him a simple, honest gift in years.

A lazy, decent boy, he thought. He never once asked who I was. I like how steady he is.

"Liang Yuhang. Hand me your phone."

He dialed a number from memory.

"Gao Zhengyi. It's Zhao Dehai."

In a living room in the provincial capital, Gao Zhengyi, a Provincial Vice Director, dropped his television remote and bolted upright, his hand flying straight to his collar.

"Governor! Yes, Governor!"

"Your inspection team is scheduled for Yunxi on Wednesday. Move it up to tomorrow, Monday, at nine."

"Tomorrow, Governor? We aren't finished preparing."

"Good. Anyone given time to prepare also has time to hide things. The surface looks calm to me, and I don't trust calm water. Also, there's a duty officer at Old Street Station named Wei Kun. If he's the man I heard about, shake his hand."

"Yes, Governor."

The line clicked dead.

"Who was that?" his wife called from the hallway.

"My old mentor." Gao Zhengyi was still fumbling with his tie.

Why on earth would a retired governor ask him to shake a county policeman's hand? The old man had never asked him for a personal favor before.
