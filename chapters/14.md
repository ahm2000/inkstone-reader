# Chapter 14: The Empty Chair

Past one in the morning, the last customer left Gong's Noodles.

Number Twenty-three stood beside the window table and looked at the fourth chair. A fresh bowl of noodles had been set there at midnight. It had gone cold.

He cleared it away, washed the bowl and set out a clean one.

"Same tomorrow?" Number Sixty-eight asked from the doorway.

"The same."

"Three years, and he has never sat in that chair. He doesn't even know this shop is ours."

"He will." Number Twenty-three lined up the chopsticks until they lay perfectly straight. "An empty chair is a promise. When the Boss finally walks in and sits down, I want the bowl to be hot."

On the wall behind the counter, a small monitor still showed one green dot inside a ring. It marked the Boss. The monitor was wired to the shop's own camera, not to the dead Black Pine network, so the masks had let it be. The Zero watch had taken the Guard's weapons and its orders tonight. It had left them their eyes.

The dot sat in Room 601 of Building 2, Fuxing New Village.

"He's asleep?" Number Sixty-eight whispered.

Number Fifty-six came in through the back door with his helmet under his arm. "Out cold. Number Nine is in the flat across the hall. He says the Boss was snoring ten minutes after he lay down."

"Fifty meters of masks around his bed," said Number Sixty-eight. "Jets over the whole county. Half the world is awake because of him, and he's snoring."

"Does he know what he did?"

"He knows he made one call and went to bed," Number Twenty-three said. "He doesn't know the capital hasn't sat down since."

They watched the green dot in silence. Not one Black Pine team could act.

Under the dot, one line of text glowed. It was Number Nine's note from the family's dinner.

*8:30 a.m.: the Boss goes to Old Street Station to sit in the lobby.*

Number Twenty-three shook his head. "A bench. I wonder what that station will do when it learns who is sitting on it."

* * *

An hour earlier, just before midnight, in the duty room of Old Street Police Station, Wei Kun's hands were still warm.

He sat at the desk and stared at them as if they belonged to somebody else.

An old man in a holding cell had taken a paper cup through the bars with both hands and said, "Thank you, young man."

Nobody in this building had thanked Wei Kun for anything in three years.

So why did he feel like a thief?

"Officer Wei."

Lei Bo leaned in the doorway, cracking sunflower seeds between his teeth. He was grinning, but the grin sat stiff on his face.
"Caught you on the hallway monitor. Room service for the guest, eh?"

"He was cold." Wei Kun kept his voice low. "It was just a cup of water."

"Sure, sure." Lei Bo shut the door and sat on the edge of the desk. "Only Director Cheng said no comforts for the Old Street holdouts. A cold night makes a quick signature. You know the drill."

"I know."

"I'd hate for him to hear about it." Lei Bo rubbed his thumb across his fingertips. "Silence is cheap, Wei Kun. A carton of good cigarettes, eight hundred yuan, and my memory goes foggy. Or it stays sharp, and I tell Director Cheng before breakfast. Your wife is seven months along, isn't she? One salary, one mortgage, one baby coming. A bad review costs a lot more than cigarettes."

"I have a mortgage."

"Everybody has bills. My cousin paid two hundred thousand for a seat on the traffic team." "Me, I'm still saving. Or I skip the saving and do Director Cheng a favor, like telling him about a soft-hearted officer." Lei Bo smiled. "Think it over."

Two hundred thousand yuan was forty-three months of Wei Kun's pay.

Then slow, heavy boots sounded in the corridor, and Lei Bo's smile vanished.

Cheng Biao, second in command of the county police and director of Old Street Station, came in with a toothpick between his teeth and liquor on his breath. When the door clicked shut behind him, he flinched. Then he cleared his throat to cover it.

At a banquet at Fuyuan Garden, Fan Weiguo, the County Party Secretary, had warned him in front of the whole table. Fan Weiguo never shouted. He had once told Cheng Biao, "I keep a list, and a chair is easy to replace." Within the hour, Yan Tiehu had vanished off the ring road. Then Mai Zhenhua, Director of the County Police Bureau, had frozen every police unit in the county inside its station and given no reason. Not one patrol car had left Cheng Biao's yard since half past ten.

His nerves were shredded, and he wanted someone to take it out on.

"Director Cheng." Lei Bo jumped up. "Any word on Brother Tie?"

"Do not say that name in this building."

"Yes, Director." Lei Bo swallowed. "Then I must report a breach of discipline. Wei Kun carried hot water to the Old Street guest. I told him not to."

"Is that so?"

Wei Kun rose and kept his hands at his sides. "Director Cheng."

"I hear somebody is running a hotel in my holding cell."

"Director, it was only boiled water. The man is fifty-two, and the floor is bare concrete."

"Did I ask how old he was?"

Nobody spoke.

"He's always soft, Director," Lei Bo said. "Last month he gave a drunk his own steamed bun."

"Lei Bo."

"Yes, Director?"

"Did you actually tell him no, or did you just think it?"

Lei Bo's mouth opened and shut. "In my heart, Director." His ears turned bright red.

Cheng Biao stepped close to Wei Kun and rolled the toothpick to the corner of his mouth.

Then his hand swung up and cracked across Wei Kun's face.

Wei Kun's head snapped sideways. His cap flew off and rolled across the tiles.

His cheek stung, and something worse burned behind it. He was thirty-six, with twelve years on the force, and he had just been slapped in front of a kid who smirked at everything. He did not lift a hand to his face.

"Kindness costs money, Wei Kun," Cheng Biao said quietly. "Who is paying for yours? You are a duty officer with a fancy title and nothing under it."

"Yes, Director."

"I can put you on the county landfill gate by Monday, and nobody will ask why. Do you know how many men would pay for your seat?"

"No, Director."

"More than you could ever scrape together. Your wife is due before the New Year, I hear. She is booked at the county maternity hospital. Its vice president eats dinner with me twice a month, and the good beds go to families with clean files." Cheng Biao pulled out the toothpick and looked at its tip. "Yours will have a note in it by Monday."

"The clerk said he would come back tomorrow for the written notice, and men like that always do. Tomorrow morning Chen Mingde's nephew sits in my lobby. At ten to eight you bring me the detention notice, the one with the blank time box. You will write the hour I tell you to write. Understood?"

"Understood, Director."

"Clean yourself up."

Cheng Biao walked out, and Lei Bo trotted after him. At the door he glanced once at the black window and the empty street beyond it. His laugh died.

The door clicked shut.

Wei Kun did not move. The door opened a crack, and Zhou Pingan slipped in. He was the nineteen-year-old auxiliary whose own cap Cheng Biao had knocked to the floor not long before.

He picked up Wei Kun's cap, brushed off the dust, and held it out with both hands. He said nothing. His eyes were wet, but his back stayed straight. Then he hurried out.

Wei Kun looked at the cap for a long time.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He knew who it was before he looked.

"Wei Kun? You're still up?"

His wife, Su Min, sounded soft and half asleep.

"The baby kicked twice just now. I swear she knows your voice. Was it a good day?"

He saw the red mark on his cheek in the dark window glass.

"A good day," he said.

"You're a terrible liar." She laughed softly. "The mortgage is due on the fifth, twenty-eight hundred. I moved it out of the baby fund. There's still six thousand one hundred left, so don't stress."

"I'll stress anyway."

"Wei Kun." Her voice went quiet. "Whatever is going on there, don't play the hero. Be a father. Come home to us."

His throat tightened. "I will."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

After she hung up, Wei Kun sat still, holding a cap he could not bring himself to put back on.
