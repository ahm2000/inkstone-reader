# Chapter 151: The Sealed Box from Zurich

Two weeks had passed since the storm in Shanghai ended, yet the lights on the sixth floor of the capital's State Security building had not gone out once.

Inside the long meeting room, the air was thick with cigarette smoke and the bitter smell of strong dark tea. Blue and red case folders covered almost every inch of the polished oak table. At the center sat a heavy steel box, its brass lock pried open, resting beside three thick stacks of freshly printed paper.

Ding Guoping stood at the head of the table with his jacket draped over his chair and his white shirt sleeves rolled to his elbows. Dark circles sat beneath his eyes, but his gaze was sharp as a blade. Across from him sat Gao Zhengyi and Zhou Zhenhua, both leaning forward with lit cigarettes resting between their fingers.

"We thought the Shanghai raid gave us the top of the ladder," Ding Guoping said quietly, his voice rough from two nights without sleep. "We were wrong."

Gao Zhengyi tapped ash into the glass tray and looked up. "You told us yesterday that the Shanghai drives pointed straight to Zurich's Northern Ledger. Did the team find a new list of names?"

"Not just names," Ding Guoping replied. He picked up a slender black drive from inside the steel box and set it gently on top of the printed pages. "When our men broke the outer locks on the Shanghai files, everything looked clean and finished. Three overseas brokers, twelve fake shipping shells, and a tidy paper trail ending in Zurich. It looked too neat. So I told our people to keep digging."

Zhou Zhenhua narrowed his eyes and exhaled a thin stream of smoke. "And they found a second layer."

"They found a hidden room inside the box," Ding Guoping said. He slid three stapled packets across the table so both men could read them. "Two hours ago, our lead team cracked the deepest file on the last drive. Zurich's Northern Ledger was never the real owner. It was only the European branch."

The room went completely silent. Only the faint hum of the ceiling vent filled the air.

Gao Zhengyi flipped open the top page. His hand paused halfway down the sheet, and the line between his eyebrows deepened. "The Seven-Harbor Consortium," he read aloud slowly.

"Yes," Ding Guoping said, pulling a cigarette from his pack and lighting it with a quick click of his lighter. "An older, far deadlier offshore group. Most of their elders left the mainland forty years ago and built private shipping empires across Southeast Asia. While everyone watched European banks and fancy Swiss offices, this group spent decades buying up private docks, crooked customs agents, and hidden money routes from Singapore all the way up to our southern coast."

Zhou Zhenhua turned two pages quickly, his face darkening with every line. "Look at these numbers. Three years ago, they moved 6 billion yuan in untaxed cargo through three southern bays. Last year alone, the hidden cash flowing through their private piers hit 15 billion yuan. They are not just smuggling goods. They are running a shadow wall right along our southern sea."

"It gets worse," Ding Guoping said, blowing a long plume of smoke toward the tall window. "Turn to page nine. When we smashed their northern brokers in Qingyan and Shanghai, we cut off their northern arm. They did not run away. They pulled all their remaining ships and money straight back to their oldest stronghold on the mainland."

Gao Zhengyi turned to page nine and tapped a bold red circle on the map. "Nanhai City."

"Nanhai City," Ding Guoping confirmed grimly. "Out of the seven harbors on their chart, six sit outside our borders. The seventh is Nanhai Port. Right now, half of the private berths on the southern edge of Nanhai answer to local dock families who act as their gatekeepers. On paper, those families run normal coastal freight companies. Behind closed doors, they decide whose ships get unloaded, whose cargo disappears, and which foreign containers slip past customs at midnight."

Zhou Zhenhua stubbed his cigarette hard into the ashtray. "No wonder our southern shipping reports looked so odd over the past six months. Every time a clean mainland company tried to open a direct coastal line into Nanhai, their ships ran into sudden delays, broken cranes, or missing permits. We thought it was ordinary local greed."

"It is not ordinary greed," Gao Zhengyi said, his voice cold and steady. He leaned back in his chair, staring at the red circle on the map. "It is a closed door. If a coastal city's docks answer to an offshore consortium instead of the law, then every honest company trying to ship south has to kneel at their gate."

Ding Guoping nodded slowly. "Our teams have already pulled the travel logs of three foreign middlemen tied to Zurich. Two of them flew into Southeast Asia last week, and one took a private boat into Nanhai Bay three days ago. The trouble is, the local dock families cover their tracks well. If we send a loud inspection team straight from the capital right now, they will hide their ledgers, burn the records, and wait out the storm."

"Then we do not kick the door down while they are watching the street," Gao Zhengyi said calmly. He picked up his teacup, found it cold, and set it back down. "Keep your people watching the money lines from Zurich and Southeast Asia. Map every single pier in Nanhai that connects to this Seven-Harbor Consortium. Do not alert the local port bureau until we know which officials are taking their envelopes."

"Understood," Ding Guoping said. He paused, glancing toward the quiet red telephone sitting on the corner cabinet. "Should we send a brief note to the Western Hills tonight?"

Gao Zhengyi thought for a long moment before shaking his head gently. "Elder Song is already aware that the Shanghai drives had a deeper bottom. Let us gather hard proof on the Nanhai docks first before we trouble him. And as for the young man in Qingyan, leave him out of this. He just finished a long month. Let him have a peaceful weekend with his family."

Zhou Zhenhua let out a dry chuckle, reaching for a fresh cigarette. "He would probably rather eat home-cooked noodles than read a stack of port ledgers anyway."

"That is exactly why we should not bother him," Gao Zhengyi said with a faint smile that faded almost as quickly as it appeared. "Because once the southern docks start biting honest people, trouble has a way of finding its own path."

Three hundred miles south in Qingyan, the Friday evening breeze felt cool and gentle along the old neighborhood lane.

There were no maps, no red circles, and no smoke-filled rooms inside Chen Xuan's small rented apartment. Only the warm light of a single ceiling bulb shone down on his wooden table.

Chen Xuan stood beside the table, carefully checking two red paper boxes of soft walnut pastries from the bakery on Old Street. Beside them sat a neatly folded dark red wool shawl, soft and thick enough to keep out the late-year mountain wind. He tucked both boxes and the shawl into a clean woven shopping bag so nothing would get crushed on the bus ride tomorrow.

His phone buzzed softly against the tabletop. He picked it up and smiled as he read the short message from his father, Chen Jianguo: *Your mother already packed three bags of fruit. Meet us at the east gate at seven tomorrow morning. Do not forget a warm coat.*

Chen Xuan typed back a quick reply: *Got it, Dad. I will be there early.*

He set the phone down, rinsed his scratched green thermos at the sink, and placed it upside down on a clean towel to dry. Tomorrow was Grandma Zhou Guilan's eighty-first birthday back in Hongshi Village, and he had already asked Sun Haoran for a quiet Saturday off.

Down on the street, fifty meters from the entrance of Chen Xuan's building, the evening crowd moved past the bright fruit stalls and noodle carts. Beneath the shadow of a bus stop sign, a Zero Guard member in a plain dark jacket stood motionless with hands resting casually in their pockets. No one walking past gave the quiet figure a second glance.

Upstairs, Chen Xuan turned off the kitchen light, checked the window latch, and went to bed early, looking forward to nothing more than a bowl of hot village noodles.
