# Chapter 151: The Sealed Box from Zurich

Two weeks after the Shanghai storm ended, the lights on the sixth floor of the capital's State Security building had not gone out once.

Inside the smoky meeting room, case folders covered the oak table around a pried open steel box and three stacks of printed paper.

Ding Guoping stood at the head of the table with rolled shirt sleeves and sharp eyes despite dark circles. Across from him sat Gao Zhengyi and Zhou Zhenhua, holding lit cigarettes.

"We thought the Shanghai raid gave us the top of the ladder," Ding Guoping said quietly, his voice rough from two sleepless nights. "We were wrong."

Gao Zhengyi tapped ash into the tray. "You told us yesterday the Shanghai drives pointed to Zurich's Northern Ledger. Did the team find new names?"

"Not just names," Ding Guoping replied, setting a slender black drive on the printed pages. "When our men broke the outer locks on the Shanghai files, everything looked finished. Three overseas brokers, twelve fake shipping shells, and a paper trail ending in Zurich. It looked too neat. So I told our people to keep digging."

Zhou Zhenhua narrowed his eyes. "And they found a second layer."

"They found a hidden room inside the box," Ding Guoping said, sliding stapled packets across the table. "Two hours ago, our lead team cracked the deepest file on the last drive. Zurich's Northern Ledger was never the real owner. It was only the European branch."

The room went silent except for the ceiling vent.

Gao Zhengyi opened the top page and paused. "The Seven Harbor Consortium," he read aloud slowly.

"Yes," Ding Guoping said, lighting a cigarette. "An older, deadlier offshore group. Their elders left the mainland forty years ago and built private shipping empires across Southeast Asia. While everyone watched European banks, this group spent decades buying private docks, crooked customs agents, and hidden money routes from Singapore up to our southern coast."

Zhou Zhenhua turned two pages, his face darkening. "Look at these numbers. Three years ago, they moved 6,000,000,000 yuan in untaxed cargo through southern bays. Last year, hidden cash flowing through their private piers hit 15,000,000,000 yuan. They are running a shadow wall right along our southern sea."

"It gets worse," Ding Guoping said. "Turn to page nine. When we smashed their northern brokers in Shanghai and Shenzhen, they did not run. They pulled all their ships and money back to their oldest mainland stronghold."

Gao Zhengyi turned to page nine and tapped a red circle on the map. "Shenzhen."

"Shenzhen," Ding Guoping confirmed grimly. "Of the seven harbors on their chart, six sit outside our borders. The seventh is Shenzhen Port. Right now, half the private berths in southern Shenzhen answer to local dock families acting as gatekeepers. On paper, they run normal coastal freight companies. Behind closed doors, they decide whose ships get unloaded, whose cargo disappears, and which foreign containers slip past customs at midnight."

Zhou Zhenhua stubbed out his cigarette. "No wonder our southern shipping reports looked odd over the past six months. Every time a clean company opened a coastal line into Shenzhen, their ships hit delays, broken cranes, or missing permits. We thought it was ordinary local greed."

"It is not ordinary greed," Gao Zhengyi said coldly. "It is a closed door. If a coastal city's docks answer to an offshore consortium instead of the law, every honest company shipping south has to kneel at their gate."

Ding Guoping nodded. "Our teams pulled travel logs of three foreign middlemen tied to Zurich. Two flew into Southeast Asia last week, and one took a private boat into Shenzhen Bay three days ago. The local dock families cover their tracks well. If we send a loud inspection team from the capital now, they will hide their ledgers, burn records, and wait out the storm."

"Then we do not kick the door down while they watch the street," Gao Zhengyi said calmly. "Keep watching the money lines from Zurich and Southeast Asia. Map every pier in Shenzhen tied to the Seven Harbor Consortium. Do not alert the local port bureau until we know which officials take their envelopes."

"Understood," Ding Guoping said, glancing at the red phone on the cabinet. "Should we send a note to the Western Hills tonight?"

Gao Zhengyi shook his head. "Elder Song knows the Shanghai drives had a deeper bottom. Let us gather hard proof on the Shenzhen docks first. And leave the young man in Shanghai out of this. He just finished a long month. Let him have a peaceful weekend with his family."

Zhou Zhenhua chuckled dryly. "He would rather eat home cooked noodles than read port ledgers anyway."

"That is why we should not bother him," Gao Zhengyi smiled faintly. "Once the southern docks bite honest people, trouble finds its own path."

Three hundred miles south in Shanghai, the Friday evening breeze was cool.

Inside his rented room, Chen Xuan stood by his wooden table checking two red boxes of walnut pastries from Old Street and a folded dark red wool shawl, packing them into a woven bag for tomorrow's bus ride.

His phone buzzed with a message from his father, Chen Jianguo: "Your mother packed three bags of fruit. Meet us at the east gate at seven tomorrow morning. Bring a warm coat."

Chen Xuan typed back: "Got it, Dad. I will be there early."

He rinsed his scratched green thermos at the sink and set it to dry. Tomorrow was Grandma Zhou Guilan's eighty first birthday in Hongshi Village, and he had asked Sun Haoran for Saturday off.

Fifty meters from his building entrance, an unnumbered Black Guard member in plain black gear stood motionless under a bus stop shadow while the evening crowd walked past without a second glance.

Upstairs, Chen Xuan turned off the light and went to bed early, looking forward to hot village noodles.
