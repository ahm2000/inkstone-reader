# Chapter 95: Packing the Green Thermos

By eleven-fifteen on Monday night, the small apartment in Fuxing New Village had settled back into its quiet, familiar routine.

Down on Old Street, Mingde Hardware's iron shutter was locked tight for the night. Chen Mingde was already asleep in his own bed for the first time in days. Wu Lanying had walked them to the mouth of the alley with a warm smile softening the lines around her eyes.

Inside the cozy kitchen, Liu Meifang twisted the metal lid onto a thick glass jar filled with bright red homemade pickled chilies, garlic cloves, and ginger slices. She pressed her thumb against the middle of the lid to check the seal, then wrapped the jar tightly in two sheets of folded newspaper and a plastic bag.

"Hold the top of the canvas bag open, Chen Xuan," Liu Meifang told him, nudging his elbow. "Set it flat at the bottom so the oil does not leak onto your clean shirts."

Chen Xuan leaned over the dining table and held open his worn dark blue travel bag. "Mom, that is already the third jar. If I add any more pickled chilies and braised pork, the strap is going to snap before I even reach the county bus station."

"That canvas strap is double-stitched," Liu Meifang said briskly, tucking a second newspaper-wrapped jar beside the first and wedging a folded cotton towel between them. "The lunch hall food near Hengxin Logistics is all grease and no taste. Last time you came home, your face looked thinner. Eating plain rice with a spoonful of my pickled chilies is ten times better than ordering oily takeout."

From the wooden chair across the table, Chen Jianguo lowered the county newspaper he had been folding and looked over the rim of his reading glasses. "Let her pack it, Chen Xuan. If you argue with Liu Meifang about food, she will stay up until one in the morning frying peanuts to stuff into your pockets too."

"I am not staying up for peanuts," Liu Meifang said, though the corner of her mouth twitched upward. She wiped her hands on her apron and glanced at the wall clock above the cupboard. "Eleven-twenty already. Chen Xuan, zip that bag up and go wash your face. Your father is right, you need to sleep."

Chen Jianguo set his folded newspaper neatly beside his teacup and looked steadily at his son. "The first express bus to Shanghai leaves at six-forty. If you miss it, the seven-thirty bus hits the ring road traffic and you will walk into Hengxin Logistics late."

"I set my alarm for five-fifty, Dad," Chen Xuan said easily. He zipped the travel bag shut and stood it upright beside the chair leg. "I will not be late."

Chen Jianguo paused, his rough fingers resting lightly on the edge of the wooden table. The worry that had kept his shoulders tight all weekend had finally faded away, leaving only a father's quiet care.

"Chen Xuan," Chen Jianguo asked softly, "how are things really going at Hengxin Logistics? Does your supervisor give you a hard time for taking two days off to come home?"

"Not at all," Chen Xuan answered with an easy, calm smile. "I finished my monthly shipping lists on Friday afternoon before I left. Twelve thousand yuan a month, steady shifts, a quiet office, and nobody bothers me as long as the shipping sheets balance."

Chen Jianguo nodded slowly, his eyes crinkling with relief. "Good. Do not envy those young men who brag about chasing fast money or trading favors with big builders. Look at what happened to Deng Yongchang in Shanghai on the evening news tonight. Yesterday everyone thought he owned half the city, and today he is in handcuffs. A clean heart and a steady twelve-thousand-yuan paycheck will let you sleep soundly every night of your life."

"Your father is right," Liu Meifang agreed, stepping back from the kitchen sink carrying Chen Xuan's scratched green thermos. She had rinsed it well with boiling water and dried the dented steel rim with a clean cloth. "Keep your head down, do your job well, and stay away from noisy crowds."

She handed the green thermos across the table. "Here. I checked the rubber ring inside the cap. It still seals tight with no leaks. Fill it with hot tea before you get on the bus tomorrow morning."

Chen Xuan took the green thermos in both hands. The chipped dark green paint along its curve felt smooth and familiar against his palms. He twisted the cap open a quarter turn, checked the clean inside, and screwed it shut again with a soft click.

"Thank you, Mom," Chen Xuan said warmly. "And Dad, do not carry heavy crates for Uncle tomorrow morning. Wait until the delivery boys unload them."

"Listen to him telling me what to do," Chen Jianguo grumbled fondly, standing up and stretching his back. "Your uncle Chen Mingde already has Yu Changshun coming over at seven to help hang that new canvas awning. We are not helpless old men."

"Still, take it easy," Chen Xuan replied, picking up his canvas bag and his green thermos. "Good night, Dad. Good night, Mom."

"Sleep early," Liu Meifang called softly as she turned off the kitchen light. "I will have boiled eggs and millet porridge ready at six."

Chen Xuan stepped into his small bedroom and clicked the latch shut. The room was small and neat: a single wooden bed with a freshly aired cotton quilt, a plain desk by the window, and a worn plastic chair.

He set his travel bag beside the desk and placed the scratched green thermos right next to his plain silver wristwatch.

Before turning off the desk lamp, Chen Xuan glanced once at the plain black second phone lying face-down on the desk. The screen stayed completely dark. There were no security alerts and no urgent messages. Beyond the quiet walls of Fuxing New Village, the nationwide clean-up was already rolling forward on its own, provincial offices were cleaning up their books, and Old Street would wake up at dawn to its first true morning of peace.

Forty meters down the dark lane beneath the locust tree, two still shadows stood watch in silence without asking for a word of thanks.

Chen Xuan did not pick up the second phone. Instead, he checked the alarm on his ordinary phone, made sure it was set for five-fifty in the morning, and switched off the desk lamp.

In the quiet dark of the bedroom, he rested his head on the cotton pillow and smiled faintly.

Tomorrow morning, he would let his family think he took the six-forty bus, then fly back to Shanghai on the jet Number 7 had arranged, carry his green thermos and his mother's jars of pickled chilies up to his rented apartment, and clock in at Hengxin Logistics as an ordinary twelve-thousand-yuan clerk.
