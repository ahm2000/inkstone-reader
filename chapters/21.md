# Chapter 21: The Forgotten Witness

Sunday afternoon settled over Old Street in Yunxi County with the dry warmth of late autumn. Sunlight slanted across cracked gray roof tiles onto damp flagstones. Normally the lane echoed with mahjong tiles and sizzling woks, but today wooden shutters hung half-drawn across most storefronts, and faded red demolition notices curled along the brick walls.

Chen Xuan walked unhurriedly down the lane in his plain dark cotton jacket, carrying his dented green metal thermos in his right hand. He never glanced toward rooftops or side alleys, yet he noticed every closed door and every wary pair of eyes behind dusty glass.

Forty paces back inside Gong's Noodles, a solitary customer in a charcoal jacket ate slowly from a bowl of clear-broth noodles. Number Twenty-three kept his eyes loosely on a folded newspaper, yet his peripheral vision held the entire lane in clean focus. Every passing scooter and shifting blind registered without a twitch of his shoulders. He never looked directly at Chen Xuan, and Chen Xuan never looked toward Gong's Noodles.

Near the southern bend, Chen Xuan stopped outside a narrow tailor shop whose wooden sign had faded almost bare. Behind cloudy glass sat bolts of dark cotton, a cast-iron sewing machine, and worn brass shears. Old tailor Yu Changshun sat outside the threshold on a low wooden stool, a pair of gray trousers resting across his knees with a threaded needle motionless between his fingers.

"Uncle Yu Changshun," Chen Xuan said softly, his voice even and respectful.

Yu Changshun blinked behind wire-framed spectacles. Recognizing Chen Xuan, the tight crease between his eyebrows eased slightly, though unease lingered in his eyes. "Chen Xuan. You came back from Qingyan."

"I came back yesterday," Chen Xuan replied. He pulled a second wooden stool from beside the doorframe, brushed the dust away, and sat beside the old tailor. "Mind if I sit for a few minutes?"

"Sit, sit," Yu Changshun muttered, setting the trousers on a scarred tea table between them. He poured hot water from a chipped enamel kettle into two porcelain mugs of coarse green tea. "The street is dead anyway. Nobody gets trousers hemmed when half the block is waiting for bulldozers."

Chen Xuan set his green thermos by his foot and accepted the warm mug with both hands. Steam rose between them, carrying the clean, slightly bitter scent of mountain tea.

"How is your father holding up?" Yu Changshun asked quietly. "Chen Jianguo has always been a proud man. What they did to Chen Mingde on Friday night was shameless. Everyone here knows Chen Mingde spent thirty years fixing neighbors' fuses and drains without taking a fen."

"My father is resting at home," Chen Xuan said calmly. "We are handling it."

Yu Changshun sighed. "Be careful, Chen Xuan. You work an ordinary office job in Qingyan, so you only see the surface of Yunxi County during holidays. This place is not what it was when you were a boy."

Chen Xuan took a slow sip of tea, his fingers steady on the porcelain. "Hongrui Real Estate has only pushed this demolition for a year, Uncle Yu Changshun. Why is everyone on Old Street too frightened to even open their shutters?"

Yu Changshun leaned forward, his eyes flicking toward the northern corner and then across to a shuttered hardware shop two doors down. His voice dropped to a guarded rasp barely louder than dry leaves scraping the gutter.

"Because this is not the first time Hongrui Real Estate wanted the southern end of Old Street," Yu Changshun murmured. "Three years ago, before those red notices ever went up, they tried to buy the four shops closest to the old stone drainage culvert. Three owners took the low offer and moved across the river. One man refused."

"Who was he?" Chen Xuan asked mildly.

"Lin Guoliang," Yu Changshun said, the name heavy on his tongue. "He ran the small hardware shop past Chen Mingde's store. A stubborn, honest man. His wife died years ago, and he raised his daughter, Lin Xiaoyu, alone behind that counter. When Zhou Quan sent men offering half the market price, Lin Guoliang threw the contract back into the street."

"Was it only about the price?" Chen Xuan asked softly.

Yu Changshun's knuckles turned pale around his mug. "At first, we thought so. Then one night over tea here, Lin Guoliang told me the truth. His back storeroom sat directly above the old masonry culvert leading toward the river quarry. For months, between two and four in the morning, he felt low vibrations through his concrete floor, like heavy wheeled carts rolling underground. When he pried up a loose stone slab near his backyard drain, he smelled wet clay and diesel exhaust rising from a tunnel that had no business being there."

The autumn breeze rattled a loose tin sheet overhead. Chen Xuan remained completely still, his fingers resting steadily against the green metal thermos beside his knee.

"Lin Guoliang photographed the slab and wrote to the county construction bureau," Yu Changshun whispered, grief darkening his eyes. "Four days later, Yan Tiehu brought six men into his shop and smashed his shelves. Lin Guoliang still refused to sign. Two weeks after that, he rode his bicycle down the empty county road east of the bridge at eleven at night to buy stomach medicine. A flatbed truck struck him from behind and crushed him against the guardrail."

"What did the station say?" Chen Xuan asked, his tone flat and quiet.

"Old Street Station closed the file in forty-eight hours," Yu Changshun said bitterly. "Station Director Cheng Biao signed the report himself, calling it a drunk-driving crash caused by Lin Guoliang swerving into the lane. Everyone on Old Street knew Lin Guoliang had not touched alcohol in twenty years because of his stomach ulcer. The driver surrendered, paid a small sum through a broker, and vanished."

"And his daughter?" Chen Xuan asked.

Yu Changshun closed his eyes briefly. "Lin Xiaoyu was nineteen, studying accounting at a college in Lanshui. She came home for the funeral and petitioned the county office. Within a month, Yan Tiehu's men showed up at her dormitory with forged debt notes claiming her father owed Hongrui Real Estate three hundred thousand yuan. They harassed her classmates and threatened administrators until she was forced out of school. The last I heard, she fled the province to stay safe. Nobody here dares speak the name Lin Guoliang anymore."

Silence settled over the wooden table. Chen Xuan sat in complete stillness, his expression calm and ordinary, his fingers steady on his green thermos. Yet Yu Changshun felt an inexplicable chill brush his neck, as though winter clouds had swallowed the sun.

"Thank you for the tea, Uncle Yu Changshun," Chen Xuan said gently. He set a small tin of Longjing tea on the table and stood. "Keep your door locked at night, and do not sign anything."

Yu Changshun looked up, startled by the calm certainty in his voice. "Chen Xuan, do not provoke Yan Tiehu or Cheng Biao. They have people behind them in the county and in Lanshui."

"I know," Chen Xuan replied mildly.

He lifted his green thermos and walked slowly back up Old Street. Across the lane inside Gong's Noodles, Number Twenty-three set down his chopsticks, tucked a banknote under the saucer, and tapped a coded sequence on a dark terminal beneath the table. Within three seconds, Lin Guoliang and Lin Xiaoyu were logged at top priority inside the Black Pine Guard archive, bound directly to the tunnel beneath Old Street.
