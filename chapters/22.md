# Chapter 22: The Shutters Go Up

Yunxi County, Old Street. Friday, 4:15 p.m.

A new notice was taped to the lamppost outside Mingde Hardware, and half the street was crowded around it.

*Notice from the Old Street Project Headquarters: The deadline for selling shops is cancelled. All signing talks are suspended until the review is finished.*

"Cancelled!" the grocer cried. "Read it again!"

"I've read it four times," said Old Yu, the tailor. He had been on this street for forty-four years, and he leaned in close with his glasses on his nose. "Look at the bottom. How many stamps do you count?"

"Three!"

"Three. In all those years, I've never seen a notice with more than one. The first is the county. The second is the city." Old Yu's voice dropped. "The third one says Provincial Inspection Team. I've never in my life stood this close to a provincial stamp."

Nobody said a word. A stamp that big on a lamppost? Whose notice was this really?

Beside the shop's front door, something else had changed. For nine days, four of Dong's men had stood on this corner in black jackets, and one of them had always sat on a red plastic stool, watching every customer. Today, the men were gone. Only the stool remained.

A little boy sat down on it and swung his legs. Nobody stopped him.

"Quick, quick, we only have forty-five minutes!" Second Aunt Wu Lanying hurried up the sidewalk, holding a bucket and a cloth. "Mingde is reopening at five o'clock sharp, and the shutter hasn't been wiped in a week!"

Within minutes, the whole street had joined in. Meng Xiaofang pushed her steamer cart to the corner, her little daughter riding on the lid. "Free buns for everybody today." The grocer carried over a tray of eggs. The tea seller brought a big pot of jasmine tea. And the fruit stall owner appeared with a long red string of firecrackers, which he hung from the shutter's rail with great pride.

"It's too long," said Second Aunt.

"It's perfect," said the fruit seller. "Nobody ever complained that a firecracker was too long."

At 4:40, Old Yu came down the street carrying a folded red cloth. He had sewn it overnight, he said, and he'd used up all his gold thread. He and the grocer lifted it over the door.

*Mingde Hardware: Still Here.*

The stitches were so straight they looked drawn with a ruler. People clapped. Old Yu pretended not to hear and fixed his glasses for a very long time.

Across the lane, the owner of a shop that had signed in week one watched the banner go up with a tight, complicated smile. He was glad for Old Chen. He was also doing arithmetic in his head about what his own shop might have been worth if he'd simply waited two more weeks, and the arithmetic wasn't kind to him.

Then Chen Jianguo came roaring up on his battered electric scooter, with Liu Meifang on the back seat holding a pot of pork-rib soup on her lap, and Chen Hao behind her, one earbud in, a math book under his arm.

"Brother!" Chen Jianguo parked, took off his helmet, and spoke in his most important voice. "As the old saying goes, 'The tortoise does not hurry, but the tortoise opens its shop on time.'"

"Which old saying, Dad?" Chen Yuan had come straight from school, and she was already crossing her arms. "Which book?"

"A very old book," Chen Jianguo said, with dignity. "It was burned."

Everybody laughed, even Chen Mingde, who stood in front of his own shutter holding the key with both hands. His fingers were still thin from a day and a night in a cell, and the key was shaking.

"Second Uncle, you can do it," Chen Hao said quietly.

"Thirty seconds!" the grocer shouted, looking at his watch.

Meng Xiaofang's little daughter climbed onto the stool, where nobody was sitting anymore, and raised both hands to count. "Three! Two! Four! One!"

"Four isn't in the right place!" Chen Yuan said.

"It's right!" the girl declared, and the whole street roared.

Chen Mingde turned the key. The iron shutter rattled up, and a long, loud, wonderful crackle of firecrackers filled Old Street. Smoke drifted, the neighbors cheered, and inside the shop the little wooden drawers waited in their rows, with *M4* and *M5* still written in his handwriting. Everything was exactly where he had left it.

Second Uncle didn't say anything. He just stood very still in his own doorway and held on to Second Aunt's sleeve.

Yunxi County, Old Street. Friday, 6:40 p.m.

Chen Xuan had taken the 5:50 bus, twenty minutes earlier than usual, because his mother had texted: *Come straight to the shop. No dinner at home. Don't ask.*

He pushed through a crowd that smelled of firecracker smoke, tea, and steamed buns. The shop was full. Old Yu was drinking tea with the tea seller. A group of schoolboys had somehow found the box of nails.

"Xuan!" Liu Meifang cried from behind the counter. "You're skinny! Eat!"

"Luck!" Chen Jianguo declared loudly, pointing at the banner. "That's all it was. We have the best luck on the whole street."

"Mm," Chen Xuan said. "Oh, nice."

The shop owner from Old Beef Noodles stepped in just then, with a tray of steaming bowls on one arm. He was a quiet, flour-dusted man with a calm face, and everyone on the street knew him only as Gong Yi.

"On the house," Gong Yi said, setting a bowl in front of Chen Mingde. "For the opening."

Chen Mingde took one bite and stopped. "Hey, this is the same bowl they sent me at the station window, with the egg!" Old Beef Noodles was the nearest shop to the station, and the only one that sent food through the family window.

"All my bowls taste the same," Gong Yi said. "That's the whole point of a noodle shop."

And he went back out, perfectly calm, while Chen Mingde frowned at the bowl and Chen Xuan took a bite of a steamed bun.

When the crowd thinned, Chen Mingde pulled his nephew behind the counter, beside the wooden drawers. He lowered his voice, and his face was serious.

"Xuan, tell me honestly. Who was behind it? Somebody must have spoken for me. I've been wondering for a whole week."

Chen Xuan thought about it for one second. "Probably my friend who knows forms."

"A friend who knows forms." Chen Mingde laughed softly and shook his head. "Then tell your friend I owe him a very long time."

He turned, pulled open the drawer marked *M4*, and scooped up a handful of small bright screws. Gently he pressed them into Chen Xuan's palm.

"For luck," he said. "Like when you were seven. You remember? You hung five of them on your fishing line, and you were sure they'd bring a fish."

"I caught a fish the size of my thumb."

"You did. And you let it go." Chen Mingde closed his nephew's fingers over the screws. "I knew then you'd be all right. I still know it. I don't need to know who helped me, Xuan. I know you came on the first bus."

The screws were cold, and then warm. Chen Xuan put them carefully into his jacket pocket, beside the thermos.

"Thank you, Second Uncle," he said.

At seven o'clock, he stepped out into the cool evening to breathe. The firecrackers were done, and the street lamps had come on, one by one, and across the road the sign of Old Beef Noodles glowed yellow.

Under the sign stood a thin girl in a jacket three sizes too big. She held a plastic bag with both hands. She wasn't looking at the food in the window. She was looking at the street, at every face that passed, one by one, with a very steady look.

Then her eyes reached Chen Xuan, and stayed.

"Hmm?" he said softly.

She didn't look away.
