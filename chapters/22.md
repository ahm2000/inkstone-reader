# Chapter 22: The Shutters Go Up

On Friday afternoon, a new notice was taped to the lamppost outside Mingde Hardware, and half the street was crowded around it.

*Notice from the Old Street Project Headquarters: The deadline for selling shops is cancelled. All signing talks are suspended until the review is finished.*

"Cancelled, it says cancelled!" the grocer cried. "Somebody read it again for me!"

"I have read it four times," said Old Yu, the tailor, who had worked on this street for forty-four years. "Look at the bottom. How many stamps do you count?"

"Three!"

"Three, and in all my years I have never seen a notice with more than one." Old Yu's voice dropped. "The first is the county, and the second is the city. The third says Provincial Inspection Team."

Silence.

A stamp that big, on a plain lamppost? Whose notice was this really?

Beside the shop's front door, something else had changed. For nine days, four of Dong's men in black jackets had stood on this corner, and one of them had always sat on a red plastic stool to watch every customer.

Today the men were gone, and only the red stool was left behind.

A little boy sat down on it and swung his legs, and nobody stopped him.

"Quick, quick, we only have forty-five minutes!" Second Aunt Wu Lanying hurried up the sidewalk with a bucket and a cloth. "Mingde reopens at five o'clock sharp, and the shutter has not been wiped in a week!"

Within minutes, the whole street had joined in.

Meng Xiaofang pushed her steamer cart to the corner with her little daughter riding on the lid, and she called out, "Free buns for everybody today!"

The grocer carried over a tray of eggs. The tea seller brought a big pot of jasmine tea. And the fruit stall owner hung a long red string of firecrackers from the shutter's rail with great pride.

"That string is much too long," said Second Aunt.

"It's perfect," said the fruit seller. "Nobody ever complained that a firecracker was too long."

Soon.

Just before reopening, Old Yu came down the street carrying a folded red cloth that he had sewn overnight, and he said he had used up all his gold thread.

He and the grocer lifted it over the door.

*Mingde Hardware: Still Here.*

The stitches ran so straight that they looked drawn with a ruler, and the people clapped, while Old Yu pretended not to hear and fixed his glasses for a very long time.

Across the lane, the owner of a shop that had signed in week one watched the banner go up with a tight, complicated smile.

He was glad for Old Chen, but he was also doing arithmetic in his head about what his own shop might have been worth if he had simply waited two more weeks, and the arithmetic was not kind to him.

Then Chen Jianguo came roaring up on his battered electric scooter, with Liu Meifang on the back seat holding a pot of pork-rib soup on her lap, and Chen Hao behind her, one earbud in and a math book under his arm.

"Brother!" Chen Jianguo parked, took off his helmet and spoke in his most important voice. "As the old saying goes, 'The tortoise does not hurry, but the tortoise opens its shop on time.'"

"Which old saying, Dad?" Chen Yuan had come straight from school, and she was already crossing her arms. "Which book?"

"A very old book," Chen Jianguo said, with dignity. "It was burned."

Everybody laughed, even Chen Mingde, who stood before his own shutter holding the key with both hands.

His fingers were still thin from nearly two days in a cell, and the key was shaking.

"Second Uncle, you can do it," Chen Hao said quietly.

"Thirty seconds!" the grocer shouted, looking at his watch.

Meng Xiaofang's little daughter climbed onto the stool, where nobody sat anymore, and raised both hands to count.

"Three! Two! Four! One!"

"Four is not in the right place!" Chen Yuan said.

"It's right!" the girl declared, and the whole street roared.

Chen Mingde turned the key, and the iron shutter rattled up.

Crack, crack, crack!

A long, loud crackle of firecrackers filled Old Street, the neighbors cheered, and inside, the little wooden drawers waited in their rows, with *M4* and *M5* still written in his handwriting.

Everything inside was exactly where he had left it a week ago.

Second Uncle did not say anything. He just stood very still in his own doorway and held on to Second Aunt's sleeve.

---

By early evening, Chen Xuan had arrived on an earlier bus, earlier than usual, because his mother had texted: *Come straight to the shop. No dinner at home. Don't ask.*

He pushed through a crowd that smelled of firecracker smoke, tea and steamed buns, and found the shop full.

"Xuan!" Liu Meifang cried from behind the counter. "You are skinny! Did you eat? Eat!"

"Luck!" Chen Jianguo declared, pointing at the banner. "That's all it was. We have the best luck on the whole street."

"Mm," Chen Xuan said. "Oh, nice."

The owner of Old Beef Noodles stepped in just then, with a tray of steaming bowls on one arm.

He was a quiet, flour-dusted man with a calm face, and everyone on the street knew him only as Gong Yi.

"On the house," Gong Yi said, setting a bowl in front of Chen Mingde. "For the opening."

Chen Mingde took one bite and stopped. "Hey, this is the same bowl they sent me at the station window, with the egg!"

Old Beef Noodles was the nearest shop to the station, and the only one that sent food through the family window.

"All my bowls taste the same," Gong Yi said. "That is the whole point of a noodle shop."

He went back out, perfectly calm, while Chen Mingde frowned at the bowl and Chen Xuan took a bite of a steamed bun.

When the crowd thinned, Chen Mingde pulled his nephew behind the counter, beside the wooden drawers, and lowered his voice.

"Xuan, tell me honestly. Who was behind it? Somebody must have spoken for me, and I have wondered for a whole week."

Chen Xuan thought about it for one second. "Probably my friend who knows forms."

"A friend who knows forms." Chen Mingde laughed softly and shook his head. "Then tell your friend I owe him a very long time."

He pulled open the drawer marked *M4*, scooped up a handful of small bright screws and pressed them gently into Chen Xuan's palm.

"For luck," he said. "Like when you were seven. You remember? You hung five of them on your fishing line, and you were sure they would bring a fish."

"I caught a fish the size of my thumb."

"You did, and you let it go." Chen Mingde closed his nephew's fingers over the screws. "I knew then that you would be all right. I still know it. I don't need to know who helped me, Xuan. I know you came on the first bus."

The screws felt cold at first, and then they slowly turned warm.

Chen Xuan put them carefully into his jacket pocket, beside the thermos.

"Thank you, Second Uncle," he said, and he meant every word.

---

Chen Xuan stepped out into the cool evening to breathe.

The firecrackers were done, the street lamps had come on one by one, and across the road the sign of Old Beef Noodles glowed yellow.

Under the sign stood a thin girl in a jacket three sizes too big, holding a plastic bag with both hands.

She was not looking at the food in the window. She was looking at the street, at every face that passed, one by one, with a very steady look.

Then her eyes reached Chen Xuan, and they stayed on him.

"Hmm?" he said softly.

She did not look away.
