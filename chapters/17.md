# Chapter 17: Thirty-Yuan Leftovers

Chen Xuan had come in on the early bus from Yunxi, still half asleep. All he wanted was hot tea and a quiet Monday.

He got a warm tea egg instead. Ge Qinghe, the sixty-one-year-old cleaner everyone called Auntie Ge, pushed it into his hand at the elevator.

"Thanks, Auntie Ge." His whole face lit up. "You're the nicest person on this floor. Don't tell the others."

Pan Xiulan, the coworker everyone called Sister Pan, stood at the whiteboard with a marker in her fist. She looked like a judge who had waited all year to bang the hammer.

"Third quarter scores, everybody! First place, Chen Xuan, 93.6. Second place, Zhang Pengfei, 93.4!"

For one second nobody breathed. Then thirty heads turned toward the same desk.

"Only zero point two? Pengfei worked nineteen late nights!"

"And Chen leaves at 4:55 every day. I've seen him run for the elevator!"

Zhang Pengfei stood in front of the board in his pressed suit. His face went white, then pink, then red as a boiled shrimp. He had worked nineteen late nights. He had lost by two-tenths of a point to a man who ate buns at his desk.

"Quiet, please." Hu Dewei, the department manager, held his flowerpot mug. "Only one name from our department goes up to General Manager Sun as Employee of the Quarter. That person goes to the top of the year-end promotion list. The gap is small, so Pengfei asked for a recount tomorrow at nine."

Chen Xuan lifted his thermos an inch. "Morning, Manager. A recount is fine with me. I like numbers. They're nice and quiet."

"Of course, of course," said Pan Xiulan. An hour ago she had called Chen Xuan a sleepwalker. Now she looked at the ceiling.

Zhang Pengfei straightened his tie. He pulled back his left sleeve until a silver watch caught the light.

"Don't take it hard, Supervisor Chen. A promotion should go to the one who earns it. Not to a low-level man who wins by luck. By the way, my wife's uncle at the Port Authority picked this Longines for me. Nine thousand eight hundred yuan. He says a future department head must look the part."

The room made a small, jealous noise. The Port Authority decided whose ships unloaded first, so his uncle could make or break a freight company.

Nine thousand eight hundred was almost a month of Chen Xuan's pay. The message was plain: my family has a harbor, and you have a bun.

Chen Xuan looked at the watch for half a second.

"Nine thousand eight hundred?" He kept a straight face. "It suits you. Wear it to the recount."

Zhang Pengfei waited for envy or anger, or even one twitch of shame. He got a nod and then a yawn.

"Chen Xuan!" Pan Xiulan snapped, changing sides in one breath. "A winner should at least look worried! Look, he's yawning!"

"Sorry, Sister Pan." Chen Xuan rubbed his eyes with a rueful half smile. "The bus. I was up at five."

Zhang Pengfei leaned close so that only Chen Xuan could hear. He smiled for the room.

"Tomorrow at nine, Supervisor, I will show everyone what you really are. My wife's uncle decides which of Hengxin's ships unload first. One call, and your name goes on the transfer list for the Harbor Road warehouse, four hours north. It is a three-year posting."

Chen Xuan looked at him for the first time that morning. He had no more heat than a man looking at a clock.

"Pengfei." His voice was very quiet. "The second container's invoice is on my desk. Your initials are on it, and it charges us storage twice. The Port's own stamp approved both. When the audit opens it, someone will ask whose uncle works there."

Zhang Pengfei's smile stayed on his face, but the color left it. Pan Xiulan's marker dropped from her fingers. Nobody picked it up.

His mouth had gone dry. How did a clerk know what was in the Port's own books?

* * *

When the hot water met the leaves, a smell rose over the cubicles. It was deep and sweet and clean, like rain on a mountain.

Pan Xiulan stopped in the middle of a word.

Hu Dewei's nose went up, and his feet followed it all the way to Chen Xuan's desk.

"Supervisor Chen. May I?"

"Sure, Manager. It's hot, so go slow."

Chen Xuan poured a small paper cup. Hu Dewei took one careful sip and closed his eyes. Then his eyes flew open.

He had tasted this once, at the birthday dinner of Sun Haoran, General Manager of Hengxin Logistics. Sun Haoran had opened a locked wooden box and handed out one pinch each, like gold.

"What is this tea?" The cup shook in his hand.

"Just green tea. Thirty yuan a bag, from Uncle Hao's stall on Binjiang Road." Chen Xuan blew on his lid. "He said it was last year's leftovers and nobody wanted it. So he gave me a good price. I know that feeling."

"Thirty?"

Pan Xiulan grabbed the cup and sniffed it once. She shrieked so loud that the whole floor jumped.

"My sister's husband runs a tea shop! This is Cloud Peak! The real one! Eighty thousand yuan a jin!"

A jin is half a kilo. The floor did the math faster than it ever had on a spreadsheet. That was nearly seven months of Chen Xuan's pay, in one bag of leaves.

"My brother-in-law says one half-jin gift box bought a man his license renewal in one week." Pan Xiulan's voice was hushed and greedy. "No forms. No waiting. Just a box!"

"Wait." Lin Dong, the youngest clerk, tapped his phone calculator until his thumb hurt. "Five grams fill a thermos. That's eight hundred yuan every morning!"

"Eight hundred?"

"Two days of his pay, poured into one scratched thermos!"

"Then Uncle Hao is a fool."

"Or a saint."

"Or Chen Xuan's family is so rich that this is what they feed the dog."

Of the thirty people on the floor, nineteen decided he was secretly rich. Six decided Hao Jingshan was a fool. Five decided the whole thing was a trick.

Chen Xuan listened to all of it and scratched his head. What was so strange about a cup of tea?

Zhang Pengfei slowly pulled his sleeve down over the Longines. A minute ago it had felt like a crown. Now it felt like a toy from a gift shop.

He had called Chen Xuan low-level. And Chen Xuan drank the price of that watch in tea every twelve days.

"Show-off." His ears burned. "Acting humble, my foot. It's a trick. Tomorrow at nine I'll prove it in front of Sun."

"Supervisor Chen." Hu Dewei wiped the rim with his sleeve. "If you ever want the window seat, it's yours."

"Thanks, Manager. But I like my corner. The sun hits my bun just right."

"Of course you do," said Hu Dewei, very softly.

* * *

That evening, in the supply closet, Ge Qinghe stood between two mop buckets with a black phone pressed to her ear. She was not smiling like a gentle cleaner now.

She was Number Fourteen of the Detail, in its Inner Ring, the inner circle of thirty. She had nearly dropped her mop that morning when the smell of the tea reached the hallway.

"Chief, I know that batch." Her voice was low. "It's from Uncle Hao's private shelf. A hundred grams of it sat on your study table last spring."

On the other end, An Zhiyuan, Chief of the Detail, let out a long breath.

Hao Jingshan was a Detail man too, with a rank far bigger than his tea stall. He served the Boss in his own way: he sold him his best leaves for thirty yuan and called them leftovers.

"Go on."

"Eleven of our people were in the building today. They sent me forty-one messages. One asked: *Fourteen, does the tea put his secret in danger?*"

"What did you answer?"

"That the tea is only tea. The danger is a whole floor talking about him."

"Does he see it?"

"He thanked me for a tea egg, Chief. And Manager Hu stood on the phone for nine minutes after the Boss left. He called General Manager Sun."

"What did Sun say?"

"He's coming down to the twelfth floor himself, tomorrow at nine, for the recount."

An Zhiyuan was quiet. Ge Qinghe felt a cold little thought land in her chest.

Nobody in the world was supposed to know how big Chen Xuan really was. Yet a scratched thermos and a thirty-yuan bag had made a whole floor argue about it. And the head of the whole company was coming down to the twelfth floor to taste it himself.

"Chief." She waited a moment. "What does a man like Sun want from a cup of that tea?"
