# Chapter 17: Thirty-Yuan Leftovers

Chen Xuan had come in on the early bus from Yunxi, still half asleep. All he wanted was hot tea and a quiet Monday.

He got neither, except for a warm tea egg Auntie Lu had pressed into his hand at the elevator.

"Thanks, Auntie Lu," he had said, and meant it.

Sister Pan stood at the whiteboard with a marker in her fist, like a judge who has waited all year to swing the hammer.

"Third quarter scores, everybody! First place, Chen Xuan, 93.6. Second place, Zhang Pengfei, 93.4!"

For one second nobody breathed. Then thirty heads turned toward the same desk.

"Zero point two? Pengfei worked nineteen late nights!"

"And Chen leaves at 4:55 every day. I've seen him run for the elevator!"

Zhang Pengfei stood in front of the board in his pressed suit. His face went white, then pink, then the color of a boiled shrimp. Nineteen nights, and he had lost by two-tenths of a point to a man who peeled buns at his desk.

"Quiet, please," said Manager Hu Dewei, clutching his flowerpot mug. "Only one name from our department goes up to General Manager Sun as Employee of the Quarter. That person goes to the top of the year-end promotion list. The gap is small, so Pengfei asked for a recount tomorrow at nine."

"Morning, Manager," said Chen Xuan, lifting his thermos an inch. "Recount is fine."

"Of course, of course," said Sister Pan, who had called Chen Xuan a sleepwalker an hour ago and now looked at the ceiling.

Zhang Pengfei straightened his tie and pulled his left sleeve back until a silver watch caught the light.

"Don't take it hard, Supervisor Chen. A promotion should go to the one who earns it, not an entry-level man who wins by luck. By the way, my wife's uncle at the Port Authority picked this Longines for me. Nine thousand eight hundred yuan. He says a future department head must look the part."

The room made a small envious noise, because the Port Authority decides whose ships unload first.

Nine thousand eight hundred was almost a month of Chen Xuan's pay. The message was plain: my family has a harbor, and you have a bun.

"Pengfei, what a good uncle," Sister Pan breathed.

Chen Xuan looked at the watch for about half a second.

"Nine thousand eight hundred?" he said, with a perfectly straight face. "It suits you. Wear it to the recount."

Zhang waited for envy or anger, or even one twitch of shame. He got a nod, and then a yawn.

"Chen Xuan!" Sister Pan snapped, switching sides like a weathervane in a gust. "A winner should at least look worried! Look, he's yawning!"

Zhang leaned close, so that only Chen Xuan could hear, and smiled for the room.

"Tomorrow at nine, Supervisor, I take the mask off you. My wife's uncle can also make a transfer order land on your desk, for a warehouse four hours north. I'd hate for your thermos to get lost on the way."

Chen Xuan looked at him for the first time that morning, with no more heat than a man looking at a clock.

"Pengfei," he said, very quietly. "Send it. Then ask your uncle how long he has left at the Port."

Sister Pan's marker dropped from her fingers, and nobody picked it up.

Zhang's smile stayed on his face, but his mouth had gone dry.

Chen Xuan went back to his thermos and unscrewed the cap.

* * *

When the hot water met the leaves, a smell rose over the cubicles, deep and sweet and clean, like rain falling on a mountain.

Sister Pan stopped talking in the middle of a word.

Hu Dewei's nose went up, and his feet followed it all the way to Chen Xuan's desk.

"Supervisor Chen. May I?"

"Sure, Manager. Careful, it's hot."

Chen Xuan poured a small paper cup. Hu took one careful sip and closed his eyes. Then his eyes flew open.

He had tasted this once, at General Manager Sun's birthday dinner. The chairman had opened a locked wooden box and handed out one pinch each, like gold.

"What is this tea?" he whispered, and the cup trembled.

"Just green tea. Thirty yuan a bag, from Uncle Hao's stall on Binjiang Road." Chen Xuan blew on his lid. "He said it was last year's leftovers and nobody wanted it, so he gave me a good price."

"Thirty?"

Sister Pan snatched the cup, sniffed it once, and let out a shriek that made the whole floor jump.

"My sister's husband runs a tea wholesale shop! This is Cloud Peak! The real one! Eighty thousand yuan a jin!"

A jin is half a kilo. The floor did the math faster than it ever had on a spreadsheet: nearly seven months of Chen Xuan's pay, in one bag of leaves.

"My brother-in-law says one half-jin gift box bought a man his license renewal in one week," Sister Pan said, in a hushed and greedy voice. "No forms, no waiting. Just a box!"

"Wait," said Lin Dong, the youngest clerk, tapping his phone calculator until his thumb hurt. "Five grams fill a thermos. That's eight hundred yuan every morning!"

"Eight hundred?"

"Two days of his pay, poured into one scratched thermos!"

"And he calls it leftovers!"

"Then Uncle Hao is a fool."

"Or a saint."

"Or Chen Xuan's family is so rich that this is what they feed the dog."

Of the thirty people on the floor, nineteen decided he was secretly rich, six decided Uncle Hao was a fool, and five decided the thermos was a trap.

Chen Xuan listened to all of it and wondered what was so strange about a cup of tea.

Uncle Hao sat outside an empty shop cracking sunflower seeds, and he looked so happy every time Chen Xuan bought a bag.

Zhang slowly pulled his cuff down over the Longines. A minute ago it had felt like a crown. Now it felt like a bracelet from a toy shop.

He had called Chen Xuan entry-level. Chen Xuan drank the price of that watch in tea every twelve days.

"Show-off," he muttered, and his ears burned. "Low profile, my foot. It's a trick. Tomorrow at nine I'll pull the mask off in front of Sun."

Nobody heard him except Hu Dewei, who was on his second half cup and did not hear anything.

Hu had been afraid of that corner desk since March, when he looked down from the window at 4:55 and saw an empty black car idling at the curb.

"Supervisor Chen," said Hu at last, wiping the rim with his sleeve. "If you ever want the window seat, it's yours."

"Thanks, Manager. I like my corner."

"Of course you do," said Hu, very softly.

* * *

That evening, in the supply closet, Auntie Lu stood between two mop buckets with a black phone pressed to her ear. She was not smiling like a gentle sixty-one-year-old cleaner.

She was Number Fourteen of the Inner Ring, and she had nearly dropped her mop that morning when the smell of the tea reached the hallway.

"Chief, I know that batch," she said quietly. "It's from Old Hao's private shelf. Two ounces of it sat on your study table last spring."

On the other end, Chief An Zhiyuan let out a long breath.

Old Hao held a rank far bigger than his tea stall. He sold the Boss his best leaves for thirty yuan, because the Boss was the only customer who never asked questions.

"Go on."

"Eleven of our field people were in the building today. They sent me forty-one messages. The best one says: *Fourteen, is the thermos an exposure risk?*"

"What did you answer?"

"I said the Boss is the risk. The thermos is just a mug." A small, tired laugh. "He thanked me for a tea egg, Chief. Manager Hu stood on the phone for nine minutes after the Boss left. He talked to General Manager Sun."

"What did Sun say?"

"He's coming down to the twelfth floor himself, tomorrow at nine, for the recount."

Chief An was quiet, and Auntie Lu felt a cold little thought land in her chest.

Nobody in the world was supposed to know how big the Boss really was. Yet a scratched thermos and a thirty-yuan bag had made a whole floor argue about it, and a General Manager was already walking toward them with his tie straight.

"Chief," she said. "What does a man like Sun want from a cup of that tea?"
