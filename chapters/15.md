# Chapter 15: Seating Order

Eleven chairs stood around the long oak table, and every chair told the room how much its owner mattered.

The Standing Committee is the small group of men who really run a county. The closer a man sits to the Party Secretary, the more power he has. So every man there felt his chair like a hand on his back.

Seat One faced the door. It was Party Secretary Fan Weiguo, in a plain white shirt, turning his teacup a quarter turn.

His eyes went down the table, one chair at a time.

Seat Two was County Magistrate Tao Shu. He ran the government, and he had waited eight years for Fan's chair.

Tao smiled at him like a kind nephew. Fan's stomach went cold. In Tao's briefcase lay a gray folder with one word on it in small, neat writing: *Fan.*

Seat Three was Kang Guangming of the Discipline Commission, a gray man who could investigate anyone, even Seat One. Fan could never guess what he was thinking.

Seat Four was Shao Wenhua of the Organization Department. He decided every promotion, and he had not spoken for nine meetings. Fan felt hope and fear in the same breath, because a silent man might be anyone's friend.

Seat Five was Executive Deputy Magistrate Lu Wenzhe, round and comfortable. He held the county's money. Fan looked at him with tired contempt.

"Secretary," said Tao softly, "the provincial team arrives Wednesday at nine. I propose a county self-inspection group, led by me. We put every Old Street file in order before then. Then the inspectors read clean paper."

He laid one finger on his briefcase. "Files are fragile things, Secretary. So are careers."

Everyone understood the price. Whoever hands the inspector the files decides which files he reads.

If Fan said no, Tao would tell the city that the Secretary did not trust his own county. If Fan said yes, Tao would hold every book in Yunxi by Monday night.

Fan's thumb pressed the cup until the tip turned white.

"Secretary Fan." Kang had not spoken for an hour, and the room turned toward him like plants toward a window. "Before any vote, I must read something."

He took a single sheet from his folder.

"This morning the official car of Executive Deputy Magistrate Lu stood for forty minutes behind the private office of Hongrui Real Estate. At the back door. Two officers of my commission watched it."

Lu laughed a little too loudly. "I went to urge Boss Dong to cooperate!"

"After the city opened a file on him at four this morning?" Kang asked mildly. "Through the back door?"

Lu squeezed his cup, and the cup cracked.

Hot tea ran across the polished table toward Tao's papers. Nobody reached for a napkin.

Behind Lu, Gu Min had laughed at every one of his jokes for six years. Now he quietly slid his notebook to the far side of his lap.

Tao's smile did not move, but under the table his hand opened and closed. His self-inspection group now looked like a fox offering to guard the henhouse.

Fan felt something unknot in his chest, and he made his move.

"Comrade Lu Wenzhe. From this minute you have no part in the Old Street project. Secretary Kang will seal your office records. Magistrate Tao, your offer is kind. The inspectors will decide when they come."

"Of course, Secretary," said Tao, still smiling. He added, so softly that only Fan heard, "A man sleeps better with friends. Count yours tonight."

In Seat Four, Shao Wenhua capped his pen. He had said nothing, and in that room it was the loudest thing anyone did.

* * *

Fan stayed to sign the seal order on Lu's office. His secretary hovered with the stamp, and Fan did not look up.

"Faster. If one page is missing tomorrow, I'll remember whose hands held the stamp."

The secretary's ears went gray. Then the phone buzzed with a number Fan did not know.

"Secretary Fan." The voice was young, polite, and in no hurry. "A retired senior leader fished at Qingshan Reservoir today. Do not approach him."

"Who is this? Which leader?"

The line clicked. The caller was one of Chief An's men.

Ten minutes later the province's General Office said the team would come tomorrow at nine, and would not say why.

Fan put the phone down very gently. For the first time in thirty years his own county felt like another man's house.

Which retired leader? And who had the nerve to give orders to the Secretary of Yunxi?

* * *

Chen Xuan sat on the step with a bun in one hand and his phone in the other.

*Mom: Your uncle slept nine hours and asked for more soup. Wear the thick jacket tomorrow. Did you eat vegetables? Do not lie.*

"A big one," he typed, and bit the bun, which was cabbage, so it was true.

His grandmother came out with a quilt. "Thanks, Grandma," he mumbled, and he was asleep sitting up, with crumbs on his chin.

She laid the quilt over him and told the cat to guard him. The cat, being a cat, ate the crumbs.

* * *

Dong Haisheng sat with his collar open and a cold cup of tea he kept forgetting to drink.

"Boss Dong, it's Gu Min," whispered the phone. "Executive Deputy Lu says he's never been in your office."

"He ate my crab last Thursday!"

The line died.

Before he could throw the phone, it rang again. This time his whole back went wet.

"Boss Dong." Chairman Huang's voice was soft, as always, like a man talking to a sleeping child. "The inspection has moved to tomorrow at nine. Is the corner finished?"

"Released, Chairman. The old man went home this morning."

"So. Which papers hurt most?"

"The Ankang ledgers. The clinic's book of injury slips, and every payment we made on them."

"Then they shouldn't exist tomorrow. Tonight, Boss Dong. A storage room fire is only a fire. And the debt book your man carries."

"Abacus called me from a noodle shop. He's shaking."

"Shaking men carry buckets well. Go and watch."

* * *

The night cleaner, who had been at Ankang four months and whose pay no clerk could trace to anyone, hummed a folk song down the hall and swung a mop.

Two men came down the corridor with a red plastic bucket that sloshed and smelled of kerosene.

One was the clinic's night manager. The other was a thin man with a little brass abacus dangling from his belt, clutching a black debt book to his chest.

Abacus pushed the door open with his shoulder.

On a metal stool in the middle of the room sat County Police Director Mai Zhenhua, with four city inspectors behind him and a camera light already glowing.

Mai's men had taken the ledgers at ten, through the door the night cleaner left open. Abacus had walked in on Dong's own order, carrying his debt book like a gift.

"Good evening," said Mai. "You're late. The ledgers have been waiting."

The bucket fell, and kerosene ran across the tiles.

Mai opened the first ledger and read with his finger.

"*Ankang to Hongrui: forty-one injury slips, three thousand yuan each.*" He turned a page. "*L.W., consulting fee, eight hundred thousand a quarter, six quarters.*"

A young inspector tapped his phone and whispered, "Four point eight million. That's eighty-six years of a duty officer's pay."

In the doorway, Dong Haisheng stood with his cigarette gone out between his fingers. He had come to watch, and Mai lifted his eyes to him.

"It was Boss Dong!" Abacus squealed at once, pointing with the debt book. "He said fire! I said the bucket was too heavy!"

"You wet-eyed rat," Dong hissed, and his open hand cracked across Abacus's cheek so hard that the brass abacus rang on his belt. "Two years I fed you. Your mother lives alone on Willow Lane, and those stairs are steep."

"Fed me? You paid me eight hundred yuan a night, and you never once said thank you!"

"Boss Dong. You're not under arrest tonight. The city commission wants you to stay in Yunxi. And I'll keep your passport."

Dong handed over the passport and the Singapore ticket folded behind it, slowly, like a man giving up a tooth.

In the car, Dong dialed with fingers that would not close.

"Chairman, they have the books. And the passport."

"I see."

There was a pause, and he heard something clink, like a cup set on a saucer.

"Then I'll send someone to sit with you," said Huang.

The phone slid from Dong's wet hand onto the seat, and the driver did not dare to look back.
