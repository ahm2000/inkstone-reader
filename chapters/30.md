# Chapter 30: Tomato Stakes

Early Sunday morning at the Hongshi Village vegetable plots beside the ditch, Chen Xuan wanted to pick tomatoes for his mother's soup and be back at his grandmother's table by half past nine, when the porridge would be ready.

He had Xiaoyu beside him in a borrowed jacket, a basket on his arm, and his green thermos in his hand, and he did not yet know that the plots were about to turn into a courtroom.

A dozen small gardens lay in a row along the irrigation ditch, each one fenced with bamboo and old wire, and cabbage, beans, leeks and late tomatoes stood in neat green lines. On a nail at the end of the path hung a wooden board covered in names and dates, called the Cabbage Schedule, and everyone in the village obeyed it.

In front of the board, two people were shouting.

"Your fence is thirty centimeters over my line, and I measured it with my own eyes!" Auntie Dong boomed. She was sixty-six, round and loud, with a straw hat tied under her chin and a voice that could cross a field without trying.

"Your tomato stakes cross my fence," Old Pei said.

He said it quietly, because he was seventy-two, thin, in a gray sweater, with his hands clasped behind his back, and he never needed to move. When he stared at someone, that person remembered every small lie told since childhood.

"Stakes don't cross, they lean!" Auntie Dong stamped her boot. "And don't change the subject, Pei, because you sit at the head of the ditch and get the water first every time, so my beans are dry by Thursday!"

"The water quota is divided by plot size," Old Pei said, "not by who stands closest."

"The ditch gate needs fixing, so who pays for it in next year's budget? Me? For a gate I use half as often as you?"

"If it is half as often, you pay half."

"I will not pay half of a gate that you broke!"

Around them, the other gardeners had stopped working, and nobody dared to move a step.

At the edge of the path stood Chen Xuan's grandfather, Chen Fugen, tall and calm in his old straw hat, with a fishing rod over his shoulder, as if he had come to watch a play. Beside him, his grandmother Zhou Guilan, tiny as a child in her padded coat, held a plastic bag of pickled eggs.

"Too thin," Zhou Guilan said the moment she saw her grandson, and then she looked at Xiaoyu. "You too! Too thin!"

"Grandma, you said that last week," Chen Xuan said.

"It was true last week, and it is still true!"

Xiaoyu almost smiled. Almost.

Chen Xuan watched the quarrel for another minute, and he saw that both people were serious, both were sure, and both were getting hoarse, so somebody should measure something.

"Mm, may I try?" he asked politely.

Everyone turned.

"Auntie Dong, Uncle Pei, I only want to look at the stake."

He walked to the old fence, pulled one stake out of the ground, and stood it on the path. Then he held his thermos against it. The thermos was exactly thirty centimeters tall, because years ago he had measured a desk drawer with it and never forgotten the number.

"Xiaoyu, could you hold the end?"

Xiaoyu took the end of the stake and kept it straight.

"Both measures start at the old stone marker," Chen Xuan said, counting along the ground. "From the marker to the old line, where the stakes always stood, it is three thermoses. From the marker to the fence post now, it is three thermoses and a thumb. So the stakes stayed where they were, and the post has leaned out about a thumb since the rain. The fence moved, not the stake."

Silence.

"Whose thumb?" Auntie Dong demanded.

"Yes," said Old Pei, and for the first time his eyes moved. "Whose thumb?"

"Hmm, maybe a neutral thumb." Chen Xuan looked at Xiaoyu. "Could you?"

"A thirteen-year-old's thumb!" Auntie Dong shouted, thrilled. "It is the fairest thumb in the whole village!"

Xiaoyu hesitated, then held out her thumb along the ground, and it fit. Auntie Dong threw up her hands as if a verdict had been announced.

"So the fence goes back one thumb and the stake stays," Chen Xuan said, "and nobody gives up anything. For the water, maybe it could go by days? Odd days for one side, even days for the other, and the sides swap on the first of each month, so nobody sits at the head of the ditch all week."

"Odd and even," Auntie Dong said slowly. "With the thirty-first of the month alternating."

"And the cost of the gate split by the days of use," Old Pei said.

"Fair," said Auntie Dong.

"Fair," said Old Pei.

"See," Auntie Dong said to no one, puffing up a little, "this is why my grandson should marry a clever girl, because fairness runs in families that think!" Old Pei said nothing, but something in his face suggested that this logic had holes.

They shook hands, and everyone in the garden let out a breath.

At the edge of the plots, a man in a plain polo shirt and clean sneakers had been standing very still, holding a paper bag of persimmons. He looked about forty, and he looked as if he had walked here on tiptoe.

"Fang Jun!" Auntie Dong swung around. "You work at the Xinhe District Government Office, you came to eat my cabbage, and you cannot even return a phone call?!"

The man flinched as if she had slapped the air in front of him.

"Auntie Dong, I..."

Then he heard it all again in his head: odd days, even days, split the gate by use. His face changed. He took a small notebook from his pocket, wrote three quick words, stuffed it away, and said very fast, "I have a meeting. Sorry. Enjoy the cabbage."

He hurried down the lane, still holding the persimmons.

"A meeting on Sunday?" Chen Fugen said. "Do government clerks work Sundays now?"

"Poor boy," said Zhou Guilan. "Too thin."

Auntie Dong was already back at her beans. "Chen Xuan, you have a gift, and next month you will measure the whole village!"

"I'll stick to tomatoes," Chen Xuan said happily, and he filled his basket with the reddest ones.

Old Pei had not moved, and he was looking at Xiaoyu.

He looked for a long time, the way he looked at everyone, and Xiaoyu felt her shoulders go tight and stared at the ground.

"Child," Old Pei said, in the quiet voice that made people forget to breathe. "Somebody has lied about you."

Xiaoyu froze.

"A long time, I think. I can see it in how you walk. You walk like a person who is still being accused."

Nobody laughed, and even Auntie Dong stopped.

"It was never yours," Old Pei said. "That story. Put it down here. Nobody in this village will pick it up."

Xiaoyu did not answer, but her hands were not tight anymore. She looked at Chen Xuan, and he gave her one small nod, the nod that meant he had heard and nothing was required.

"Pei, you scare the child," Auntie Dong said gruffly, wiping her nose with her sleeve.

"I am telling her she can stop looking behind her," said Old Pei.

Xiaoyu took a tomato out of the basket and held it in both hands. It was still warm from the sun.

Then she looked past the garden, down the lane to the village road, and her breath stopped.

Two blocks away, a black sedan sat by the old stone well with its engine running, and in the driver's seat a man in a gray jacket was reading a newspaper.

It was upside down.

"Brother Xuan," Xiaoyu said quietly. "That man's newspaper."

Chen Xuan looked and considered it carefully.

"Mm. Maybe he's learning to read it from the other side."

The newspaper stayed upside down all morning.
