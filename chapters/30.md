# Chapter 30: Tomato Stakes

Dongqiao Town, Hongshi Village, the vegetable plots beside the ditch. Sunday, 7:30 a.m.

Chen Xuan wanted to pick tomatoes for his mother's soup and be back at his grandmother's table by nine-thirty, when the porridge would be ready.

He had Xiaoyu beside him in a borrowed jacket, a basket on his arm, and his green thermos in his hand. He did not yet know that the plots were about to turn into a courtroom.

A dozen small gardens lay in a row along the irrigation ditch, each one fenced with bamboo and old wire. Cabbage, beans, leeks, and late tomatoes stood in neat green lines. On a nail at the end of the path hung a wooden board covered in names and dates. It was the Cabbage Rota, and everyone in the village obeyed it.

In front of the board, two people were shouting.

"Your fence is thirty centimeters over my line!" Auntie Dong boomed. She was sixty-six, round and loud, with a straw hat tied under her chin and a voice that could cross a field without trying. "Thirty. I measured it with my own eyes!"

"Your tomato stakes cross my fence," Old Pei said.

He said it quietly. He was seventy-two, thin, with a gray sweater and his hands clasped behind his back. He did not move at all. When he stared at someone, that person remembered every small lie he had told since childhood.

"Stakes don't cross! Stakes lean!" Auntie Dong stamped her boot. "And don't change the subject, Pei! You sit at the head of the ditch, so you get the water first, every time, and my beans are dry by Thursday!"

"The water quota is divided by plot size," Old Pei said. "Not by who stands closest."

"The ditch gate needs fixing! Who pays for it in next year's budget? Me? For a gate I use half as often as you?"

"If it is half as often, you pay half."

"I will not pay half of a gate that you broke!"

Around them, other gardeners had stopped working. Nobody moved. At the edge of the path, Chen Xuan's grandfather Chen Fugen stood in his old straw hat, tall and calm, with a fishing rod over his shoulder, as if he had come for the show. Beside him, his grandmother Zhou Guilan, tiny as a child in her padded coat, was holding a plastic bag of pickled eggs.

"Too thin," Zhou Guilan said the moment she saw her grandson. Then she looked at Xiaoyu. "You too! Too thin!"

"Grandma, you said that last week," Chen Xuan said.

"It was true last week. It is still true!"

Xiaoyu almost smiled. Almost.

Chen Xuan watched the quarrel for another minute. Both people were serious, both were sure, and both were getting hoarse. Somebody, he thought, should measure something.

"Mm. May I try?" he asked politely.

Everyone turned.

"Auntie Dong. Uncle Pei. I just want to look at the stake."

He walked to the old fence, pulled one stake out of the ground, and stood it on the path. Then he picked up his thermos and held it against the stake. The thermos was exactly thirty centimeters tall. He had known that for years, ever since he had once measured a desk drawer with it.

"Xiaoyu, could you hold the end?"

Xiaoyu took the stake and kept it straight.

"Both measures start at the old stone marker," Chen Xuan said, counting along the ground. "From the marker to the old line, where the stakes always stood: three thermoses. From the marker to the fence post now: three thermoses and a thumb. So the stakes stayed where they were. The post leans out about a thumb since the rain. The fence moved, not the stake."

Silence.

"Whose thumb?" Auntie Dong demanded.

"Yes," said Old Pei, and for the first time his eyes moved. "Whose thumb?"

"Hmm. Maybe a neutral thumb." Chen Xuan looked at Xiaoyu. "Could you?"

"A thirteen-year-old's thumb!" Auntie Dong shouted, thrilled. "The fairest thumb in the whole village!"

Xiaoyu hesitated, then held out her thumb along the ground. It fit. Auntie Dong threw up her hands as if a verdict had been announced.

"So the fence goes back one thumb, and the stake stays," Chen Xuan said. "Nobody gives up anything. And for the water, maybe it could go by days? Odd days for one side, even days for the other, and the sides swap each month. Then nobody sits at the head of the ditch all week."

"Odd and even," Auntie Dong said slowly. "With the thirty-first of the month alternating."

"And the cost of the gate split by the days of use," Old Pei said.

"Fair," said Auntie Dong.

"Fair," said Old Pei.

They shook hands. Everyone in the garden let out a breath.

At the edge of the plots, a man in a plain polo shirt and clean sneakers had been standing very still with a paper bag of persimmons in his hands. He looked about forty, and he looked as if he had walked here on tiptoe.

"Fang Jun!" Auntie Dong swung around. "Xinhe District Government Office! You came to eat my cabbage, but you can't return a phone call?!"

The man flinched as if she had slapped the air in front of him.

"Auntie Dong, I..."

Then he heard it all again in his head: odd days, even days, split the gate by use. His face changed. He took a small notebook from his pocket, wrote three quick words, stuffed it away, and said very fast, "I have a meeting. Sorry. Enjoy the cabbage."

He hurried down the lane, still holding the persimmons.

"A meeting on Sunday?" Chen Fugen said. "Government clerks work Sundays now?"

"Poor boy," said Zhou Guilan. "Too thin."

Auntie Dong was already back at her beans. "Chen Xuan! You have a gift. Next month you will measure the whole village!"

"I'll stick to tomatoes," Chen Xuan said happily. He filled his basket with the reddest ones.

Old Pei had not moved. He was looking at Xiaoyu.

He looked for a long time, the way he looked at everyone. Xiaoyu felt her shoulders go tight, and she stared at the ground.

"Child," Old Pei said, in the quiet voice that made people forget to breathe. "Somebody has lied about you."

Xiaoyu froze.

"A long time, I think. I can see it in how you walk. You walk like a person who is still being accused."

Nobody laughed. Even Auntie Dong stopped.

"It was never yours," Old Pei said. "That story. Put it down here. Nobody in this village will pick it up."

Xiaoyu did not answer. Her hands, though, were not tight anymore. She looked at Chen Xuan, and he gave her one small nod, the nod that meant he had heard and nothing was required.

"Pei, you scare the child!" Auntie Dong said gruffly, wiping her nose with her sleeve.

"I'm telling her she can stop looking behind her," said Old Pei.

Xiaoyu took a tomato out of the basket and held it in both hands. It was still warm from the sun.

Then she looked past the garden, down the lane to the village road.

Two blocks away, a black sedan sat idling by the old stone well. The engine was running. In the driver's seat, a man in a gray jacket was reading a newspaper.

It was upside down.

"Brother Xuan," Xiaoyu said quietly. "That man's newspaper."

Chen Xuan looked. He considered it carefully.

"Mm. Maybe he's learning to read it from the other side."

The newspaper stayed upside down all morning.
