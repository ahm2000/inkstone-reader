# Chapter 11: The Hostage Call

By early afternoon, the whole apartment in Fuxing New Village was tense and loud with worry.

"Mom, if you stir that pot any harder, it will file a complaint," Chen Yuan sighed from the kitchen door.

"Do not talk to me about complaints!" Liu Meifang waved her ladle in the air. "Your uncle has sat in a cold cell for a whole day, and my hands will not stay still!"

Behind a bedroom door, Chen Hao groaned every few minutes over a practice math exam.

"Does anyone here remember how to solve a quadratic?" he called out.

"Eat something first!" his mother shouted back.

In the living room, Second Aunt sat on the edge of the sofa and held her phone in both hands, never taking her eyes off the dark screen.

Chen Xuan sat at the dining table, laid the photo of the holding notice beside Old Yu's signed page, and wrote the times down in neat rows.

Suddenly, the phone rang!

Second Aunt jumped as if it had burned her fingers.

"It is him," she whispered, her face turning pale. "Boss Dong."

"Do not answer that crook!" Chen Jianguo shouted.

"Put it on speaker, Second Aunt," Chen Xuan said calmly. "Let's hear what he wants."

She tapped the green button with a shaking finger, and a smooth, oily voice filled the room.

"Sister Wu? This is Boss Dong. Do not be frightened, I am only calling to help! Your husband is in a hard spot, you know. Ten days, maybe fifteen, is no small thing, because it leaves a police record, and your daughter will pay for it when she looks for a job after university! Lucky for you, I have friends. Sign the buyout contract today, and I will make one call, and Old Chen will sit at your dinner table tonight! Four hundred and fifty-six thousand yuan, cash within a week. Think of your family, Sister Wu!"

Second Aunt shook so hard that she almost dropped the phone.

This was a trade, and Dong only called it a favor.

What he really sold was her husband's freedom, and the price was less than half of what the shop was worth. Everyone in the room heard it for exactly what it was.

"Four hundred and fifty-six thousand is still money," Second Aunt whispered, tears on her lashes. "Xuan, maybe I should sign, so your uncle can come home tonight."

"Second Aunt." Chen Xuan stood up, walked over and pressed the red button.

Silence.

"That shop is worth nearly one million yuan," he said steadily, looking into her eyes, "and the moment you sign, they will say Second Uncle agreed freely, and after that nobody can undo it, not a lawyer and not a judge. Please trust me for one more day."

"Xuan, I want to trust you, I really do," she said. "But how can you promise he will be home in one day when you are only an office clerk?"

"A logistics clerk, yes, a supervisor really." Chen Xuan gave her a warm smile. "Checking messy forms is my job. Their paper has an eighteen-hour gap in it, their injury note came from a private clinic, and we hold Old Yu's signed statement. They are the ones who should be nervous."

Second Aunt looked up at him, and her breathing slowly settled.

Nobody spoke, and then Chen Jianguo cleared his throat and lifted one finger. Out of lifelong habit, the whole family turned to hear his wisdom.

"As the old proverb says, the fisherman who holds the net is not afraid of a thrashing carp!"

"Dad," Chen Yuan groaned, "that is not an old proverb."

"It is a new one," Chen Jianguo said proudly. "I just made it up!"

"Dad is right about one thing," Chen Hao called through his door. "Brother never says maybe."

Second Aunt let out a wet little laugh that turned into a sob, but it was a real laugh all the same.

Liu Meifang hurried over and held her sister-in-law tightly by the shoulders.

A few minutes later, beside the river.

In a black Audi, Dong Haisheng stared at his dead phone with his smooth face growing dark.

Hung up on, by a hardware seller's wife!

"That nephew will regret this," he muttered.

Worse, right before the click, he had heard a calm young man's voice in the background.

Eleven families had already signed for him, so why would a nobody and his office-clerk nephew refuse?

A clerk?

Was somebody coaching them? Did that clerk have a backer? Why did the nephew sound so sure?

Before he could curse, the phone buzzed again, and the name on the screen made his stomach sink: Chairman Huang.

He sat up straight, even alone in his own car. "Chairman Huang! Good afternoon!"

"Boss Dong." The voice was mild and almost sleepy, yet every word was cold. "I dislike asking twice. Is the corner lot cleared?"

"Monday, Chairman! Monday morning, I swear it!" Dong's palm turned slick on the steering wheel. "The shopkeeper is locked up, and his wife is breaking down, and my friend at the police bureau says she will sign by Monday!"

"I have no interest in your small-town friends," Chairman Huang said softly. "Your bank is watching your loan, and I know what happens to a developer who defaults. Monday, Boss Dong. Not Tuesday."

Click.

Dong sat frozen and looked across the gray river at the dark rooftops of Old Street. After twenty years of deals, he felt real fear for the first time, because he had borrowed far more than he could survive losing.

---

That night, in the County Magistrate's study, Tao Shu did not have a cold.

Everyone in the county office believed he did, but he sat in house slippers with a mug of green tea at his elbow, looking perfectly healthy. Behind his thin glasses, his eyes were wide awake.

To put it nicely, Tao Shu was the number-two official in Yunxi, and to put it bluntly, Fan Weiguo's word was final and his was not.

For eight years he had waited for the Party Secretary to slip, and tonight he could smell a mistake in the air, the way an old farmer smells coming rain.

But he had not written the secret letter to the provincial capital.

The reason was simple: someone had sent an anonymous tip straight to the Governor's office, and people up in the province were already asking questions.

Tao Shu would have loved to see Fan Weiguo fall, of course, yet he knew better than to write such a letter himself, because in officialdom the one who writes it is usually the first to be blamed.

So who had written it?

One of the frightened shop owners? Unlikely. A county reporter? None of them had the nerve. Somebody in Qingyan City who knew far too much about the relocation fund?

His wife had once asked why he never simply took what was his.

"Patience is for people who cannot afford mistakes," he had told her. "I cannot afford them either, so I just hide it better."

Tao Shu opened his desk drawer and took out a thin folder with one word on the cover, written in his own fountain-pen hand: Fan.

He did not open it, and only laid his palm flat across the cover.

Not yet.

But who had sent that letter, and what else did that person know?
