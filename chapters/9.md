# Chapter 9: The Eighteen Hours

Outside the station, Chen Jianguo snatched the sheet out of the slot before it finished sliding.

"Ten days! They gave my brother ten days for a crime he did not do!"

"Dad, let me read it first. Then you can read it loudly."

Chen Jianguo huffed and handed it over.

Outside, Chen Xuan read the notice top to bottom, one thumb under each line.

"Dad, what time did the police take Second Uncle?"

"Two o'clock yesterday. Five past, Old Yu says."

"And this box says *Time of Decision: Saturday, 7:50 a.m.*"

"So?" said Chen Jianguo.

"So the police must write down the moment they decide to hold a man. Before that moment, a locked door is only a locked door."

"Almost eighteen hours after the handcuffs," Chen Xuan said quietly. "And the injury proof is from Ankang Private Clinic, stamped ten to two. A real fight case gets a police doctor, not a street clinic."

"Ten to two," Chen Jianguo repeated, three times, like a password. "A private clinic writes whatever somebody pays it to write. Xuan, they are the ones who broke the law!"

"One more thing." Chen Xuan lowered his voice. "If they wanted to hide it, they'd have written Friday afternoon. Everybody does. Someone in there wrote the real time on purpose."

Behind the lobby glass, Wei Kun had not moved for a minute.

He had heard nothing, and yet he felt as if a stranger had set a quiet hand on his shoulder.

"Then what do we do?" said Chen Jianguo.

Chen Xuan did not answer.

He stood under the old locust tree with the notice in his hand, and for the first time since the bus, nothing lazy was left in his face.

"Wait here, Dad. I need to make a call."

He walked ten steps away and took out his phone.

Last night he had used this number for the first time to give an order, and the order was to watch and touch nothing.

This one would be bigger.

He photographed the notice and typed.

*Chief An. Good work last night. Finish it. Eighteen hours is not a mistake. Lay out the notice, the clinic and the money by tonight. Keep the old tailor safe.*

The answer came at once: *Yes, Boss.*

He bought two fried dough sticks from the cart, handed one to his father, and bit into the other.

* * *

Inside the station, Zhong Hai sat alone at the end of a steel table in the canteen with a lunch box he was not eating.

He was thirty-eight, Wei Kun's desk neighbor, and a good man in every way but one: he was afraid.

His six-year-old son Tiantian needed a heart operation. The hospital wanted 80,000 yuan by Monday, more than a year of his pay, and he had 9,000.

His phone buzzed.

*Lei Bo: Director Cheng has one question. What time did Wei write on the Chen notice? A 60,000 loan waits for a straight answer. No interest.*

He typed *7:50 a.m.* and pressed send before the rest of him could stop it.

The answer came in four seconds: a thumbs-up, and *Good brother.*

Zhong Hai pressed his sleeve over his mouth and sat very still, like a man who swallowed something that won't go down.

Down in the holding corridor, Cheng Biao stopped walking, turned around, and slapped Lei Bo across the face.

"Seven-fifty! A true hour, on a paper you stamped without reading one word!"

"My fault, Director!" Lei Bo cried, holding his cheek, and out of old habit he began to laugh one second later.

Lei Bo's laugh died in his throat, and a red hand rose slowly on his cheek.

Cheng Biao went on alone to the bars.

"Old Chen. Your brother and nephew came to the lobby. The clerk asked for paper. Very cute. Only you can open this door. Sign."

He leaned closer.

"Your daughter is in Wuhan, isn't she? Room 412, I hear. A father's police record can reach a university in one letter. And a dormitory corridor is so dark after midnight. Wuhan is a big city, Old Chen. Girls walk home alone."

He tapped the toothpick once against the bars, slowly, like a clock.

Chen Mingde's hands began to tremble between his knees.

His daughter.

For one second the cold of the concrete went all the way into his chest, and he forgot to breathe.

Then he thought of one thing: They came.

His brother and his nephew had sat on a bench for two hours, for him.

The trembling slowed, and his mouth moved into a small smile.

"My nephew came? Then I'll keep waiting, Director Cheng. My brother always says, when the fish bites, stay calm and wait."

Cheng Biao stared.

He had broken eleven men in this corridor, and none of them had ever smiled.

In the hall he took out his phone and spoke low.

"Hu. The old tailor saw too much on Friday. This afternoon, on the river road, a van. Make it look like traffic. Visit him tonight anyway."

Then he beckoned Lei Bo, who came with a hand still pressed to his cheek.

"Wei Kun wrote the true hour, and he knows who told him to write the false one. Bring him a cup of tea with the packet from Ankang. All of it. And Lei Bo, if you spill it, you'll drink the next one."

"To make him sleep, Director?"

"To make him whatever it makes him. A dead witness can't sign anything."

Lei Bo carried a paper cup of jasmine tea toward the duty window in both hands, like a man carrying a baby bird.

His mouth was dry, and he kept hearing two words: dead witness.

At the records-room door, a stooped man in sleeve covers stepped out with a stack of files and bumped Lei Bo's wrist.

The cup flew, tea slapped across the tiles, and the paper cup split.

"Aiya! My old eyes!" cried Old Ding, and dropped to his knees with a rag.

The rag mopped the puddle, and a small glass tube slid into his sleeve cover.

On the wet floor the tea had left a gray foam.

Lei Bo stared at it, and his knees went soft.

A prank powder does not foam.

"Forgive an old clerk, Officer Lei." Old Ding poured plain tea from his own flask into a fresh cup. "Tell Wei it comes from you, and you get the credit."

"From me?"

"Who else carried it?"

It was the cruelest question Lei Bo had ever heard. And it was true. The one who carries the cup gets the blame.

He took the plain tea to the window with a hand that would not keep still.

"Thank you, Lei Bo," said Wei Kun, puzzled, and drank. "It is good tea."

Lei Bo leaned on a wall and muttered, "The old fool tripped on his own feet. What luck."

In the records room, Old Ding sealed the tube and typed: *Poison in cup for Wei. Ordered by Cheng, carried by Lei Bo, powder from Ankang. Cup saved. Wei unharmed.*

* * *

Behind the bread factory, inside a windowless van painted like a flower-delivery truck, Captain Tang was halfway through a cold dumpling when his screen flashed.

On the wall map, two hundred and fourteen green dots turned amber at once.

"Captain!" Deputy Pei dropped his tea. "A message from the Boss, through Chief An. It is an order."

The van went silent.

Since last night they had recorded everything and touched nothing, waiting for the next word, and it had come from a man eating a fried dough stick.

"Read it exactly."

"Finish it. Lay out the notice, the clinic and the money by tonight. Keep the old tailor safe."

"Then we move," said Captain Tang. "All of us, by the book, with no questions."

A bread truck rolled out first, then a laundry van, then three plain gray cars with city plates.

A line scrolled across the screen from the records clerk: *Cheng ordered Hu to put a van on the old tailor this afternoon, river road. Also tea for Wei, stopped. Recorded.*

"A murder order," Deputy Pei said quietly. "Do we stop it?"

Captain Tang tapped the river road on the map, in the voice of a man ordering lunch.

"Bread truck at the arch by four. The doctor from Everbright Family Clinic on the corner with her bag open. Our man on the traffic team takes the arch beat."

"And if the van comes early?"

"Then the bread gets there early."

Chief An's voice came over the speaker, dry and a little amused.

"Wake everyone," it said. "Last night he said 'touch nothing.' Now he has said 'finish it.'"

What happens to a county when a man who rarely gives orders says that word?
