# Chapter 23: The Girl at the Noodle Shop

Yunxi County, Old Street. Friday, 7:30 p.m.

The firecrackers were finished, the eggs were eaten, and Mingde Hardware was still crowded with neighbors telling each other they had "never doubted it for one second."

Chen Xuan had been at the shop since 6:40, and he'd already been hugged by four people and fed by six!

"Come on, Xuan," Chen Jianguo said, brushing eggshell off his sleeve. "Your mother's soup won't be ready until eight-thirty. And as the old saying goes, an empty stomach climbs six floors twice as slowly."

"Is that an old saying?" asked Chen Hao. He had a math book open in one hand and one earbud in his ear, and he hadn't looked up all evening.

"It is now," said Chen Jianguo.

So the three of them crossed the lane to Old Beef Noodles, the little six-table shop under the red awning. Halfway there, Chen Xuan stopped.

A girl stood at the edge of the awning, just outside the circle of yellow light. She looked about thirteen and small for it, wearing a man's gray work jacket so big that the sleeves were rolled up. Her hair had been cut short and crooked, as if somebody had done it in a hurry. A school backpack hung from one shoulder, and a plastic bag was twisted tight around her fist.

She wasn't looking at the noodle shop. She was watching the street. Every passerby got exactly one look, and then she moved on to the next one, very steady, very serious. It was a look far too tired for a girl her age!

A woman walking past had already clicked her tongue at her, the particular sound adults make at children they've decided are trouble before they've said a word, and crossed to the other side of the lane.

"Hmm?" said Chen Xuan.

"Is she lost?" Chen Hao finally pulled out his earbud.

"Maybe." Chen Xuan walked over and stopped at a polite distance. "Good evening. Are you waiting for someone?"

"My dad," the girl said at once.

"Oh. Is he inside the shop?"

"He's coming. He's late from work."

She said it fast and flat, like a sentence she'd practiced many times. And right on cue, her stomach growled so loudly that Chen Jianguo heard it from three steps away!

"I'm not hungry," said the girl.

"Of course not," Chen Xuan said, nodding seriously. "We're about to eat, though. We'll be at the window table. If your dad's late, you're welcome to wait inside where it's warm."

He didn't ask again. He walked in, and his father and brother followed.

"Brother," Chen Hao whispered. "She's lying."

"Mm."

"So why didn't you say so?"

"If I say it, she needs a bigger lie tomorrow," Chen Xuan said mildly. "If I don't, she can tell the truth when she's ready."

Chen Hao opened his mouth, closed it, and put the earbud in his pocket for good!

"Young Chen!" The cook, Gong Yi, a lean man in a flour-dusted apron, waved a ladle from behind the counter. The whole street called him Brother Gong. "I heard your uncle's firecrackers all the way from my stove! Three bowls?"

"Three, please. And a fourth bowl for the girl outside, whenever she's ready. Extra egg."

The first three bowls landed on the table in under a minute, set down with both hands, each with two fried eggs nobody had ordered.

"Fast service!" Chen Jianguo said happily.

Ten minutes later, the door opened a crack. The girl stood there with her bag.

"I can wash dishes," she said to the floor. "For a bowl."

"The kitchen's that way," said Chen Xuan. "But I already ordered your bowl, so it's paid for. Would you wash after you eat? Then your stomach and your hands will both be warm."

She thought about it for a long moment. "After?"

"After."

"...Okay."

She ate fast, then slowly, as if she'd remembered her manners halfway through. When the bowl was empty, she carried it to the sink and washed it herself. Then she washed the three bowls from the window table, then every pair of chopsticks in the box!

"Brother Gong," Chen Jianguo called, "this girl washes better than your dishwasher!"

"I don't have a dishwasher."

"That explains it!"

Chen Xuan waited until she dried her hands. "What's your name?"

"Tang Xiaoyu."

"Do you live near here, Xiaoyu?"

"At my aunt's." Her eyes slid away. "It's close."

He nodded and let that one go too. "And your dad's name? Old Street is small. Somebody might know him."

"Tang Dawei," she said, so quickly it seemed to jump out of her. "He works at the yard on Dongqiao Road."

"Which yard?"

She hesitated. "The one that knocks buildings down. Tiancheng."

"Hmm," said Chen Xuan softly. Dongqiao was his grandparents' town. He'd ridden down Dongqiao Road a hundred times.

Behind the counter, Gong Yi's ladle stopped over the pot.

Gong Yi was Number Twenty-three of the Detail, the hidden team of protectors who work for Chen Xuan and act only when he gives the signal. The name Tiancheng meant something to him. It was the demolition company owned by the brother-in-law of Cheng Biao, the police deputy director who had jailed the shop owners, the one with the huge Old Street contract. Last night, as he did for any hungry kid who hung around his door, he'd handed this girl two steamed buns. She'd said she would wash dishes for them, and he'd said nobody washes dishes for two buns. Tonight she'd come back to prove she meant it. Everything in him wanted to sit her down and call every office in town before the soup cooled! But the Boss sat six feet away, ordering noodles like any customer, and an ordinary noodle cook doesn't make calls about a child whose father is "late." If anyone was going to start this, it had to be the Boss. So Gong Yi turned his back, typed one line to Chief An under the counter, and cracked another egg into the pan, because that was the only thing he was allowed to do.

At the window table, Chen Xuan folded his napkin. Was a father really this "late"? Why had she said the name so fast? He didn't know yet. But he knew what a normal person does when a child is alone at night.

"Xiaoyu, let's ask the police to help find your dad. That's what they're for."

The girl went stiff. "I didn't take anything."

"Nobody said you did."

"They always say that first."

Chen Xuan kept his voice very ordinary. "I'll walk in with you and sit beside you the whole time. If it's awful, we'll walk right out. Deal?"

A long pause. "Not in a cell."

"There's no cell. There's a steel bench, and it isn't even comfortable."

The corner of her mouth moved. It wasn't a smile yet, but it was close.

Yunxi County, Old Street Police Station. Friday, 8:50 p.m.

The lobby was nearly empty. By pure habit, Chen Xuan pulled a number from the ticket machine and looked at it. Number one. In all his visits to this lobby, he had never been number one before!

Behind the glass sat Instructor Qiu Guoqiang, who had been running the station since the director was suspended. He had gray at his temples, reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, and a chipped tea mug in his hand.

"Mr. Chen!" Instructor Qiu came out through the side door at once. "I heard your uncle's shutters went up today. I'm very glad."

Chen Xuan explained in a few plain sentences. Instructor Qiu listened, then bent down so his eyes were level with Xiaoyu's, and said gently, "Nobody here thinks you did anything wrong, little one."

She looked at him for a long second and nodded.

He searched the missing-persons list on his screen and found no child and no Tang Dawei. Dongqiao's station was closed for the night, he said, but he'd call them first thing Monday. The station had a cot in the rest room, and Auxiliary Officer Ma, a patient woman on the night shift, would sit with her.

"She can sleep safely here tonight," said Instructor Qiu. He rubbed his tired face and looked truly sorry. "But a child with no guardian isn't police business after that. It belongs to Civil Affairs. That's the county office that looks after children who have no guardian. Take her there. It opens Monday."

Chen Xuan looked at the calendar taped to the glass.

Friday night. Then Saturday. Then Sunday.

And beside him, Xiaoyu's eyes had already turned to the front door, counting the steps.
