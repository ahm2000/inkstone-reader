# Chapter 102: General Qin Zhengguo's Inspection

By four in the afternoon, the bare concrete briefing room on the second floor of Southern Theater Command smelled of cheap tobacco and damp sea air. A single ceiling fan pushed thick ribbons of gray cigarette smoke across the white walls.

Ten younger officers sat along both sides of the long table. None was over forty-five. Several wore fresh rank patches stitched onto their dark green uniforms before dawn. None of them touched the white porcelain teacups in front of them. They sat straight-backed, eyes forward, waiting.

The heavy door swung open without a knock. General Qin Zhengguo walked in alone and shut it with his heel. He carried no speech and brought no secretary, only a thick brown cardboard folder tied with rough cotton string.

"Attention!" Colonel Lin Jianjun barked, springing up with the other nine officers.

"Sit down, sit down," Qin Zhengguo said, waving a weathered hand as he reached the head of the table. He dropped the folder onto the surface with a dull thud. "Save the parade drill for the courtyard. Today we talk like soldiers keeping the southern coast alive."

The ten officers sat back down, though none relaxed.

Qin Zhengguo pulled a rumpled pack of unfiltered cigarettes from his pocket, lit one with a brass lighter, and blew a steady stream of smoke toward the ceiling. He tossed the pack down the center of the table.

"If you smoke, light one," Qin Zhengguo said. "If you do not, open your ears wider. What we are about to go through will turn your stomach."

Lieutenant Colonel Gu Haibo, a broad-shouldered logistics officer from Zhanjiang, took a cigarette and lit it with steady fingers. Two more officers followed his lead.

Qin Zhengguo untied the cotton string and spread four thick sheets of printed tables across the wood.

"Forty-eight hours ago, military discipline teams arrested Senior Colonel Ma Zhenyu and nineteen officers from his coastal supply ring," Qin Zhengguo said, his voice gravelly. "Some of you served under them. Some of you were passed over for promotion three years running because you refused to sign their dirty fuel receipts. That is why you are sitting here today."

He tapped the top sheet with two nicotine-stained fingers.

"Do not think Ma Zhenyu was just a greedy quartermaster skimming food money from the mess hall. Over six years, Ma Zhenyu and his ring took 85 million yuan in overseas kickbacks and hidden bank deposits. Do you know what foreign buyers pay 85 million yuan for?"

Silence settled over the room, heavier than the smoke.

"Not spoiled rice or cheap boots," Qin Zhengguo said coldly. "They paid for our coastal radar repair schedules. They paid for exact reserve fuel counts inside our southern underground tanks. They paid for patrol boat refueling timetables along three sea lanes."

Colonel Lin Jianjun's jaw clenched hard. "General, that is not corruption. That is treason."

"Of course it is treason," Qin Zhengguo snapped, planting both palms flat on the table. "Tell me, Lin Jianjun. You ran fuel barges in Hainan for eight years. What happens if a foreign carrier group pushes into our southern waters at night, knowing which coastal radar station is offline for parts and which port fuel tank is half-empty on paper?"

"We lose the first two hours before a ship clears harbor," Lin Jianjun answered grimly. "In a modern sea fight, losing the first two hours means losing half the fleet."

"Exactly," Qin Zhengguo said. He turned his sharp gaze to Major Tang Yumei, a lean supply auditor pulled straight from an island warehouse the night before. "Tang Yumei. You worked on southern port sheets last autumn. Tell this table how Ma Zhenyu hid missing fuel and spare radar parts for six years without triggering a theater audit."

Tang Yumei spoke without hesitation. "They broke every large repair order into dozens of small invoices under 100k yuan, General. Anything under 100k yuan only needed a deputy supply chief's stamp instead of a full command review. Then they paired the fake bills with private shipping firms in Hong Kong. Those ships carried our spare parts out and brought back empty crates stamped as inspected."

An angry murmur ran down the table. Major Zhou Weiqiang, a thirty-eight-year-old port engineer, clenched his fists. "No wonder our patrol crews waited four months for engine valves last winter while the warehouse ledger claimed we had two hundred in storage."

"Two hundred on paper, and zero on the shelf," Qin Zhengguo said, tapping gray ash into a tin ashtray. "While our sailors patched thirty-year-old pumps with wire in heavy swells, Ma Zhenyu's wife was shopping for a lakefront house in Zurich."

He paused, letting the bitter weight sink into every officer at the table. Smoke curled slowly between his fingers.

"Let me tell you all something plainly," Qin Zhengguo said, his voice dropping low. "Two weeks ago, this country came closer to losing its southern coastal defense secrets than at any time in thirty years. Not because an enemy warship broke our line, and not because our soldiers were afraid to fight. Because men wearing your exact green uniform traded our coastal keys for foreign bank accounts."

Lieutenant Colonel Gu Haibo exhaled smoke, his eyes fierce. "General, give us seventy-two hours. We will strip every fuel depot, spare-parts warehouse, and coastal radar log from Guangdong to Hainan down to the bare floor. If one bolt does not match the ledger, it goes straight to your desk."

"You have seventy-two hours to start, and the rest of your careers to keep it clean," Qin Zhengguo replied. He looked down the row of ten faces, one by one.

"Every person here was checked three times before your transfer orders were signed last night. Not one of you took a single gift carton of cigarettes from a private contractor. Not one of you signed a padded bill for a quiet life. Some of you lived in cramped base apartments for ten years while Ma Zhenyu's friends drove luxury cars off base."

Qin Zhengguo crushed his cigarette butt into the ashtray and stood up.

"I will not promise you easy posts or loud praise," he said. "Your new jobs will be hard and watched closely from Beijing. People with deep pockets will try to buy your signature again in two years, or five years, when they think the storm has passed. Remember this room when they knock. Remember how close we came to handing our coastline away."

His eyes turned cold as steel. "If any one of you softens, takes a red envelope, or looks past a crooked receipt, do not wait for discipline teams from the capital. I will march into your office myself, rip those fresh patches off your shoulders, and put you before a military court."

All ten officers rose sharply, chairs scraping loudly across the concrete.

"Understood, General!" ten voices shouted as one.

Qin Zhengguo returned a crisp, heavy salute. "Dismissed. Get to your depots before nightfall."

When the door closed behind the last officer, Qin Zhengguo stood alone in the smoky room, looking down at the coastal maps. Outside, the southern wind rattled the glass, steady and clean.
