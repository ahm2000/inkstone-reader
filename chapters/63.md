# Chapter 63: A Stranger at Hengxin

The black executive sedan glided smoothly off the expressway ramp and merged onto Qingyan City's riverside boulevard.

In the leather-lined rear cabin, Li Weiming sat with one ankle resting neatly over the other, an encrypted slate balanced on his knee. The glass partition separating him from the hired driver was raised and opaque. On his passport issued in Geneva, his name read Marcus Vance. On his domestic travel documents, it read Li Weiming. Both identities were spotless, backed by clean tax filings, a Zurich restructuring partnership, and an invitation to next week's provincial investment forum.

Neither document mentioned the Northern Ledger.

Li Weiming tapped the slate's screen, expanding a crimson timeline that stretched across the past fourteen days. Three major regional pillars sat highlighted in dead gray.

First, Huaxin Bank's Lanshui desk, whose credit officers had quietly laundered sovereign maritime loans for eleven years. Frozen, audited, and dismantled in a single afternoon.

Second, Huang Boren's Banyan Club, the private hospitality clearinghouse where provincial officials met offshore brokers behind soundproof mahogany doors. Raided, sealed, and stripped bare down to the floorboards.

Third, Zhou Guodong's Shenghua Maritime, the coastal freight carrier that moved sensitive port manifests and gray cargo under clean customs seals. Seized before a single tanker could reach international waters.

Li Weiming pinched the screen to zoom in on the timestamps.

"Twelve hours before Huaxin Bank's desk collapsed, their branch manager tried to squeeze a small warehouse loan," Li Weiming murmured to himself, his Mandarin smooth and precise, carrying only the faintest overseas cadence. "Six hours before Huang Boren's Banyan Club vanished, a local developer tried to force a demolition contract on Yunxi Old Street. And on the exact morning Zhou Guodong lost his shipping licenses, his nephew had an argument with a logistics clerk at the docks."

He tapped a single white node sitting at the dead center of the crimson web.

A basic civil registry photo appeared on the glass. Beneath it were four lines of unremarkable data.

Name: Chen Xuan. Employer: Hengxin Logistics. Position: Operations Supervisor. Monthly salary: five thousand yuan.

Li Weiming stared at the ordinary young face in the photograph, his fingers resting lightly against the slate's brushed aluminum frame.

"The analysts in Zurich called it statistical noise," Li Weiming whispered, his eyes narrowing behind his rimless glasses. "They wanted to look at provincial secretaries, rival banking cartels, or central inspection teams. They never learned the simplest rule of intelligence work. When three castles burn down in the same week, you do not stare at the clouds. You look at the man who walked past all three front gates carrying a matchbox."

He scrolled through the rest of Chen Xuan's public file. Ordinary provincial university. Rented apartment in an old residential block. Parents Chen Jianguo and Liu Meifang running a modest household. Grandparents Chen Fugen and Zhou Guilan living on Yunxi Old Street. Zero offshore holdings. Zero registered corporate shares. Zero political affiliations.

It was so clean that it looked freshly washed.

Li Weiming switched off the slate and slid it into his calfskin briefcase. Outside the tinted window, the glass and concrete towers of Qingyan City's commercial district rose against the pale autumn sun.

"Stop across from the Hengxin Logistics building," Li Weiming instructed through the intercom. "Park two blocks down and wait for my call."

* * *

At four fifty in the afternoon, Li Weiming sat beside the floor-to-ceiling window of a quiet coffee shop directly across the four-lane street from Hengxin Logistics.

The shop smelled of roasted beans and warm butter. Soft jazz drifted from ceiling speakers. Li Weiming wore a tailored charcoal wool overcoat with the collar turned neatly flat, a small white porcelain cup of double espresso resting beside his elbow. To the barista behind the counter, he looked like a senior banker visiting from Shanghai or Hong Kong.

Across the street, the aging twelve-story office tower that housed Hengxin Logistics looked thoroughly unremarkable. Its blue-tinted glass needed washing, and a delivery van sat half-parked by the loading bay while two drivers unloaded cardboard cartons.

Li Weiming glanced at the silver face of his Swiss watch. Four fifty-three.

He lifted the espresso cup, took a measured sip, and kept his gaze fixed on the double glass doors of the lobby.

At four fifty-five sharp, before the five o'clock commuter rush had even begun, the lobby doors swung open.

A young man stepped out onto the concrete stairs.

He wore a washed gray zip-up jacket over a plain cotton shirt, dark trousers that had been ironed cleanly at home, and comfortable walking shoes. Over his left shoulder hung a faded canvas messenger bag. In his right hand, he carried a dark-green stainless steel thermos with a long pale scratch running down its side.

Chen Xuan.

He paused half a step from the glass door to hold it open for a middle-aged courier balancing three parcel boxes. When the courier thanked him, Chen Xuan offered a mild, unhurried nod, exchanged two polite words with the lobby security guard, and walked calmly down the front steps toward the crosswalk.

There was no tension in his shoulders. No habit of checking reflections in shop windows. No brisk urgency in his stride. He looked like a man whose biggest concern for the evening was whether the neighborhood market still had fresh ribs.

A slow, cold smile curved Li Weiming's lips.

"Punctual," Li Weiming murmured softly. "Five minutes early, green thermos in hand, zero security detail in sight. Either you are the luckiest clerk in Lanshui Province, Chen Xuan, or you are wearing the finest mask I have seen in ten years."

He set the porcelain cup down cleanly on its saucer and tapped his left ear, where a flesh-colored bone-conduction transmitter sat invisible beneath his jawline.

* * *

Two tables away, near the coffee shop's rear exit, two lean men in plain civilian windbreakers finished folding their newspapers.

They did not look like bodyguards or street thugs. One wore round glasses and carried a scuffed leather backpack; the other had a runner's build and held a paper takeout cup. Both held European commercial passports, both had spent seven years in private maritime intelligence and urban reconnaissance, and both had arrived in Qingyan City on separate high-speed trains that morning.

"Target is on the south sidewalk," Li Weiming said quietly, his voice barely louder than the jazz music. "Gray jacket, canvas shoulder bag, green thermos. Heading east toward the bus station."

The man with the round glasses gave a barely perceptible tilt of his head and stood up. His partner followed three seconds later, exiting onto the sidewalk smoothly without a single glance toward Li Weiming's table.

"Do not approach him openly," Li Weiming instructed, watching through the glass as Chen Xuan waited patiently for the pedestrian light to turn green. "Stay in standard staggered box formation. Wait for a light crowd near the station or a street stall. Plant one passive micro-beacon inside the lower seam of his canvas bag. Once the signal locks, hold a sixty-meter tail and log every address he visits and every person he speaks with until midnight."

A faint double click sounded in Li Weiming's ear, confirming the order.

The pedestrian light shifted. Chen Xuan crossed the avenue at an easy, unhurried pace, blending effortlessly into the stream of office workers and shoppers. Thirty paces behind him, the two foreign-trained trackers crossed on the same light, moving with fluid, practiced discipline.

Li Weiming leaned back in his leather armchair, swirling the last dark drop of espresso at the bottom of his cup.

"Let us see who you really talk to after five o'clock, Supervisor Chen," Li Weiming whispered.
