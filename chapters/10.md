# Chapter 10: Two Witnesses

An hour later, across the lane from the station, the Yu Tailor Shop smelled of steam and hot iron, and the door was shut.

Chen Xuan knocked twice, and then once more.

"Uncle Yu? It's Xuan, Mingde's nephew. We brought soy milk."

The latch clicked, and Old Yu's face appeared in the gap, gray as his hair.

"I'm closed."

"Mm. But the sign says open."

"I have seen nothing, Xuan." The old man's eyes slid sideways. "Old eyes. Too much dust on this street. Go home."

"I'm sitting," said Chen Xuan. "The soy milk is getting cold."

Old Yu stood aside.

At the end of the cutting table, a little girl in a school uniform was coloring a rabbit purple.

She looked up, and her eyes went straight to the paper bag.

"Is that a fried dough stick?"

"Yes," said Chen Xuan, and tore one in half for her. "Do rabbits come in purple?"

"Mine does."

"Good. Then it is correct."

Ning laughed, and Old Yu's shoulders came down a little.

"Grandpa has a secret," she told Chen Xuan happily. "A big man patted my head. Then Grandpa put a fat envelope in the thread drawer, and he cried when he thought I was drawing."

The room went still.

Old Yu's face crumpled.

"Ning, go and wash your hands!"

She ran to the back sink.

Old Yu sat down on the wooden stool and put both hands over his eyes.

"Twenty thousand yuan," he said into his palms. "From Big Tooth. 'You saw nothing.' He told me the stairs by the river are slippery. Xuan, she is nine."

Chen Xuan sat beside him and put a warm cup of soy milk between his hands.

"Uncle Yu," he said, "you don't have to be brave. You just have to be honest, once, on paper."

"And then?"

"The paper goes to a lawyer I know, not to Hongrui. Nobody here has to know who wrote it. Your name stays in a drawer."

"They will know."

"Then they will have to find the drawer. Keep that envelope exactly as it is. Don't spend it. A bribe that sits in a drawer is also a witness."

Old Yu stared at him.

"Xuan, who are you?"

"A clerk. I check forms."

He took out a plain notebook and a pen.

Old Yu's hand reached out as if it had made the choice before the rest of him, and he wrote slowly, in square characters.

*On Friday, at about 2:03 p.m., I saw Mingde Hardware's counter from my door. Chen Mingde did not leave it. A young man in a driver's uniform looked at Brother Hu and threw himself into the paint shelf. At 2:05 the police came and handcuffed Mingde.*

He signed his name and pressed his thumb beside it, and his tears dropped on the page and wrinkled one corner.

"Thank you, Uncle Yu," said Chen Xuan, and put the page away. "And Ning will not walk to school alone from today. I will see to it."

He looked out of the window at the noodle shop across the lane, where a black security camera hung under a red awning and stared down the street.

"That is a second witness," he said. "A camera doesn't get tired and doesn't get scared. Who owns that shop?"

"Gong's Noodles. A quiet fellow, three years now. His broth is good."

"Mm," said Chen Xuan. "I'll have a bowl one day."

Behind the shutter of the closed noodle shop across the lane, Gong Yi, Number Twenty-three, in his flour-dusted apron, was reading a screen with all his teeth showing.

"He talked about my camera!" he told the delivery rider who had slipped in through the back. "The Boss looked right at it and said, 'I'll have a bowl one day.' Little Ke, did you hear?"

"I heard, Twenty-three. I sat beside him in the lobby. He ate a bun."

"Get serious. Look here."

He slapped down nine weeks of public records.

"Eleven shopkeepers, eleven signatures, each one the morning after an 'assault' arrest at Old Street Station. And every injury slip came from Ankang Private Clinic, which shares an accountant with Hongrui."

"Same clinic, same price," said Little Ke, and his grin faded. "That is not a clinic. That is a vending machine."

"And this notice, Saturday, 7:50 a.m., in plain ink. Somebody in that station refused to write the lie." Gong Yi tapped the screen. "It's my camera. A shop owner can hand his own footage to a lawyer, through the front door, with a receipt."

Across the lane, a man in a gray jacket sat at the tea stall with a briefcase.

"Attorney Ye Tong just happened to need tea," said Gong Yi. "He will carry the footage and Old Yu's page to the city tonight. But if Brother Hu comes down this street again, I won't promise to keep my hands clean."

"Keep one clean," said Little Ke, "and lend me the other."

* * *

At twenty past four that afternoon, Old Yu walked Ning to her mother's workshop with her small hand held tight in his.

He kept to the road side, the way a man shields a candle from the wind.

Big Tooth had said the stairs by the river were slippery, and he had not slept since.

Behind them, an engine woke up without a horn.

A gray van with brown paper taped over its plates had been idling by the arch.

Now it came on, drifting toward the curb.

Old Yu saw a cap, two hands on the wheel, and a driver who did not mean to stop.

From a side lane, a bread truck painted with golden wheat shot across the road and stopped broadside.

Bang!

The van hit its flank, spun into the sandbags by the river wall, and hot buns bounced across the asphalt like rain.

A round baker named Fatty Cao jumped down, dragged the driver out by the collar and sat on him.

A county traffic officer on a motorbike arrived in ten seconds, as if he had been waiting around the corner, which he had.

"I lost control!" the driver cried.

"With brown paper on your plates, brother?" The cuffs clicked.

A woman in a white coat ran up from Everbright Family Clinic at the corner and knelt to check Ning's scraped palm.

Nobody on the road knew that the bakery, the clinic and the officer were all one team.

"Heaven has eyes!" cried Meng Xiaofang.

"Luck," said Cobbler Sun. "A drunk, and a baker with good timing."

Old Yu sat on the curb with Ning in his arms and watched the baker whistle and pick up buns.

Nobody sends a bread truck to an old tailor.

So who had?

* * *

After dark, four men came down the lane with their hands in their pockets, and every shutter went down at once.

Big Tooth walked first, and Thin Monkey followed with an iron pipe, tapping the wall.

Behind them came Brother Hu, smiling softly, a towel on his shoulder.

"Old Yu! Come out! Brother Hu has brought a present!"

The old tailor stepped into his doorway with Ning behind his leg.

"I wrote nothing," he said.

"Did I say you wrote anything?" Brother Hu laughed. "It's just that a little bird told me an old tailor was telling stories. Boss Dong pays me eight hundred yuan a night per man to stop stories. Five thousand if a window breaks. A good night's work, hm?"

He looked past the old man at the little girl, and the towel on his shoulder twitched. "How old is she? Nine? Schools have back lanes, you know. Nine is a good age to learn that pipes are heavy. Thin Monkey, show him what a good night looks like."

Thin Monkey swung the pipe, and the tailor's wooden sign flew off its hook and cracked on the stones.

Ning screamed.

Then, from across the dark lane, a heavy iron shoe last came spinning through the air.

Clang!

It caught Thin Monkey right on the shoulder, and he howled and dropped the pipe.

Cobbler Sun stood in his doorway with a second iron last in his hand and his gray head pushed forward.

He was sixty-one, thin as a broom, and shaking so hard that his teeth rattled.

"You broke an old man's sign in front of his granddaughter," said Cobbler Sun. "I'm an Old Street man, and I don't like it."

Brother Hu looked at the old cobbler as if a stool had spoken.

Then, very slowly, he stopped smiling.

"Big Tooth," he said softly. "Get the old man. Not the face. Boss Dong doesn't like marks."

At the corner, a bread truck with a dented side, parked there since afternoon, quietly turned on its lights.
