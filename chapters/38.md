# Chapter 38: The Red Line That Doesn't Ring

By twenty-five minutes past one on Monday morning, a low haze of cigarette smoke already hung beneath the lamp of Vice Premier Zhou Zhenhua's study.

Zhou Zhenhua sat upright behind his desk, his wool coat still over his shoulders. Along the left edge of the blotter stood four encrypted red telephones linked by dedicated underground fiber to four pillars of the state: the Premier's office, the Ministry of State Security, the Central Military Commission duty desk, and the Supreme People's Procuratorate.

Across the desk, Tang Weimin stood rigid, clutching a notebook against his chest so hard his knuckles had lost their color. His spectacles kept slipping slightly down his damp nose, and every few seconds he pushed them up with a nervous jab.

Zhou Zhenhua lifted the first red receiver, the direct line to the Premier's residence, and held it to his ear for five full seconds.

Not a ring sounded. Not a dial tone, not a busy chime, not even static. Just a cold, airless silence.

He set the receiver back on its cradle with a quiet click.

Without speaking, he lifted the second handset, wired to the Ministry of State Security. Five seconds of dead, silent hold. He hung it up. Then the third line, straight to the Central Military Commission command center. Silent hold. Finally, the fourth line to the Supreme People's Procuratorate. The exact same cold void.

"Not a single one rings," Zhou Zhenhua said softly, resting his fingertips against the desk.

Tang Weimin's breath caught audibly. "Vice Premier, those are Tier-One encrypted red lines! They run on independent underground power and are shielded against electromagnetic pulses. Even during the ninety-eight floods and border alerts, the red network never went dark for a second. How could all four lines be dead at once?"

"They are not dead, Tang Weimin," Zhou Zhenhua corrected him, lighting a fresh cigarette from the dying ember of the old one. He dropped the stub into the ashtray. "If the fiber were cut, the handset would buzz with a line-fault tone. Listen to what you did not hear. There is no fault tone. Every single one of those lines is being held in silent suspension at the central exchange."

Tang Weimin's eyes widened, sudden dread draining his face. "Someone seized the central red exchange? Vice Premier, that is treason! If someone has cut off a sitting Vice Premier from the Premier and the military, we are under house arrest!"

"Sit down before your legs give out," Zhou Zhenhua ordered sharply, pointing two smoke-stained fingers at the chair opposite him. "And stop shouting the word treason inside a room whose walls might be listening."

Tang Weimin flinched as if struck. He lowered himself onto the edge of the chair, his eyes darting involuntarily toward the corners of the ceiling. "Vice Premier, you mean the room is monitored too?"

"Assume every wire, every microphone, and every window in this compound is under observation until sunrise," Zhou Zhenhua said evenly. He took a long drag, letting thick gray smoke drift between them. "And get house arrest out of your head. What you are witnessing right now is an Absolute Order."

Tang Weimin stared at him, lips parting slightly. "An Absolute Order? I have handled your classified briefings for seven years, Vice Premier. I have never seen a document with that title."

"Because you are not supposed to see it on paper," Zhou Zhenhua replied, his voice dropping low and heavy. "When the founders built this republic, they knew that one day the machinery of power would grow so tangled with alliances, factions, and family patronage that ordinary discipline committees could no longer touch the highest seats. Try to arrest one corrupt minister through regular channels, and three secretaries leak the warrant, two vice-governors burn their ledgers, and a dozen relatives board private jets before midnight."

Tang Weimin's grip tightened on his notebook. "So the Absolute Order pauses everything?"

"It freezes the entire state apparatus mid-breath," Zhou Zhenhua said, tapping ash into the tray. "No red phones ring. No private cars leave the ministerial compounds. No senior official can pick up a receiver to warn an old classmate, a former subordinate, or a patron on a committee. Eight sentries stand in my courtyard not because they distrust me personally, but because tonight, rank buys nobody an exception."

Silence pressed down on the study as smoke curled thicker around the lampshade.

"Vice Premier," Tang Weimin whispered, leaning forward, terror mixing with sudden realization, "if they locked down the entire capital, the ministries, and the Central Military Commission, who could possibly be under sweep tonight? You do not freeze the red network over a municipal party secretary or a provincial bureau chief."

Zhou Zhenhua's eyes narrowed into sharp slits. He stared at the glowing orange tip of his cigarette, his mind turning through every ministry, state bank, and procurement board he had sat across from over the last twenty years.

"Look at what has been sitting in front of our faces for years, Tang Weimin," Zhou Zhenhua murmured. "State bank shortfalls that vanished into restructuring funds. Strategic resource auctions where winning bids went to shell companies registered through offshore islands. Defense contractors whose audits were sealed under national security clauses before my office could even inspect the invoices."

Tang Weimin paled further. "You think tonight's sweep is hitting the financial ministries? Or the state conglomerates?"

"Worse," Zhou Zhenhua said grimly. "If it were only money, Black Pine Guard would have handled it quietly over six months with sealed indictments and silent removals. You do not bring Zero Guard out of the dark and put mute sentries outside every leader's gate just because someone stole a few billion yuan."

"Then what?" Tang Weimin breathed, barely audible. "What is worse than billions stolen from the treasury?"

Zhou Zhenhua looked straight into his secretary's terrified eyes. "Selling the state itself."

A sharp chill seemed to drop the room's temperature. Tang Weimin's jaw clenched so hard a muscle jumped beneath his cheek. He hesitated for several long seconds before forcing out the question clawing at his throat.

"Vice Premier," Tang Weimin said tightly, "forgive me for asking, because I have to know. We oversee twelve ministries and four major state banks. If deputy ministers or directors who report to your desk are being dragged out of their beds right now, what happens to us?"

Zhou Zhenhua did not look away. His face was carved from cold stone, without a flicker of panic. He took one final drag and crushed the stub into the porcelain tray.

"Have I ever taken a single fen that did not belong to me, Tang Weimin?"

"Never, Vice Premier," Tang Weimin answered instantly, his voice fierce with certainty. "Not a single fen. Not even a box of tea from a provincial governor."

"Then stop sweating," Zhou Zhenhua said calmly, though his eyes stayed dark and heavy. "If our hands are clean, those eight sentries will vanish before the morning broadcast. And if any deputy minister under my portfolio sold out the republic's lifeblood behind my back, then I hope Zero Guard drags him out in chains before dawn, because if they leave him to me, I will sign the execution warrant myself."

Tang Weimin let out a long, shaky breath and lowered his notebook to his lap. Outside the curtained window, the capital remained frozen in dead silence. Inside the study, Zhou Zhenhua lit another cigarette without a tremor in his hand and settled back to wait for sunrise.
