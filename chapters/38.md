# Chapter 38: Still Here

By nine on Saturday morning, half of Old Street was standing in front of Mingde Hardware.

The crooked shutter had been fixed in the night by three neighbors and a borrowed drill. The wet red circle on the corner wall was gone too. Han Guifu, the cobbler, had painted over it with a pail of white paint, and he was still wiping his hands on his apron.

"Fresh paint, fresh start," he told everybody. "I used the good white. It cost me forty yuan, and I'm not sending anybody a bill."

Chen Xuan came off the first bus from Qingyan with a canvas bag in his hand. Inside it were three lunch boxes of cold pork.

"What is that?" Liu Meifang asked, grabbing the bag before he could sit.

"The company took us to dinner at a hotel. There was a lot left. I packed it."

"A hotel." She sniffed one box and her face softened. "Good boy. You didn't waste anything. Now eat. You look like a coat hanger."

She pushed a steamed bun into his hand. Meng Xiaofang, who ran the bun stall, had brought a whole tray from her shop and was handing them out like a general with medals.

Above the door, Yu Changshun, the old tailor everyone called Old Yu, was holding the end of a long red banner. Chen Hao stood on a ladder and tied the other end to a nail.

The banner was sewn from red cloth, with gold letters Old Yu had cut himself: *Mingde Hardware: Still Here.*

"Still here," Chen Mingde read aloud. His voice shook. He pulled off his glasses and wiped them on his shirt. "Old Yu, it's too much."

"It's cloth," Yu Changshun said. "Cloth is cheap. Standing is not."

Han Guifu lit a string of firecrackers on the pavement. It cracked down the street like a machine gun, and Wu Lanying squeaked and covered her ears. Yu Changshun's little granddaughter, Yu Ning, clapped so hard that her hat fell off.

Then Wu Lanying let go of her ears and grabbed Chen Xuan. She pulled his head onto her shoulder and cried into his jacket.

"Little Xuan. You came so early. Did you eat?"

"I had a bun, Second Aunt."

"One bun is not eating." She patted his back with a shaking hand. "Your uncle is home. The shop is home. Nobody is taking anything."

Chen Xuan held on and let her cry. His own eyes were hot, and he did not hide it.

Chen Jianguo cleared his throat. He stood in front of the shop with his chin up, like a man about to give a speech at a wedding.

"As the old saying goes. A hardware shop is like a good friend. You only miss it when something falls off."

"Dad," Chen Hao said from the ladder. "Which book is that from?"

"The book I am writing."

Everybody laughed, even Chen Mingde. It was the first time anyone in the family had laughed in two weeks.

Chen Mingde took his nephew's wrist. "Little Xuan. You work in the city. Do you know who got those inspectors to come two days early? I would like to thank him."

"Hmm?" Chen Xuan swallowed his bun. "I think it was a friend who knows forms."

"What kind of forms?"

"Inspection forms. Probably."

"Luck and a friend with forms," Chen Jianguo said, nodding. "It's the same thing, Mingde. Don't ask a lucky man to explain."

"You're hopeless, Big Brother," Chen Yuan said, and stole the last bun from his hand.

* * *

Around eleven, a delivery tricycle rattled down Old Street with forty parcels tied on its back. It stopped in front of the banner.

Wang Lei jumped off. He had a sunburned neck, a worn jacket and the same shy grin he had worn in senior year.

"Old Chen." He stopped when he saw the crowd. "Uncle Chen. I heard you were back. I wanted to see it with my own eyes."

"Wang Lei." Chen Mingde hurried out from behind the counter and held both his hands. "Come in. You were the only one who came when I was gone."

"Only for a hinge. I'm back for screws." Wang Lei scratched his head. "A box of M4s, for my cargo rack. It keeps rattling."

Chen Mingde filled a small paper box. When Wang Lei pushed a crumpled eight yuan across the counter, he shook his head hard.

"Not from you."

"No." Wang Lei put his palm flat on the money. "A shop with no sale is not a shop, Uncle Chen. Please. Let it be the first one."

Chen Mingde looked at him a long time. Then he took the bills and pinned them to the wall above the register with a thumbtack, smoothing out the creases.

"The first sale of Still Here," he said. His voice cracked.

Chen Xuan watched from the doorway. When Wang Lei turned, he gave a small smile.

"How's your dad, Wang Lei?"

The grin slipped for a second. Wang Lei looked at the floor.

"Not good. It's his kidneys. The doctor says he needs an operation, and the hospital wants 90,000 yuan before the twentieth. I have 22,000. I pay 1,500 every month on the rest." He tried to laugh. "It will be fine. I run fast."

"Oh." Chen Xuan's throat closed. "I'm sorry, Wang Lei."

"Don't be. You have enough trouble." Wang Lei pushed the screw box into his jacket. "I have forty parcels. Next time I will buy you a real lunch."

The tricycle clattered off toward the river.

Chen Xuan stood where he was. He could still see a plastic tray in a school cafeteria, and a hand putting eight yuan beside it. Every day for thirty days. Two hundred and forty yuan. Nobody had ever asked for it back.

He slipped out the back door of the shop, into the narrow lane where the garbage bins stood. He took out the gray phone and typed with his thumb.

*Number One. Wang Lei's father gets the best bed and the surgeon he needs. Wang Lei gets a real job and a loan that never comes due. He must think it is luck.*

The answer came in seconds. *Yes, Boss.*

In a quiet courtyard in Qingyan, Number One opened a locked drawer. He turned to the first page of the first ledger and found the name Wang Lei. Beside it were three words in his own hand: *Eight yuan. Thirty days.*

He crossed out the small number and began to write a larger one.

* * *

By late afternoon, the street had gone back to its old rhythm. Meng Xiaofang counted her unsold buns. Han Guifu hammered a heel.

Chen Xuan sat on the back step of the shop with a cold bun in his lap, watching Chen Hao sort screws into drawers. M4. M5. Flat head. Brass. His brother did it with the same slow care he had once used himself.

His phone rang. The name on the screen was Wang Lei.

He picked up. All he heard at first was a sound like a kettle that could not decide between laughing and crying.

"Xuan. Xuan, are you there?"

"I'm here. What happened?"

"Two phone calls. Two, in one hour." Wang Lei's voice tripped over itself. "First, a courier company called me. Rongtong Express. They said they pick one driver a month from the good-review list, and this month it is me. Team leader, 9,000 yuan a month, and a loan for a van with no interest, nothing due until I say so. I thought it was a scam, so I asked them three times."

"That sounds good."

"Then the hospital called. A charity fund, the Warm Lantern Fund. They paid the whole 90,000. The whole thing, Xuan. They moved my dad to a single room, and the head surgeon says he will do the operation himself." He sobbed out loud now. "Who does that? I'm nobody. I deliver parcels."

Chen Xuan pressed the back of his wrist against his eyes. Behind the shop, Chen Hao looked up from the drawers and then politely looked down again.

"Lucky," Chen Xuan said. His voice was not steady. "That's really lucky, Wang Lei."

"I know. I know it is." Wang Lei laughed, wet and loud. "I'll buy you a lunch. I'll buy you ten."

"One is plenty."

When the call ended, Chen Xuan sat very still. A boy had put eight yuan beside his tray, eleven years ago, and had never mentioned it. Today it had turned into a bed, a surgeon and a job, and the boy would think it was luck all his life.

He wiped his face on his sleeve. Then, because he was hungry, he ate the cold bun anyway.
