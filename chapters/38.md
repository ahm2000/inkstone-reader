# Chapter 38: The Red Line That Doesn't Ring

By twenty-five minutes past one on Monday morning, a thin haze of cigarette smoke already hung beneath the lamp in Vice Premier Zhou Zhenhua's study.

Zhou Zhenhua sat straight behind his desk with his wool coat still draped over his shoulders. Along the left edge of the desk stood four private red phones connected by underground lines to four main offices of the state: the Premier's office, the Ministry of State Security, the Central Military Commission duty desk, and the Supreme Prosecutor's Office.

Across the desk, Tang Weimin stood stiffly, hugging a notebook against his chest so tightly his knuckles turned white. His glasses kept slipping down his damp nose, and every few seconds he pushed them back up with a nervous finger.

Zhou Zhenhua picked up the first red phone, the direct line to the Premier's home, and held it to his ear for five full seconds.

Not a single ring sounded. No dial tone, no busy beep, and not even static. Just a cold, empty silence.

He set the phone back down with a quiet click.

Without saying a word, he picked up the second phone, wired straight to the Ministry of State Security. Five seconds of dead silence. He hung it up. Next came the third line, straight to the Central Military Commission command desk. Dead silence. Finally, he lifted the fourth line to the Supreme Prosecutor's Office. The exact same cold void.

"Not a single one rings," Zhou Zhenhua said softly, resting his fingertips on the desk.

Tang Weimin let out a sharp gasp. "Vice Premier, those are top-level private red lines! They run on their own underground power and are built to survive even a major attack. Even during the ninety-eight floods and border alerts, the red lines never went dark for one second. How could all four lines be dead at once?"

"They are not dead, Tang Weimin," Zhou Zhenhua corrected him. He lit a fresh cigarette off the glowing end of the old one and dropped the stub into the ashtray. "If the cable were cut, the phone would make a broken-line buzz. Listen to what you did not hear. There is no fault buzz. Every single one of those lines has been put on silent hold at the main switchboard."

Tang Weimin's eyes went wide as sudden dread drained all color from his face. "Someone took over the main red switchboard? Vice Premier, that is treason! If someone has cut off a sitting Vice Premier from the Premier and the army, then we are under house arrest!"

"Sit down before your legs give out," Zhou Zhenhua ordered sharply, pointing two smoke-stained fingers at the chair across from him. "And stop yelling the word treason inside a room whose walls might be listening."

Tang Weimin flinched as if he had been slapped. He lowered himself onto the edge of the chair, his eyes darting up toward the corners of the ceiling. "Vice Premier, you mean this room is being watched too?"

"Assume every phone wire, every wall, and every window in this home is under watch until sunrise," Zhou Zhenhua said evenly. He took a long drag and let thick gray smoke drift between them. "And get house arrest out of your head. What you are seeing tonight is an Black Order."

Tang Weimin stared at him, his mouth opening slightly. "An Black Order? I have handled your secret reports for seven years, Vice Premier. I have never once seen a file with that name."

"Because you are not supposed to see it on paper," Zhou Zhenhua replied, his voice dropping low and heavy. "When the founders built this republic, they knew that one day the halls of power would grow so tangled with favors, factions, and family ties that regular inspection teams could no longer touch the highest seats. Try to arrest one corrupt minister through normal steps, and three secretaries leak the warrant, two vice-governors burn their account books, and a dozen relatives jump on private planes before midnight."

Tang Weimin tightened his grip on his notebook. "So the Black Order stops everything?"

"It freezes the whole government mid-breath," Zhou Zhenhua said, tapping ash into the tray. "No red phones ring. No private cars leave the ministry yards. No senior official can pick up a phone to warn an old classmate, a former aide, or a backer on a committee. Eight guards stand in my yard not because they doubt me personally, but because tonight, rank buys nobody a free pass."

Heavy silence pressed down on the study as smoke curled thicker around the lamp.

"Vice Premier," Tang Weimin whispered, leaning forward as fear mixed with sudden shock, "if they locked down the whole capital, the ministries, and the Central Military Commission, who could possibly be under sweep tonight? You do not freeze the red phone network over a city party secretary or a province office head."

Zhou Zhenhua's eyes narrowed into sharp slits. He stared at the glowing orange tip of his cigarette, thinking back over every ministry, state bank, and supply board he had worked with over the last twenty years.

"Look at what has been sitting right in front of our faces for years, Tang Weimin," Zhou Zhenhua murmured. "Missing state bank money that vanished into rescue funds. Key resource sales where winning bids went to shell companies set up on overseas islands. Defense suppliers whose checks were sealed under state secret rules before my office could even look at the bills."

Tang Weimin turned even paler. "You think tonight's sweep is hitting the finance ministries? Or the big state companies?"

"Worse," Zhou Zhenhua said grimly. "If it were only money, White Guard would have handled it quietly over six months with secret charges and quiet removals. You do not bring Black Guard out of the dark and put silent guards outside every leader's gate just because someone stole a few billion yuan."

"Then what?" Tang Weimin breathed, barely loud enough to hear. "What is worse than billions stolen from the state?"

Zhou Zhenhua looked straight into his secretary's terrified eyes. "Selling the country itself."

A sharp chill seemed to drop the temperature in the room. Tang Weimin clenched his jaw so hard a muscle jumped under his cheek. He waited several long seconds before forcing out the question clawing at his throat.

"Vice Premier," Tang Weimin said tightly, "forgive me for asking, because I have to know. We oversee twelve ministries and four major state banks. If deputy ministers or directors who report to your desk are being dragged out of their beds right now, what happens to us?"

Zhou Zhenhua did not look away. His face looked carved from cold stone, without a hint of fear. He took one last drag and crushed the cigarette into the tray.

"Have I ever taken a single fen that did not belong to me, Tang Weimin?"

"Never, Vice Premier," Tang Weimin answered right away, his voice fierce and certain. "Not a single fen. Not even a box of tea from a province governor."

"Then stop sweating," Zhou Zhenhua said calmly, though his eyes stayed dark and heavy. "If our hands are clean, those eight guards will be gone before the morning news. And if any deputy minister under my watch sold out the republic's lifeblood behind my back, then I hope Black Guard drags him out in chains before dawn. Because if they leave him to me, I will sign his death warrant myself."

Tang Weimin let out a long, shaky breath and lowered his notebook to his lap. Outside the curtained window, the capital stayed frozen in dead silence. Inside the study, Zhou Zhenhua lit another cigarette without a single shake in his hand and settled back to wait for sunrise.
