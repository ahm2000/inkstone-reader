# Chapter 38: The Red Line That Doesn't Ring

By twenty-five past one on Monday morning, thin cigarette smoke hung beneath the lamp in Vice Premier Zhou Zhenhua's study.

Zhou Zhenhua sat straight behind his desk, wool coat still over his shoulders. Along the left edge stood four private red phones wired underground to the Premier's office, the Ministry of State Security, the Central Military Commission duty desk, and the Supreme Prosecutor's Office.

Across the desk, Tang Weimin stood stiffly, hugging a notebook until his knuckles shone white, pushing his slipping glasses up his damp nose.

Zhou Zhenhua lifted the first red phone, the direct line to the Premier's home, and held it for five full seconds.

Not a ring sounded. No dial tone, no busy beep, no static. Only cold, empty silence.

He set it down with a quiet click.

Without a word, he tested the second line to the Ministry of State Security, the third to the Central Military Commission, and the fourth to the Supreme Prosecutor's Office. Every single one returned the exact same void.

"Not a single one rings," Zhou Zhenhua said softly, resting his fingertips on the desk.

Tang Weimin gasped. "Vice Premier, those are top-level private red lines! They run on their own underground power and survive major attacks. Even during the ninety-eight floods and border alerts, they never went dark for a second. How could all four be dead at once?"

"They are not dead, Tang Weimin," Zhou Zhenhua corrected, lighting a fresh cigarette off the glowing stub. "If the cable were cut, you would hear a broken-line buzz. There is no buzz. Every line has been placed on silent hold at the main switchboard."

Dread drained all color from Tang Weimin's face. "Someone took over the main red switchboard? Vice Premier, that is treason! Cutting off a sitting Vice Premier from the Premier and the army means we are under house arrest!"

"Sit down before your legs give out," Zhou Zhenhua snapped, pointing two smoke-stained fingers at the chair. "And stop yelling treason inside a room whose walls might be listening."

Tang Weimin flinched and lowered himself onto the chair, eyes darting to the ceiling corners. "Vice Premier, this room is being watched too?"

"Assume every wire, wall, and window in this house is watched until sunrise," Zhou Zhenhua said evenly, blowing gray smoke between them. "And forget house arrest. What you are seeing tonight is a Black Order."

Tang Weimin stared, mouth slightly open. "A Black Order? I have handled your secret reports for seven years, Vice Premier. I have never once seen a file with that name."

"Because you are not supposed to see it on paper," Zhou Zhenhua replied, voice dropping low. "When the founders built this republic, they knew the halls of power would grow tangled with favors, factions, and family ties until normal inspection teams could not touch the highest seats. Try to arrest one corrupt minister through regular steps, and three secretaries leak the warrant, two vice-governors burn account books, and a dozen relatives board private planes before midnight."

Tang Weimin gripped his notebook tighter. "So the Black Order stops everything?"

"It freezes the whole government mid-breath," Zhou Zhenhua said, tapping ash into the tray. "No red phones ring. No private cars leave ministry yards. No senior official can call to warn an old classmate, former aide, or committee backer. Eight guards stand in my yard not because they doubt me, but because tonight rank buys nobody a free pass."

Heavy silence pressed down on the study.

"Vice Premier," Tang Weimin whispered, fear mixing with shock, "if they locked down the capital, the ministries, and the Central Military Commission, who is under sweep tonight? You do not freeze the red network over a city party secretary or a province office head."

Zhou Zhenhua narrowed his eyes, thinking back over twenty years of ministries, state banks, and supply boards.

"Look at what has sat right in front of us for years, Tang Weimin," he murmured. "Missing state bank money vanishing into rescue funds. Key resource sales going to shell companies on overseas islands. Defense suppliers whose bills were sealed under state secret rules before my office could even read them."

Tang Weimin turned paler. "Tonight's sweep is hitting the finance ministries? Or the big state companies?"

"Worse," Zhou Zhenhua said grimly. "If it were only money, White Guard would have handled it quietly over six months with secret charges and quiet removals. You do not wake Black Guard and post silent sentries outside every leader's gate just because someone stole a few billion yuan."

"Then what?" Tang Weimin breathed. "What is worse than billions stolen from the state?"

Zhou Zhenhua looked straight into his secretary's terrified eyes. "Selling the country itself."

A sharp chill dropped over the room. Tang Weimin clenched his jaw before forcing out the question clawing at his throat.

"Vice Premier," Tang Weimin said tightly, "forgive me for asking, because I have to know. We oversee twelve ministries and four major state banks. If deputy ministers or directors who report to your desk are being dragged from their beds right now, what happens to us?"

Zhou Zhenhua's face looked carved from stone, without a hint of fear. He took one last drag and crushed the cigarette into the tray.

"Have I ever taken a single fen that did not belong to me, Tang Weimin?"

"Never, Vice Premier," Tang Weimin answered right away, fierce and certain. "Not a single fen. Not even a box of tea from a province governor."

"Then stop sweating," Zhou Zhenhua said calmly, eyes dark and heavy. "If our hands are clean, those eight guards will be gone before the morning news. And if any deputy minister under my watch sold out the republic's lifeblood behind my back, I hope Black Guard drags him out in chains before dawn. Because if they leave him to me, I will sign his death warrant myself."

Tang Weimin let out a shaky breath and lowered his notebook. Outside the curtains, the capital stayed frozen in silence. Inside, Zhou Zhenhua lit another cigarette with a steady hand and settled back to wait for sunrise.
