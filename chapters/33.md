# Chapter 33: Grandma's Birthday

Hongshi Village, the grandparents' courtyard. Grandma's eighty-first birthday.

Today Zhou Guilan turned eighty-one, and every relative in the Chen family knew the unwritten rule: the party was for the birthday lady, but the gift table was for everybody else!

Chen Xuan stood by the courtyard wall with a plain paper bag in one hand and his scratched green thermos in the other, and his plan for the day was small. He would eat well, sit near his grandmother, and let the relatives enjoy their contest.

"Xuan, look, look!" Liu Meifang pinched his sleeve. "Big Aunt is here!"

A shiny white SUV had stopped in the lane, right across the gate, so nobody could miss it.

Out stepped Chen Jinhua, Chen Jianguo's older sister, in a purple jacket with her chin held high, and her son Guo Peng followed her, carrying a red liquor box in both hands like a trophy.

"Two hundred thousand yuan, that car," Liu Meifang whispered, because she knew the price of everything in Yunxi County. "It is more than a year of your pay!"

"Oh, nice," said Chen Xuan.

"That is all you say?!"

"It's a good color."

Behind them, Chen Yuan snorted into her tea so hard that she nearly dropped the cup.

"Mother, happy birthday!" Chen Jinhua marched up to Zhou Guilan and slid a gold bracelet over her tiny wrist. "Eleven thousand yuan, real gold, and I checked the stamp myself!"

It was a generous gift, but to put it bluntly, it was a gift that wanted every guest to ask how much it cost!

The bracelet slid down to Zhou Guilan's elbow and stayed there.

"And this," Guo Peng announced, lifting his box, "is from Director Liu of the Land Office, and he said it is only for special guests!"

Chen Fugen tipped back his straw hat and looked at the shelf above the stove, where three identical red boxes already sat in a row, each one "from a director."

"Put it with the others, Peng," the old man said kindly. "They will keep each other company."

Chen Hao pulled out his one earbud to laugh properly.

Guo Peng sidled past Chen Xuan on his way to refill his own cup.

"Still at that little logistics company?" he asked, not really asking. "I hear Director Liu's nephew just made regional manager somewhere, and he is twenty-six, if you can believe it."

Chen Xuan said he could believe it, and meant it.

Inside, Liu Meifang rebuilt the gift table three times, with big boxes in front, small ones behind, and the family's own pork ribs in the very back.

"Mom, the ribs are hiding," Chen Yuan said.

"Ribs are not a gift, ribs are lunch, so do not confuse the guests!"

Then came the red envelopes, which in the Chen family were read like report cards.

Chen Jinhua's was thick enough to stand up by itself. Second Uncle Chen Mingde, still a little thinner after his terrible week, handed his over with both hands, and it was slim, but on the front he had written a blessing in the same careful handwriting he used for the M4 and M5 labels on his drawers.

Zhou Guilan read it twice. Then she tucked it into her inner pocket, not into the drawer with the others.

Nobody said a word, but everyone saw it.

Last came Chen Xuan, who took a flat box out of his paper bag.

It held a heating pad with a soft blue cover and three heat settings, and it cost sixty-nine yuan.

"Sixty-nine?!" Liu Meifang hissed. "Xuan, your cousin brought gold!"

"Grandma's back hurts when it rains," he said mildly. "It rained all week."

Zhou Guilan looked at him, then at the box. She plugged it in right away, sat down on her stool, and pressed it to her back, and her whole face softened.

"Oh!" she said. "It's warm, right where it hurts!"

She pinched his arm out of habit. "Too thin! But this is the best gift of all. Jinhua, take back this bracelet, because it is too heavy for my arm. Put it away so I don't lose it."

Chen Jinhua's smile slid sideways, just like the bracelet.

"Sixty-nine yuan?" an aunt whispered by the stove.

"And the eleven thousand is sitting in a drawer..."

Eleven thousand yuan had just lost to sixty-nine.

"As the old proverb says," Chen Jianguo announced, raising one finger, "gold shines on the wrist, but warmth goes into the bone!"

"Dad, which old book is that from?" said Chen Yuan.

"The side of Xuan's heating pad box," said Chen Jianguo, pointing. "Very wise people, those heating pad people."

---

Out in the village lane, long-life noodles had to be bought fresh from the village shop before noon, or the birthday luck would be "short," and that was Chen Jianguo's rule. The trouble was that his electric scooter, which he had ridden twelve kilometers with great pride, would not start.

He pressed the button nine times.

"You old rascal!"

A thin man in a gray cap came down the lane, pushing a bicycle with a battered toolbox tied to the back. Everyone in Hongshi Village knew Old Wen, the bicycle repairer who passed through now and then, but nobody knew where he lived.

He squatted by the scooter, tipped it, and pressed one loose plug under the seat with his thumb.

Click.

The headlight came on.

"Two minutes!" Chen Jianguo cried, delighted. "How much do I owe you?"

"Two pickled eggs," Old Wen said.

Zhou Guilan, who had followed the sound of her son's complaining, gave him four. Old Wen's phone rang in his pocket, and he glanced at it with a small, tired sigh.

"No," he said quietly into it. "Tell them the tire is flat, all of them. I'll be back when I'm back."

Tang Xiaoyu stood beside the toolbox, holding Zhou Guilan's heating pad against her chest. The lid was still open, and under a greasy rag she saw a heavy brass block with a small red star on its handle and a line of tiny characters ending in "People's Government."

"What's that?" she asked.

Old Wen closed the lid gently.

"A stamp for bicycles. Every good bicycle gets one."

When the lane was empty a minute later, Xiaoyu was still looking at the spot.

How could a man pushing a loaded bicycle disappear in the time it took to eat half an egg?

"A stamp for bicycles," she told Chen Yuan, very seriously.

"That must be a lot of bicycles," said Chen Yuan.

---

Back in the courtyard, the lunch table was a big round one, and in the Chen family a seat was never only a seat. Zhou Guilan and Chen Fugen sat facing the door, and Chen Jinhua sat at Grandmother's right as the eldest child, straightening her bracelet-less wrist. Chen Jianguo and Second Uncle sat close to the soup. The youngest sat nearest the kitchen, because the youngest carried the bowls.

Xiaoyu was carrying them now. She put down the soup, fixed the ladle, ran back for the rice, and ended up standing by the kitchen door with her hands folded, because she had learned in the last few weeks that a girl who takes up no room is never sent away.

Chen Fugen put down his chopsticks.

"Xiaoyu," he said, in a low voice rough from years of fishing wind.

"Come here."

"I'll sit after, Grandfather. I'm helping."

"No." He pointed to the empty stool on his left. "You are a Chen now. At the table."

Nobody spoke, and even Chen Jinhua looked at her bowl.

Xiaoyu walked over slowly and sat on the edge of the stool, hands in her lap. After a long moment, Liu Meifang slid a rib into her bowl without a word, and Xiaoyu picked up her chopsticks.

---

By late afternoon, the guests were leaving in clouds of dust and noise.

Zhou Guilan sat on the bench by the gate with the heating pad against her back, and she patted the space beside her until Chen Xuan sat down.

"This is good," she said. "Warm."

"I'm glad, Grandma."

"You are a good boy."

She looked at the lane and spoke so quietly that even Chen Yuan, listening from the doorway, had to lean forward. "Xuan. When are you bringing a girl home for me to feed?"

"Hmm?"

"Don't 'hmm' me."

Zhou Guilan's eyes were small, bright, and completely serious. "I'm eighty-one. At my age I don't wait for slow fruit."
