# Chapter 33: Grandma's Birthday

Hongshi Village, Dongqiao Town, the grandparents' courtyard. Sunday, 10:30 a.m.

Today Zhou Guilan turned eighty-one, and every relative in the Chen family knew the unwritten rule: the party was for the birthday lady, but the gift table was for everybody else!

Chen Xuan stood by the courtyard wall with a plain paper bag in one hand and his scratched green thermos in the other. His plan for the day was small. Eat well, sit near his grandmother, and let the relatives enjoy their contest.

"Xuan, look, look!" Liu Meifang pinched his sleeve. "Big Aunt is here!"

A shiny white SUV had stopped in the lane, right across the gate, so nobody in the whole village could miss it. Out stepped Chen Jinhua, Chen Jianguo's older sister, in a purple jacket with her chin held high. Her son Guo Peng followed, carrying a red liquor box in both hands like a trophy.

"Two hundred thousand yuan, that car," Liu Meifang whispered. She knew the price of everything in Yunxi County. "More than a year of your pay!"

"Oh, nice," said Chen Xuan.

"That's all you say?!"

"It's a good color."

Behind them, Chen Yuan snorted into her tea so hard she nearly dropped the cup.

"Mother, happy birthday!" Chen Jinhua marched up to Zhou Guilan and slid a gold bracelet over her tiny wrist. "Eleven thousand yuan, real gold! I checked the stamp myself!"

To put it nicely, it was a generous gift. To put it bluntly, it was a gift that wanted every guest to ask how much it cost!

The bracelet slid down to Zhou Guilan's elbow and stayed there.

"And this," Guo Peng announced, lifting his box, "is from Director Liu of the Land Office! He said it's only for special guests!"

Chen Fugen tipped back his straw hat and looked at the shelf above the stove. Three identical red boxes already sat there in a row, each one "from a director."

"Put it with the others, Peng," the old man said kindly. "They'll keep each other company."

Chen Hao pulled out his one earbud so he could laugh properly.

Guo Peng sidled past Chen Xuan on his way to refill his own cup. "Still at that little logistics company?" he asked, not really asking. "I hear Director Liu's nephew just made regional manager somewhere. Twenty-six, if you can believe it." Chen Xuan said he could believe it, and meant it.

Inside, Liu Meifang rebuilt the gift table three times. Big boxes in front, small ones behind, and the family's own pork ribs in the very back.

"Mom, the ribs are hiding," Chen Yuan said.

"Ribs aren't a gift, ribs are lunch! Don't confuse the guests!"

Then came the red envelopes, which in the Chen family were read like report cards. Chen Jinhua's was thick enough to stand up by itself. Second Uncle Chen Mingde, still a little thinner after his terrible week, handed his over with both hands. It was slim, but on the front he had written a blessing in the same careful handwriting he used for the M4 and M5 labels on his drawers.

Zhou Guilan read it twice. Then she tucked it into her inner pocket, not into the drawer with the others.

Last came Chen Xuan. He took a flat box out of his paper bag: a heating pad with a soft blue cover and three heat settings. Sixty-nine yuan.

"Sixty-nine?!" Liu Meifang hissed. "Xuan, your cousin brought gold!"

"Grandma's back hurts when it rains," he said mildly. "It rained all week."

Zhou Guilan looked at him, then at the box. She plugged it in right away, sat down on her stool, and pressed it to her back. Her whole face softened.

"Oh!" she said. "It's warm! Right where it hurts!" She pinched his arm out of habit. "Too thin! But this is the best gift of all. Jinhua, take back this bracelet, it's too heavy for my arm. Put it away so I don't lose it."

Chen Jinhua's smile slid sideways, just like the bracelet.

"As the old proverb says," Chen Jianguo announced, raising one finger, "gold shines on the wrist, but warmth goes into the bone!"

"Dad," said Chen Yuan, "which old book is that from?"

"From me. Just now. It will be old by tomorrow."

Hongshi Village, the lane outside the gate. Sunday, 11:40 a.m.

Long-life noodles had to be bought fresh from the village shop before noon, or the birthday luck would be "short." That was Chen Jianguo's rule, and his electric scooter, which he had ridden twelve kilometers with great pride, would not start.

He pressed the button nine times. "You old rascal!"

A thin man in a gray cap came down the lane, pushing a bicycle with a battered toolbox tied to the back. Everyone in Hongshi Village knew Old Wen, the bicycle repairer who passed through now and then. Nobody knew exactly where he lived.

He squatted by the scooter, tipped it, and pressed one loose plug under the seat with his thumb. Click. The headlight came on.

"Two minutes!" Chen Jianguo cried. "How much do I owe you?"

"Two pickled eggs," Old Wen said.

Zhou Guilan, who had followed the sound of her son's complaining, gave him four. His phone rang in his pocket, and he glanced at it with a small, tired sigh.

"No," he said quietly into it. "Tell them the tire is flat. All of them. I'll be back when I'm back."

Tang Xiaoyu stood beside the toolbox, holding Zhou Guilan's heating pad against her chest. The lid was still open. Under a greasy rag she saw a heavy brass block with a small red star on its handle and a line of tiny characters ending in "People's Government."

"What's that?" she asked.

Old Wen closed the lid gently. "A stamp for bicycles. Every good bicycle gets one."

When the lane was empty a minute later, Xiaoyu was still looking at the spot. How could a man pushing a loaded bicycle disappear in the time it took to eat half an egg?

"A stamp for bicycles," she told Chen Yuan, very seriously.

"That must be a lot of bicycles," said Chen Yuan.

Same courtyard. Sunday, 12:30 p.m.

The lunch table was a big round one, and in the Chen family a seat was never only a seat. Zhou Guilan and Chen Fugen sat facing the door. Chen Jinhua sat at Grandmother's right as the eldest child and kept straightening her bracelet-less wrist. Chen Jianguo and Second Uncle sat close to the soup. The youngest sat nearest the kitchen, because the youngest carried the bowls.

Xiaoyu was carrying them now. She put down the soup, fixed the ladle, ran back for the rice, and ended up standing by the kitchen door with her hands folded. She had learned in the last few weeks that a girl who takes up no room is never sent away.

Chen Fugen put down his chopsticks.

"Xiaoyu," he said. His voice was low and rough from years of fishing wind. "Come here."

"I'll sit after, Grandfather. I'm helping."

"No." He pointed to the empty stool beside him, the one on his left. "You are a Chen now. At the table."

Nobody spoke. Even Chen Jinhua looked at her bowl.

Xiaoyu walked over slowly. She sat down on the very edge of the stool, with both hands in her lap. After a long moment, Liu Meifang slid a rib into her bowl without a word, and Xiaoyu picked up her chopsticks.

Sunday, 4:30 p.m.

The guests were leaving in clouds of dust and noise. Zhou Guilan sat on the bench by the gate with the heating pad against her back, and she patted the space beside her until Chen Xuan sat down.

"This is good," she said. "Warm."

"I'm glad, Grandma."

"You are a good boy." She looked at the lane and spoke very quietly, so quietly that even Chen Yuan, listening from the doorway, had to lean forward. "Xuan. When will you bring me a granddaughter-in-law?"

"Hmm?"

"Don't 'hmm' me." Zhou Guilan's eyes were small, bright, and completely serious. "I'm eighty-one. At my age At my age I don't wait for slow fruit."
