# Chapter 118: Director Wei Kun's Promotion

Inside the quiet study on the top floor of the Provincial Party Committee building, the faint smell of dark ink blended with green tea. Provincial Party Secretary Zhao Dehai sat behind his mahogany desk, reading a single crisp white page with steady attention.

Across the desk stood a young secretary holding a red lacquer folder, waiting quietly.

Zhao Dehai read the name at the top twice: Wei Kun, Acting Director of Old Street Police Station, Yunxi County. Below the name were two short paragraphs covering the past two years. Under former Director Cheng Biao, Wei Kun had refused every bribe envelope, kept clean shift logs, warned elderly shopkeepers when Yan Tiehu's thugs planned trouble, and protected Chen Mingde's hardware store during the darkest week before the clean-up began.

Zhao Dehai picked up his black fountain pen and signed his name cleanly across the bottom line. Then he reached for the square red provincial seal, pressed it onto the ink pad, and stamped the corner of the order.

"Send this to the Yunxi County Party Committee and the Provincial Public Security Department before noon," Zhao Dehai instructed, capping his pen. "Make sure the provincial clean-service award goes with the promotion order today, not next month. When a province clears out rotten officials, ordinary people must see honest officers rewarded right away."

"Yes, Secretary Zhao," the young secretary replied briskly, slipping the signed document into the red folder. "Shall we notify the local newspaper?"

"A short, plain notice is enough," Zhao Dehai said, leaning back slightly. "Wei Kun is not the kind of man who wants photographers chasing him down the street. Just let the people of Yunxi County know who runs Old Street Station now."

Two hours later, back in Yunxi County, the narrow courtyard of Old Street Police Station was washed clean by the afternoon breeze. Fresh paint gleamed on the front gate, and the blue-and-white station signboard looked bright above the entrance.

Inside the small duty room, Wei Kun stood beside his battered metal desk staring down at the heavy red-bordered certificate and the formal appointment letter resting next to his chipped enamel mug.

For a long moment, he said nothing. His broad shoulders, which had stayed tense for nearly two years under Cheng Biao, slowly relaxed. He picked up the letter, read the line promoting him from Acting Director to full Director of Old Street Police Station, and traced his rough thumb over the embossed red seal of Provincial Party Secretary Zhao Dehai.

A knock sounded on the open wooden door frame. Wei Kun looked up and saw Bao Haowen standing in the doorway, holding two paper cups of hot milk tea from the corner shop.

"Look at our new full Director," Bao Haowen said with a warm, crooked grin, stepping inside and setting one cup beside the enamel mug. "I brought tea before you get too important to sit with an old neighbor and beat officer."

Wei Kun let out a quiet, genuine laugh and shook his head. "Sit down, Bao Haowen. If I ever start acting important inside this two-room station, you have my permission to pour that whole cup over my head."

Bao Haowen pulled over a folding chair and sat across the desk. He glanced at the red-bordered clean-service award beside the appointment letter, and his grin softened into real respect.

"You earned every word on that paper, Wei Kun," Bao Haowen said sincerely. "People outside Old Street only saw the big arrests last week. They did not see the last two years. I still remember last winter when Cheng Biao ordered you to fine six stalls along the riverfront so Deng Yongchang's men could move in. You paid two hundred yuan out of your own pocket to cover Old Grandma Lin's stall fee and wrote a clean report instead."

"That was nothing," Wei Kun replied quietly, sitting back in his creaky chair. He peeled the lid off his cup and let the warm steam drift up between them. "A uniform means something, Bao Haowen. If you wear it just to look away when a bully kicks an old woman's vegetable basket, you might as well throw the badge in the river."

Bao Haowen took a slow sip of tea and nodded. "Still, it was not easy. Every time Cheng Biao drank with Liang Guodong, he threatened to transfer you out to the mountain checkpoint fifty kilometers away. Most men would have bent their backs just to stay close to their families."

"My wife asked me about it once," Wei Kun admitted softly, looking out the sunlit window at the street outside. Children were riding bicycles past Mingde Hardware while shopkeepers stacked fresh crates of oranges. "She asked why I kept arguing with Cheng Biao when nobody above us seemed to care. I told her that if every honest officer stepped aside, Old Street would have nobody left standing in the doorway."

He paused, his fingers tightening slightly around the warm cup.

"The truth is, Bao Haowen, two weeks ago I thought my time here was finished," Wei Kun said honestly. "The night Deng Yongchang's men went after Chen Mingde's shop, I went home thinking Cheng Biao would strip my uniform off by Monday. Then the whole sky flipped over in forty-eight hours."

"And look at the street now," Bao Haowen said quietly, following Wei Kun's gaze out the window. "Nobody crosses the road when they see a police car anymore. This morning, Auntie Tian from the dried-goods stall brought a bag of boiled eggs to the front desk just to thank the night shift."

Wei Kun smiled faintly. "Did the duty officer refuse them?"

"Of course he did," Bao Haowen laughed. "He followed your new rule to the letter. No free cigarettes, no free meals, not even a boiled egg. Though Auntie Tian looked ready to scold him for being too polite."

"Good," Wei Kun said firmly, setting his cup down. His voice grew steady and clear, carrying the quiet weight of a promise. "Keep that rule posted right behind the front desk. From today on, Old Street Station answers to the law and to the people who live on this street. Not to rich developers, not to county big shots, and never to street thugs."

Bao Haowen straightened in his chair with quiet pride. "What is our first order of business on Monday morning, Director Wei?"

"First, we walk the whole length of Old Street on foot," Wei Kun said simply. "Every doorway, from Mingde Hardware down to the noodle shop at the bridge. We hand out the new station phone number, help the shopkeepers gather their repair receipts for the county repayment office, and let every family know that if anyone ever tries to bully Old Street again, they will have to walk through us first."

"Count me in," Bao Haowen said warmly, raising his cup of milk tea. "To Old Street, and to never letting bullies set foot here again."

Wei Kun lifted his cup and tapped it lightly against Bao Haowen's. Outside the open window, the afternoon breeze carried cheerful voices and bicycle bells down a street that finally belonged to its own people again.
