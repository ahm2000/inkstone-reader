# Chapter 5: Home

Back in Room 601, Liu Meifang opened the door, saw her husband's left cheek, and dropped the ladle.

"Who did that?"

"Nobody," Chen Jianguo muttered. "I walked into a door."

"Brother Hu of Hongrui slapped him," Chen Xuan said. "In front of the whole street."

"I will kill him!"

She snatched the ladle off the floor and marched toward the door, and it took both her children to hold her back.

"Mom, he is twice your size."

"Then I need a bigger ladle!"

Chen Hao laughed, and Wu Lanying laughed through her tears, and the room was a little lighter.

Chen Xuan filled a basin with cold water.

He wrung out a towel, sat his father down in the good chair, and held it against the swollen cheek himself.

"Sorry, son," Chen Jianguo whispered. "You shouldn't have seen your old father like that."

"Dad. You held the brush. I saw that."

His father turned his face to the wall.

"Well?" Chen Yuan said from the dining table.

"You said ten minutes. It's been two hours. Dad got slapped, the station turned you away, and what did you bring back? A thermos."

"The station said tomorrow."

"Tomorrow! You leave work at five o'clock. You have never won an argument in your life. You say 'mm' and eat."

"Yuan, enough," Liu Meifang said, but she did not sound angry.

She sounded frightened.

Chen Yuan's voice cracked.

"Second Uncle is in a cell, and the only man in the family with a city job says, 'Hmm.'"

Chen Xuan folded the towel to a cooler side and set it back on his father's cheek.

"Hmm," he said. "You're right, Yuan."

"Don't agree with me! That's worse!"

"He is trying," Wu Lanying said gently, wiping her face. "Let him eat."

Liu Meifang ladled two bowls and set them down with a clack.

"Two bowls. I am counting."

Chen Xuan ate the first one slowly, because his mother was watching.

"Best ribs in the county, Mom," he said, and he pushed the pot toward his aunt. "Auntie, have some too."

"Tomorrow morning I am going to the station," he said between spoonfuls.

"To do what?" Chen Yuan cried. "Shout?"

"Sit."

Behind the math book, Chen Hao leaned toward his sister.

"Has he ever won a fight?"

"Only with Mom, and only about bedtime."

Chen Xuan drank his soup.

Then he looked at the red mark on his father's cheek.

"Dad, what time did he hit you?"

"Ten past eight. Why?"

"Mm. Just remembering," said Chen Xuan, so mildly that Chen Yuan rolled her eyes.

Then three slow knocks sounded on the door.

Nobody moved.

Liu Meifang's hand went to the ladle, and Wu Lanying's glass trembled against her teeth.

Chen Xuan wiped his hands, opened the door, and looked out at the dark stairwell.

The bulb was out, and the stairs were empty.

On the doormat lay a wet red paintbrush, and a strip of red paper was taped to the door with one character on it: *chai*.

"Three knocks," Chen Xuan said into the dark. "I counted."

He dropped the brush into the trash bin by the shoe rack, peeled the paper off, and closed the door.

Chen Yuan stared at him with her mouth half open, and for once she had no line at all.

* * *

In the holding room, the bench was concrete, and the cold came up through it like water through a cloth.

Chen Mingde sat with his jacket folded under him and his hands tucked into his armpits.

Above him, a single tube light buzzed.

At eleven, Lei Bo had come by with a contract and a pen.

"Sign, Old Chen. Go home, take a hot shower. Why suffer?" He tapped the pen on the bars. "Your girl graduates next summer, right? A father with a record is a bad line on a young woman's file. Sign."

"Please take it away," Chen Mingde had said, in the same voice he used for customers who wanted a discount.

His heart had hammered so hard that he thought the whole cell must hear it, and cold sweat had soaked his collar.

But he did not sign.

He thought of his daughter in her dorm. She would never know any of this unless he told her.

Now there were footsteps, and a shadow fell across the bars.

It was the duty officer, the thin one with tired eyes.

He glanced at the corridor, then poured hot water from a steel thermos into a paper cup and pushed it through the bars with a hand that shook.

"Drink it slowly," Wei Kun said. "It's only water."

"Thank you, Officer."

Chen Mingde took the cup in both hands, like a candle in a window.

"Will the paper come tomorrow?"

"I don't know." Wei Kun looked at his own shoes. "I am sorry. I do not know."

"Don't be sorry. You're shaking. Are you cold or scared?"

Wei Kun gave a short laugh that was almost a sob.

"Both, Uncle."

"I thought so. You wear a ring, and you keep touching your phone. Someone is waiting for your call."

"My wife. Seven months pregnant."

"Then don't get into trouble for me."

"Uncle, I."

"Listen." Chen Mingde sipped the water, and the heat crept into his chest. "When my nephew was seven, I took him fishing. A fish pulled, he pulled back, and the line snapped. I told him, 'Wait. Let the fish tire itself out.' I am only a hardware man, Officer. But waiting is the one thing I am good at."

Wei Kun's eyes stung.

"If you can," Chen Mingde said softly, "tell my wife I ate. She won't believe it, but tell her."

At the end of the corridor, a door creaked.

Wei Kun jumped, but no one came.

He nodded once to the old man, then walked to the duty desk and sat down in front of the blank box on the form.

For twelve years, he had written whatever the Director wanted.

Tonight, for the first time, he knew exactly what he would not write.

* * *

In a quiet walled courtyard in the east of Qingyan City, a great map glowed on the wall of Chief An's study. Two hundred and fourteen green dots shone across it.

One dot, in Yunxi County, had turned yellow and was blinking.

"Old Street Station, Chief," said the duty captain on the speaker. "Our records clerk. His note says, 'Awaiting order.'"

"Read me what happened."

The captain read it.

Chief An listened to every line, and his tea went cold.

"His father was slapped."

"Yes, Chief. By a man named Hu. We have the face and the license plate."

"And the Boss?"

"Number Nine reports he held a wet towel to his father's cheek and ate two bowls of soup. Someone knocked three times and left a paintbrush on his mother's doormat. He threw it in the bin and went to bed. He was asleep in eight minutes. He snores a little."

For a moment, Chief An could not speak.

Then he laughed, a short sound with a crack in it.

"His grandfather's boy. Cold as a stone, and soft as bread."

"Shall we move, Chief?"

"The Boss gave his first order tonight. Record everything on Cheng Biao and Hongrui, and touch nothing yet. He has told us only to record."

"Chief, there are two hundred and fourteen of us, and we are standing around a slapped old man."

"Then we record." Chief An's voice went flat. "Write down the time of every insult. Every one, with the name. I will not forget a single line."

He stared at the yellow dot.

"And if the uncle's heart gives out tonight?"

"Dr. Shen Wei, a heart specialist, swapped into the night shift at People's Hospital. He says it was chance." The captain coughed. "Head Nurse Qiao has the uncle's pill record on her desk."

"The law?"

"Attorney Ye Tong is at the tea stall on Old Street with a briefcase and one cup of tea he's made last two hours. The Detail's law firm keeps a partner in every county seat."

"And a roof?"

"Apartment 302, Building 5, Willow Lane. Bed made, rice in the jar, key under the geranium pot. The Boss has never heard of it."

"Of course not." Chief An shook his head. "Wherever he goes, a nurse, a lawyer and a made bed arrive first, and he thinks the buses are just on time."

"What does the Boss plan for tomorrow?"

"Eight-thirty, Chief. He will go to the station's front hall and sit on the bench."

"Sit? Like a customer at a bank?"

"Yes, Chief."

Chief An put both hands over his face, and when he took them away he was not smiling.

"He has told us only to record, Captain. When he says more, who in this county will still be standing?"
