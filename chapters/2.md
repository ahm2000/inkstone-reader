# Chapter 2: Old Street

In Building 2 of Fuxing New Village, Liu Meifang's voice reached the stairwell long before the rest of her did.

"Is that him? Jianguo, is that our son? Look how thin he is! Does that company not feed him?"

Chen Xuan climbed the last flight with his green thermos in one hand and his father puffing behind him.

At the sixth floor the door flew open, and his mother pulled him inside by the sleeve as if he had been gone ten years.

"Hi, Mom. It smells great already."

"Sit, sit! The ribs have been on since five o'clock!"

On the sofa, Wu Lanying stood up with a glass of cold water in both hands.

Her cardigan was buttoned wrong, and her eyes were swollen.

"Xuan. Your uncle never hit anyone in his whole life."

"I know, Second Aunt."

He took the glass, put it on the table, and guided her back down by the elbows.

"Sit, Auntie. I'm here now."

It was a small thing, but her shoulders came down an inch.

"Big brother," Chen Hao called from the dining table, one earbud still in his ear, "you have a pillow line on your cheek. Did you sleep through the whole crisis?"

"On the bus. Mm."

"The family is on fire," Chen Yuan said, folding her arms, "and he arrives with a thermos."

She taught junior high, and she used that exact tone on bad essays.

"It's still hot," Chen Xuan said, and took a sip.

"Don't pick on your brother!" Liu Meifang snapped. "He came all that way on a bus!"

"Mom, he sleeps on buses. He sleeps in meetings. At New Year he slept through the fireworks."

"Were there fireworks?" Chen Xuan asked.

Chen Hao snorted so hard his earbud fell out.

"Oh, and Wang Lei from your class came into the shop Monday," he said, picking it up. "Bought one hinge, counted out the coins. He drives a delivery tricycle now."

"His father is in the hospital," Liu Meifang said, sighing. "That poor boy."

Chen Xuan said nothing.

In senior year, his meal card ran empty. Wang Lei slid eight yuan into his hand every day for a month. That was 240 yuan, and he never mentioned it once.

Chen Xuan remembered every coin. A small, warm ache sat under his ribs.

Chen Jianguo hung his jacket on the hook and lowered himself into his chair.

"Enough, you two. A slow ox still reaches the field."

"Dad," Chen Yuan said, "an ox that naps in the road ends up at the butcher."

"Yuan!"

"What? It's a proverb too."

Chen Xuan put the cap back on his thermos.

"Dad, before the soup, I want to see the shop."

"Now?" Liu Meifang cried. "The ribs!"

"Ten minutes, Mom. Then I eat two bowls."

His mother looked at him, then at the pot, then at her sister-in-law on the sofa.

Her chin trembled, and she turned the flame down low.

"Two bowls. And I will count."

"Thanks, Mom."

Wu Lanying pulled a small brass key on a red thread from her pocket. She pressed it into his palm with both hands.

"The lock doesn't close right anymore. They broke it Thursday. Mingde told me the cat did it."

"Thanks, Second Aunt." Chen Xuan closed his fingers over the key. He held it tighter than anyone noticed.

"Ten bucks he naps on the doorstep," Chen Hao whispered.

"Twenty," said Chen Yuan.

* * *

Old Street ran two hundred meters from a stone arch to the river, and tonight half of it was dark.

On every dark wall, someone had painted a red circle with one big character inside: *chai*, which means "tear down."

A circle went up only after the owner signed.

Eleven shops wore one, and Chen Jianguo walked past every one of them with his eyes on the ground.

Mingde Hardware, on the corner, did not wear it yet, and its shutter hung crooked from one bolt.

Inside, the glass counter lay in pieces.

Every little drawer on the back wall had been pulled out and emptied, and hundreds of screws lay across the floor like spilled rice.

"Thursday afternoon," Chen Jianguo said hoarsely. "Eight men with sticks. They took nothing. They only broke. Your uncle swept for three hours and told your aunt the cat did it."

Chen Xuan crouched down and began to pick up screws.

He dropped them into the drawers one by one, reading the labels out loud, softly, the way he had at seven years old.

"M4. M5. Flat head. Brass."

His father stood in the doorway and looked away, because a man can't cry in front of his son.

Then headlights swept across the wall, and a black van stopped at the curb.

Five men climbed out, and the one in front carried a bucket of red paint.

Along the lane, shutters creaked up a finger's width.

"Brother Hu," somebody breathed.

Brother Hu led Hongrui's "security team." That's a polite name for eight men who collect debts, push out stubborn people and break things for money.

He had a shaved head, a neck as wide as his skull, and a towel over one shoulder.

Everyone on Old Street knew his name, and nobody said it louder than a whisper.

"Look at that," he said, grinning at the open shutter. "The family came to clean up for us. How thoughtful."

He dipped a brush in the bucket and walked to the corner wall.

"You can't paint there!" Chen Jianguo burst out, and he strode forward. "My brother has not signed! This is his wall!"

"Old man, by Monday this wall is gravel."

Chen Jianguo grabbed the brush handle.

Smack!

The sound rang along the whole lane.

Chen Jianguo's glasses flew off, and he hit the shutter shoulder first and slid down it.

On his left cheek, a red hand rose slowly, five fingers wide.

Chen Jianguo had never been slapped in the street before.

Shame burned hotter than the cheek. He could not lift his eyes.

"He hit an old man," Meng Xiaofang whispered from her bun stall, her hand over her mouth.

"Shh! Not so loud!"

At the tailor's door, Old Yu clenched his fists and did not move.

A delivery rider across the lane gripped his handlebars until his knuckles went pale, and he did not move either.

Chen Xuan stood up.

He did not run, and he did not raise a hand.

He picked up his father's glasses, wiped them on his sleeve, and slid them gently back onto his father's nose.

"Dad, sit down."

Then he turned, still holding half a red-bean bun that he had taken from his bag on the way.

"Hit him again," he said, "and I will remember the hand."

He took a bite of the bun.

Brother Hu blinked, and the lane went so quiet that the van's engine ticked as it cooled.

Then he roared with laughter, and his four men laughed with him.

"Boys, he will remember the hand! And who are you?"

"Chen Xuan. The owner's nephew."

"What do you do, Nephew?"

"Operations supervisor. Logistics."

"An office clerk!" Brother Hu slapped his own thigh. "Hey, Thin Monkey, the clerk came with his lunch!"

"Mm," said Chen Xuan. "Dinner, actually."

Thin Monkey cackled, and Big Tooth pretended to wipe a tear.

"Clerk, let me teach you how Old Street works," Brother Hu said, stepping close and tapping the bun with a finger as thick as a sausage. "Your people are in Fuxing New Village, Building 2, sixth floor. We know every door in this county. Doors are easy to knock on at night."

"Knock on that door," Chen Xuan said, "and I will count the knocks."

He said it flatly, as if reading a bus timetable.

He did not flinch.

He only looked up.

For one blink, something behind his eyes went still and cold, like a pond in January.

Brother Hu's finger stopped on the bun. He didn't know why the back of his neck prickled.

He swallowed. Then he laughed louder, to cover it, and wiped the hand on his trousers.

"Go home, clerk. Eat your bun."

He slapped a wet red circle onto the corner wall, and the character dripped down the bricks.

At the van door he turned and shouted, so that the whole lane could hear.

"Want to know who sent me? Go ask Cheng Biao at the station! He's my uncle there!"

The van roared away.

Chen Jianguo's whole body shook as he got to his feet.

"Xuan, you stood there. You just stood there and ate a bun."

"Mm," said Chen Xuan, looking at the red circle. "Dad, which way is the police station?"
