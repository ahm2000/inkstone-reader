# Chapter 35: The Fourth Chair

Liu Meifang's message came at half past eight, and it was short.

*I'm at your uncle's with Second Aunt. There is nothing at home. Take your father and Hao and get noodles. Chen Xuan, no fried food.*

"No fried food," Chen Jianguo read over his son's shoulder. "She sends it to you, not to me. Why is that?"

"Because you'd eat it anyway, Dad," Chen Hao said.

They locked the shop, and the three of them walked up Old Street under the new red banner. The street was quiet. Most shops had closed early to rest after the long day.

One window was still lit at the end of the lane. Gong's Noodles had four tables, a steaming pot and a hand-painted sign. The owner, a big man with a thick neck and a towel on his shoulder, was wiping the counter. He was about to close.

Chen Xuan had walked past that window for three years, and he had never gone in. He could not have said why. Something about the quiet of the place had always made him think, *Next time.*

"Here," Chen Jianguo said, and pushed the door. "Sixteen yuan a bowl. A man can eat honestly here."

The owner looked up.

For one second, he did not move. The towel stayed on his shoulder. Then he nodded, once, like a man greeting a customer.

"Sit anywhere."

Chen Jianguo took the chair by the door, to watch the street. Chen Hao dropped into the seat beside him. That left one chair at the window table, with its back to the warm wall.

Chen Xuan sat down in it. It was a plain wooden chair with a flat, clean cushion.

"Three beef noodles," Chen Jianguo said. "Big bowls. And one with extra, for the boy. He's thin."

"Dad."

"You are thin. Don't argue."

The owner said nothing and went into the kitchen.

He was Number Twenty-three of the Black Pine Guard, and he was holding the ladle too tightly.

*Three years,* he thought. *Three years of bowls at midnight. I threw out a thousand of them. Every one went cold in this chair.*

He ladled broth into the first bowl. His hands were perfectly steady, which was strange, because his heart was not.

*Number Sixty-eight said I was foolish. Number One said it was a habit, not a duty. But I promised. When he sits, the bowl will be hot.*

He set a pinch of green onion on top and carried the bowl out with both hands.

He put it in front of Chen Xuan, and steam rose between them.

Chen Xuan picked up his chopsticks. He lifted the first mouthful and blew on it. The broth tasted of beef bones, pepper and something warm and old, like a kitchen from a long time ago.

"Oh, nice," he said. His eyes went a little wet, and he did not know why. "Boss Gong. Thanks. This is really good."

Number Twenty-three turned away fast. He pretended to look for a clean spoon.

In three years behind that counter, nobody had called him that and meant it. He had never wanted a title so much. And he could not say one word.

*It is hot,* he thought. *It is exactly hot. It is the best bowl I have ever made.*

* * *

They were halfway through the noodles when Chen Hao looked out the window.

"Dad. There's a kid outside."

She stood by the lamp post, a thin girl of about thirteen, in a jacket far too big for her. The sleeves hung past her hands. She was holding a plastic bag against her chest and looking at the bowls through the glass.

When Chen Jianguo waved her in, she took a step back. Then she came in, stiff as a wooden doll.

"I'm waiting for my dad," she said, before anyone had asked. "He works at Tiancheng. On Dongqiao Road, in the yard. He's coming to pick me up. In ten minutes."

She said it very fast, like a lesson she had learned by heart.

Chen Xuan looked at her. He saw the wet socks above her sneakers, the dry lips and the broken thumbnail. He also saw the way her eyes had gone to the soup before she said a word.

He did not say any of it.

He stood, picked up the teapot from the empty table and poured a cup. He pulled out the empty chair across from his own. It was the one with its back to the room, so nobody would stare at her.

"It's cold out there. Sit. The tea is free."

"I'm fine. I already ate."

Her stomach growled so loudly that Chen Hao coughed into his sleeve to hide a laugh.

The girl turned red up to her ears. She stood very straight.

"I can wash dishes," she said. "For a bowl. I'm good at it. I don't need charity."

The cook had stopped in the kitchen doorway with his towel. He looked at Chen Xuan like a man waiting for an order he was not allowed to ask for.

Chen Xuan did not look back at him. "Sit down first," he said to the girl. "Then wash dishes if you want. A person should sit before she works."

After a long second, she sat. Both hands stayed wrapped around her plastic bag, as if it held something that could break.

The bag had slipped open at the top. Inside lay a school workbook and a man's work glove, rough brown leather, stained with grease. It was three sizes too big for her.

She saw him look at it, and pulled the bag shut.

"What's your name?" Chen Hao asked, with his mouth full.

"Tang Xiaoyu."

"Tang Xiaoyu. I'm Chen Hao. This is my dad. That's my brother."

"Hello." She did not look up. Number Twenty-three put a big bowl in front of her. It had extra beef, though nobody had asked.

She stared at it. Her hand shook as she lifted the chopsticks. After the first bite, she did not stop until the bowl was empty, and not once did she look at anyone.

Then she stood up, took her bowl and went straight to the sink behind the counter. She washed it with both hands, scrubbing the same spot twice, as if there were a debt in the porcelain.

Number Twenty-three stood at the end of the counter, drying a ladle. Behind her back, his thumb moved on the phone in his apron pocket.

*Number One. The Boss sat in the fourth chair. The bowl was hot. A girl, thirteen, named Tang Xiaoyu. Says her father works at Tiancheng, in the yard on Dongqiao Road. She is lying. The Boss let her. He did not ask her anything.*

The answer came in seconds. *Hold. Do nothing until he speaks.*

Across the street, in a dark doorway, a figure in a gray cap leaned on the wall with both hands in his pockets. He had been there since eight. He had not looked at the noodle shop once, and he had not looked away from it either.

Number Twenty-three did not look at him. He had learned long ago that the gray cap was not his.

The girl set her clean bowl on the rack. She came back to the table, wiping her hands on her jacket.

She stood by the window and looked at the cook, then at the chair Chen Xuan sat in, then at Chen Xuan.

"Mister," she said slowly. "Is this your shop? The cook looked at you before he put the bowl down. Like he was waiting for you to say it's okay."

Chen Jianguo and Chen Hao both turned to stare at him.

Chen Xuan laughed out loud, and for a moment he looked as young as the boy he had been.

"I wish," he said. "I only work in an office."
