# Chapter 8: The Cold Bench

In the public lobby of Old Street Police Station, the ticket machine hummed and spat out a slip of paper.

Thirty-seven.

Chen Jianguo read the number twice and then looked up at the red screen above the Case Questions window.

Twelve.

"Thirty-seven?" he groaned. "Xuan, take a number! By the time they call that, your uncle will have a beard down to his belt!"

"No, thanks," said Chen Xuan.

He walked past the machine to the window, where Wei Kun sat behind the glass.

"Morning. The detention notice for Chen Mingde. In writing."

"Take a number," Wei Kun said stiffly. "Wait your turn."

"I'll sit, thanks."

Chen Xuan took the steel bench, put his scratched green thermos on his knee, and unwrapped a red-bean bun his mother had packed.

He looked as peaceful as a man waiting for a bus.

Next to him, a delivery rider with a headset on turned around and stared.

He glanced at Chen Jianguo's left cheek, which had gone a yellow-purple color overnight.

"Brother, your family is in trouble, right? Your father looks it. You don't."

"Mm," said Chen Xuan, and took a bite.

"Don't you want to shout? Bang the glass? Everybody does."

"Does it work?"

"No."

"Then I'll eat. They will bring it to me."

The rider shook his head, sorry for him.

On the wall hung a sign that said Legal Aid Desk, Open Monday to Friday. A paper arrow below it pointed to an empty table with a dusty chair.

Chen Jianguo's phone buzzed. It was his wife, and her voice filled a room anyway, so he put her on speaker.

"Jianguo! Have you seen him? Did they let you in?"

"We are in the lobby, Meifang. Xuan is sitting."

"Sitting?" Liu Meifang's voice rose so high that two people on the bench jumped. "Your brother has slept on concrete for a night, and he is sitting? Where is Xuan? Put him on!"

"Mom," Chen Xuan said.

"Are you sitting? Like at the dumpling house, with a bun?"

"Mm."

"I told your father to shout! Ten shouts! Why do I have one son who never shouts and one who sleeps through floods?"

"Tell him if he falls asleep on that bench, I'm disowning him!" Chen Yuan's voice cried in the background.

"Eat the bun I packed for you!" Liu Meifang added, and hung up.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"I'm eating it," Chen Xuan told the phone.

The old woman with a sack of onions on her knees leaned toward the rider.

"Rich families stay calm," she said, not quietly enough. "The rest of us shout."

"Rich? In those shoes?"

"Then lazy," said the old woman. "Look at the father. He is the only one here who looks angry. It is good for a father to be angry."

Chen Jianguo sat up, flattered. Then he saw she was praising him by insulting his son.

"Lazy is right," he muttered.

"Mm," said Chen Xuan. He offered the old woman half his bun, which she refused with great dignity, and took another bite.

* * *

Across the lane from the station, Old Yu was ironing a pair of trousers that were already flat.

A shadow filled his doorway.

"Uncle Yu."

The man was broad as a door, with front teeth that stuck out and a Hongrui security-team jacket.

The street called him Big Tooth, and he worked for Brother Hu.

"I'm not open," Old Yu said, though his sign said otherwise.

"I came to say good morning. Brother Hu likes his neighbors well-fed." Big Tooth set a thick brown envelope on the cutting table. "Twenty thousand. A little something for your granddaughter's school."

Twenty thousand yuan was four months of Old Yu's pay.

"I did nothing for it."

"Right. That's exactly what it is for. You did nothing. You saw nothing. Old eyes, old street, too much dust."

He dragged a stool across the floor with a long scrape and sat down, in no hurry.

Then he smiled, and patted the head of a little girl who sat at the end of the table with a coloring book.

Nine-year-old Ning had come over to do her homework on Saturdays, because her mother worked weekends.

"Pretty," said Big Tooth. "Walks home from school alone through the lane, hm?"

The iron stopped.

"Be careful with the stairs by the river," said Big Tooth kindly. "They're slippery. Little ones fall in every year, and the river doesn't give them back."

He left.

Old Yu stood without moving until the sound of boots faded.

His hands shook so badly that the steam from the iron wobbled.

"Grandpa, you look sick," Ning said.

"The iron is hot, little one."

He picked up the envelope as if it might bite, dropped it into the drawer under the thread, and closed it.

He had an eyewitness statement in his head and a grandchild in front of him.

Which one was heavier?

Across the lane, Meng Xiaofang peeked out from her steamed-bun stall, and their eyes met.

Neither spoke.

* * *

The screen chimed at last. Thirty-seven.

It had taken an hour and twenty minutes, and twenty-five numbers.

Chen Jianguo had checked his watch eleven times.

Chen Xuan had checked it zero times, and he had finished the bun.

Chen Xuan stood and walked to the window, and Wei Kun looked up from the form on his desk.

One of his cheeks was a little redder than the other, and Chen Xuan saw it.

"The charge against my uncle," said Chen Xuan. "And the notice."

"Assault," Wei Kun said stiffly. "Under review. No visits."

"Officer Wei, your cheek is red."

Wei Kun's ears went red too.

"I walked into a door."

"Mm. Hard door."

For a moment, something moved in Wei Kun's eyes, as if he had braced for a blow and been given kindness instead.

"We will mail the family notice," he said quickly. "You are holding up the line."

"By law it reaches us within twenty-four hours. It's been almost twenty."

"I said we'll mail it."

"Then I'll sit."

Chen Xuan went back to the steel bench, and Wei Kun reached for the desk phone with a damp hand.

"Director Cheng? The nephew is here. He is still sitting."

Ten minutes later, the steel door at the back of the lobby clicked, and everyone stood up.

The rider stood, and the onion woman half stood, because her sack was heavy.

Lei Bo, at the guidance desk, jumped up so fast that he knocked over his tea.

Zhou Pingan, the thin young auxiliary by the door, stood very straight and kept his eyes on the floor.

Behind the glass, Wei Kun rose with his hands at his sides, and at the next desk Zhong Hai rose too, with his eyes on the floor.

Even the old records clerk, Old Ding, got up from his stool in the corner, slow and stooped, with a small recorder running in his sleeve, and did not look at anybody.

Cheng Biao strolled out with a toothpick in his mouth.

"That's Director Cheng," someone whispered. "The boy is finished."

Cheng Biao looked at Chen Jianguo and did not see him.

His eyes went over Chen Xuan, from plain sneakers to neat hair, and stayed there, thinly amused.

"Back again, logistics clerk?"

"Director Cheng. The notice," said Chen Xuan, screwing the cap on his thermos. "Now."

"Give them their paper and clear my lobby, Wei. And tell them the visiting room is closed this weekend. Pipe repairs." Cheng Biao leaned toward Chen Jianguo's bruised cheek. "Take your father home, boy. Old men fall down stairs."

Chen Jianguo's fists closed, and Chen Xuan put a hand on his arm.

"The legal aid desk is empty," Chen Xuan said.

"What?" Cheng Biao's lip curled. "Want a lawyer? Then find a good one. A man who knows this county charges five thousand yuan an hour. That's a month of your pay, clerk, for one hour."

He did not wait for an answer.

"Director," Chen Xuan said to his back, in a flat voice. "I hope you sleep well tonight. It's the last good one you'll have."

Cheng Biao took it for a joke, and he laughed once without turning.

The steel door clanged behind him.

Inside the corridor, the toothpick snapped between his teeth, and his smile fell off.

A clerk this calm, who asks about lawyers when he has none, is no ordinary clerk.

Who had taught him to ask?

Cheng Biao would find out before Monday.

Through the slot beneath the glass, one stamped sheet slid out at last.

Wei Kun stared at its final line, and forgot to breathe.

Would the nephew read that far?
