# Chapter 8: Wait Your Turn

Yunxi County, Old Street Police Station lobby. Saturday, 8:30 a.m.

The public waiting hall was gray, drafty, and crowded with early errands.

Overhead, blue plastic signs marked the service windows: Family Records, ID Cards, and Case Questions. An old woman sat with a cloth sack of onions balanced on her knees, waiting to fix her family booklet. A young delivery rider paced back and forth arguing into his headset, while a little girl kept sliding down the steel bench despite her grandfather's scolding.

"Dad, hold this." Chen Xuan pulled a thin white paper slip from the ticket machine by the glass doors.

Chen Jianguo squinted at the printed digits. "Thirty-seven."

Above the Case Inquiries window, the red LED screen showed the number currently being served: twelve.

"Thirty-seven?!" Chen Jianguo groaned as if reading his own sentence. "Xuan, by the time they call thirty-seven, your uncle will have a beard down to his waist!"

"Then we wait," Chen Xuan said easily, sitting down on the steel bench with his scratched green thermos resting on his knee.

Behind the thick service glass, Wei Kun hadn't slept a wink.

He'd spent the whole night sitting in the duty room with his boots still laced. Every time he shut his eyes, he saw a thin, gray-haired shopkeeper holding a paper cup with both hands. At 7:40 this morning, Director Cheng Biao had stepped into the duty room, his face tight after last night's dinner. "The nephew is showing up today," Cheng Biao had ordered flatly. "Finish the detention decision paper. Write Friday, 2:30 p.m., right after the arrest."

Wei Kun had picked up his black gel pen. Then he'd set it back down.

It was only a date and a time. Yet writing it meant signing a lie on purpose in his own handwriting on an official paper a man's family would hold in their hands. He thought of his pregnant wife at home, who asked him every single night when he walked through the door, "Was it a good day?"

So Wei Kun wrote the true time: Saturday, 7:50 a.m. It was the single honest line on the entire sheet. When Cheng Biao frowned at it, Wei Kun stammered that he'd slipped up and would reprint it right away. He never reprinted it.

It was the smallest rebellion of his twelve-year career, and he still didn't know why his palms were sweating!

At 9:50, the red screen chimed and flashed thirty-seven.

The service counter had the old style of glass barrier with a small speaking grille and a narrow slot at the bottom. Wei Kun looked up from a half-eaten sesame bun and saw a young man with a very mild, calm face.

"Good morning, Officer," Chen Xuan said politely. "My name is Chen Xuan. My uncle, Chen Mingde, was brought in yesterday at two in the afternoon. We'd like to know the exact charge and when his family can visit him."

"Chen Mingde." Wei Kun's fingers tapped across his keyboard while his voice sounded far away. "Assault and hurting another person. The case is still being checked. He is being held in jail. No visits allowed."

"For how long?"

"Ten days, maybe fifteen. Depends on his attitude."

"May we have the written holding notice, please?"

Wei Kun stopped chewing!

In twelve years behind this window, he'd faced screaming families, weeping mothers, and panicked relatives who tried to kneel on the tile floor. He'd never once seen a young man ask for an official police notice as calmly as someone asking for a bank receipt! Worse, his heart jumped straight into his throat because he knew exactly what was written on that sheet!

"Who are you, a lawyer?" Wei Kun forced himself to ask stiffly.

"No, Officer. I'm an operations supervisor at a logistics company. I check forms every day for work."

Someone waiting in the line behind Chen Xuan let out a sudden snort of laughter!

Wei Kun's ears flushed bright red. An office clerk, in front of a crowded lobby, lecturing a veteran officer about paperwork? Yet underneath his embarrassment crept a sharp, cold fear: what if this guy really knew how to read forms? What if he spotted the timestamp right away?

"Sir, you're holding up the queue," Wei Kun said coldly. "We're busy. We'll mail the family notice."

"By police rules, the station has to tell the family in writing within twenty-four hours, Officer," Chen Xuan replied gently, never raising his voice. "It's been nearly twenty hours already. Could we please take our copy now so we don't have to trouble you again?"

"I told you, we'll mail it!"

"Then we'll sit right here and wait until you have a moment."

Chen Xuan turned around and sat calmly back down on the steel bench.

He didn't shout, didn't bang the glass, and didn't leave. He simply sat beside his fuming father with his green thermos on his knee, looking completely content to wait all morning, leaving Wei Kun utterly stuck!

"Tch!" Wei Kun grabbed the desk phone and dialed the inner office. "Director Cheng? It's Wei. The nephew is here, and he won't leave without the written notice. Yes, Director."

Ten minutes later, a heavy steel door at the rear of the lobby clicked open.

A broad-shouldered man in a black leather jacket strode out with a wooden toothpick tucked in the corner of his mouth. The moment he stepped into the hall, the chatter dropped to a nervous hush. Even the little girl stopped sliding off the bench.

Cheng Biao didn't spare a glance for Chen Jianguo. His eyes swept slowly over Chen Xuan from his plain sneakers to his neat hair, sizing him up with thinly veiled contempt.

"So you're the logistics clerk," Cheng Biao said coolly.

"Good morning, Director Cheng."

"Hand them the family notice and clear my lobby, Wei." Cheng Biao plucked the toothpick from his lips. "And inform them the detention visiting room is closed this weekend. For pipe repairs."

"Director Cheng," Chen Xuan asked politely, "is the lawyer reception window open on Saturdays?"

Cheng Biao's lip curled in a faint, mocking smile. "Have you hired a lawyer?"

"Not yet."

"Then I suggest you find a very good one."

He turned sharply on his heel and walked back through the heavy steel door, letting it clang shut behind him.

A moment later, a single stamped sheet of white paper slid quietly out through the slot beneath the glass.
