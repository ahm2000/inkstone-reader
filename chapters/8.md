# Chapter 8: The Cold Bench

In the lobby of Old Street Police Station, the ticket machine gave out a slip of paper.

Thirty-seven.

Chen Xuan's father, Chen Jianguo, read the number twice. Then he looked up at the red screen above the Case Questions window.

Twelve.

"Thirty-seven?" he said. "By then your uncle will have a long white beard!"

He walked fast to the window. Wei Kun sat behind the glass.

"We want the detention notice for my brother, Chen Mingde. In writing!"

"Take a number. Wait your turn." Wei Kun did not look up.

Chen Jianguo came back angry. "Xuan, go and sit. I'll sit and look angry."

"Okay, Dad." Chen Xuan's voice was small. "Does your cheek hurt?"

"It's nothing."

It was not nothing. The cheek had turned yellow and purple overnight, and Chen Xuan could not stop looking at it.

He sat on the steel bench with his green thermos on his knee and unwrapped the red-bean bun his mother had packed. His hands were not steady.

Next to him, a delivery rider with a headset turned around and stared.

"Brother, your father looks like trouble. You look like you're waiting for a bus."

"I'm scared." Chen Xuan rubbed his eyes with his sleeve. "My uncle slept in a cell last night, and I don't know what to do."

"Don't you want to shout? Everybody does."

"Does it work?"

"No."

"Then I'll sit and eat, so my hands have something to do."

On the wall hung a sign that said Legal Aid Desk. A paper arrow pointed to an empty table with a dusty chair.

Chen Jianguo's phone buzzed. It was his wife, Liu Meifang. Her voice filled a room anyway, so he put her on speaker.

"Jianguo! Have you seen him? Did they let you in?"

"We're in the lobby, Meifang. Xuan is sitting."

"Sitting?" Liu Meifang's voice went so high that two people on the bench jumped. "Your brother slept on concrete last night, and he is sitting? Put Xuan on!"

"Mom." Chen Xuan's voice cracked. "They won't let us in. Is Second Aunt okay? Did she eat?"

For one breath, Liu Meifang said nothing.

"She drank some water." Her voice was smaller. "You boy, are you eating?"

"Yes. I'm sorry, Mom. I don't know what else to do."

"I told your father to shout! Ten shouts! Why do I have one son who never shouts and one who sleeps through everything?"

"Eat the bun I packed for you!" Liu Meifang added, and hung up.

Chen Xuan sniffed and told the silent phone, "Yes, Mom."

An old woman with a sack of onions on her knees leaned toward the rider.

"Why is the son so calm? Lazy, I say." She did not say it quietly. She pointed at Chen Jianguo. "Look at the father. He's the only one here who looks angry. It's good for a father to be angry."

Chen Jianguo sat up, proud. Then he saw she was praising him by insulting his son.

He muttered, "Lazy is right."

"I'm not lazy, Auntie. I'm just hungry and scared." He held out half his bun. She said no, proudly, and he gave a wet little laugh and took another bite.

* * *

Across the lane from the station, the old tailor Yu Changshun was ironing a pair of trousers that were already flat. Everyone called him Old Yu.

A shadow filled his doorway.

"Uncle Yu."

The man was as wide as a door, with front teeth that stuck out. He was Niu Dali, called Big Tooth by the street. He worked for Yan Tiehu, known on Old Street as Brother Tie, because tie means iron and the man was hard.

Yu Changshun kept ironing. "I'm not open." The sign on his door said otherwise.

"I came to say good morning. Brother Tie likes his neighbors to eat well." Niu Dali put a thick brown envelope on the cutting table. "Twenty thousand. A little something for your granddaughter's school."

Twenty thousand yuan was four months of Yu Changshun's pay.

"I did nothing for it."

"Right. That's exactly what it is for. On Friday you saw nothing. Old eyes, old street, too much dust."

He smiled and patted the head of a little girl at the end of the table.

His granddaughter, nine-year-old Yu Ning, came on Saturdays. Her mother worked weekends.

Niu Dali eyed her. "Pretty. Saturdays at four you walk her to her mother's workshop, down the river lane, right? Nine years old. Nobody on that lane looks out of a window."

The iron stopped.

Niu Dali stood and spoke kindly. "Friday you saw nothing. Keep seeing nothing, and she gets home every Saturday."

He had not said her name. He had not needed to.

He left.

Yu Changshun stood still until the boots faded. His hands shook so badly that the steam from the iron wobbled.

He picked up the envelope as if it might bite. He dropped it into the drawer under the thread and closed it.

On Friday he had seen the truth from this very door. Now he could tell it, or he could keep his granddaughter safe. He did not know how to do both.

Across the lane, Meng Xiaofang had watched the big man come and go from her bun stall. Her eyes met the old tailor's, and neither spoke.

* * *

The screen chimed at last. Thirty-seven.

It had taken an hour and twenty minutes.

Now he stood and walked to the window. Wei Kun looked up from the form on his desk.

One of his cheeks was redder than the other. Cheng Biao had slapped him last night for giving Chen Mingde a cup of water.

"Please tell me the charge against my uncle. And the notice."

"Assault. Under review. No visits." Wei Kun's voice was stiff.

"Officer Wei, your cheek is red. Does it hurt?"

Wei Kun's ears went red too. "I walked into a door."

"That's a hard door. Put some ice on it."

"We'll mail the family notice. You're holding up the line." He said it too fast.

"The law says the family gets the notice within twenty-four hours. I know it's not your fault. Then I'll sit."

Chen Xuan went back to the bench. Wei Kun picked up the desk phone with a damp hand.

"Director Cheng? The nephew is still sitting."

Ten minutes later, the steel door at the back of the lobby clicked, and everyone stood up. The onion woman half stood, because her sack was heavy.

Behind the glass, Wei Kun and his desk neighbor Zhong Hai rose with their hands at their sides. The old records clerk, Ding Shouyi, got up slowly from his stool. A small recorder was running in his sleeve.

Cheng Biao, who ran the station, walked out with a toothpick in his mouth.

Someone whispered, "That's Director Cheng. That poor family is finished."

Cheng Biao did not look at Chen Jianguo. His eyes went over Chen Xuan, from plain sneakers to neat hair, amused.

"Back again, logistics clerk?"

Chen Xuan screwed the cap on his thermos. "Director Cheng. The notice. Now."

"Give them their paper and clear my lobby, Wei. And tell them the visiting room is closed this weekend. Pipe repairs." Cheng Biao leaned toward Chen Jianguo's bruised cheek and spoke low. "Take your father home, boy. Building 2, sixth floor, no lift, and the stairwell light has been broken since spring. At his age, one bad fall means a year in a hospital bed. Nobody ever asks who was on the stairs."

Chen Jianguo's fists closed, and his whole arm shook.

Chen Xuan held his father's sleeve. His own hand shook and his eyes were wet, but he took out his phone and typed with one thumb. His voice was small and not steady.

"Ten forty-one. Director Cheng Biao, to my father, about the stairs. I wrote it down."

Chen Xuan added, "The sign says Legal Aid. Where is the lawyer?"

"Want a lawyer?" Cheng Biao's lip curled. "Then find a good one. A good one charges three thousand yuan an hour. That's half a clerk's month." He turned for the steel door.

Chen Xuan spoke to his back, thin and clear. "Director. If my father or my uncle is hurt, I have your words on my phone. They go to the Discipline Commission."

Cheng Biao took it for a clerk's bluster and laughed once without turning. The steel door clanged behind him.

In the corridor, the toothpick snapped between his teeth, and his smile fell off. The clerk had not shouted. He had said it like the date on a calendar. Cheng Biao would find out who stood behind him before Monday.

Under the glass, one stamped sheet slid out at last.

Wei Kun stared at the time box. He had written the true time there, 7:50 a.m. Saturday, though Cheng Biao had ordered him to write Friday afternoon.

Would the nephew read that far?
