# Chapter 8: Wait Your Turn

The public waiting hall at Old Street Police Station was gray, drafty and already full.

An old woman sat with a sack of onions on her knees, a delivery rider argued into his headset, and a little girl kept sliding off the steel bench.

"Dad, take this." Chen Xuan pulled a paper slip from the ticket machine.

Chen Jianguo squinted at the number. "Thirty-seven."

Above the Case Questions window, the red screen showed who was being served right now.

Twelve.

"Thirty-seven?!" Chen Jianguo groaned. "Xuan, by the time they call that, your uncle will have a beard down to his waist!"

"Then we wait," Chen Xuan said calmly.

He sat on the bench with his scratched green thermos on his knee, as relaxed as a man waiting for a bus.

Behind the thick glass, Wei Kun had not slept at all.

He had spent the night in the duty room with his boots still laced.

At 7:40 that morning, Director Cheng Biao had walked in with a tight face. "The nephew will come today," he said flatly. "Finish the detention paper. Write Friday, 2:30 p.m., right after the arrest."

Wei Kun picked up his pen.

Then he put it down.

It was only a date and a time.

But writing a false one meant signing a lie in his own handwriting, on a paper a family would hold in their hands.

He thought of his pregnant wife, who asked him every night at the door, "Was it a good day?"

So he wrote the true time: Saturday, 7:50 a.m.

It was the only honest line on the whole sheet.

Cheng Biao frowned at it, and Wei Kun mumbled that he had slipped and would reprint it. He never did.

In twelve years on the job, it was the smallest thing he had ever refused, and his palms still would not dry.

Nobody in the lobby knew about that line yet.

At last, the red screen chimed. Thirty-seven.

An hour and twenty minutes, twenty-five numbers.

Chen Jianguo had checked his watch eleven times. Chen Xuan had not checked his once.

The service window was the old kind, with a speaking grille and a narrow slot at the bottom.

Wei Kun looked up from a half-eaten sesame bun and saw a young man with a mild, calm face.

"Good morning, Officer," Chen Xuan said politely. "I'm Chen Xuan. My uncle, Chen Mingde, was brought in yesterday at two in the afternoon. May we know the exact charge, and when the family can see him?"

"Chen Mingde." Wei Kun typed slowly, his voice far away. "Assault. Still under review. He's in holding. No visits."

"For how long?"

"Ten days. Maybe fifteen. Depends on his attitude."

"May we have the written notice, please?"

Wei Kun stopped chewing.

He had met every kind of family at this window. Shouting ones. Weeping ones. Once, a man who dropped to his knees on the tile. For each, he had a speech ready: sit down, wait, we will let you know.

But a polite young man who asked for the paper as if asking for a bank receipt? He had no speech for that!

And the worst part was that he knew what the paper said.

"Who are you, a lawyer?" he asked stiffly.

"No, Officer. I'm an operations supervisor at a logistics company. I check forms all day."

Behind them, someone in the line snorted with laughter.

Wei Kun's ears turned red.

A clerk! A clerk, in front of a full hall, teaching a veteran officer about paperwork!

Yet underneath the shame, cold fear crept in.

What if this man really could read forms? What if he went straight to the bottom line? Who had taught him to ask? Was somebody standing behind him?

He decided one thing. This was too big to handle alone.

"Sir, you're holding up the line," he said coldly. "We'll mail the family notice."

"The station must give the family a written notice within twenty-four hours, Officer," Chen Xuan said gently. "It has been almost twenty. May we take our copy now, so we don't trouble you twice?"

To put it simply, that was the rule. Once someone is detained, the police must tell the family in writing, and a day is the limit. Chen Xuan knew it well. He also knew that a mailed letter could arrive after the day was over, and that a paper in his hand could not.

"I said we'll mail it!"

"Then we will sit here until you have a moment."

Chen Xuan turned and sat back down.

He did not shout, bang the glass or leave.

He sat beside his fuming father with the thermos on his knee, content to wait all morning, and Wei Kun was stuck!

Behind them, the whispers started.

"So polite, that young man..."

"Is he a lawyer?"

"A clerk, he said."

"A clerk who sits like that?"

"Rich families stay calm. The rest of us would be shouting," the old woman with the onions said to the rider, not quietly enough.

She was right about the calm. About the rest, she had no idea.

Wei Kun grabbed the desk phone. "Director Cheng? It's Wei. The nephew is here. He won't leave without the written notice. Yes, Director."

Ten minutes later, the steel door at the back of the lobby clicked open.

Just then, the hall went quiet.

Who was that? A man in a black leather jacket, a toothpick in the corner of his mouth, walked out as if the floor belonged to him. The rider lowered his voice. The little girl stopped sliding.

"That's Director Cheng," someone whispered.

"Now the boy is finished..."

Cheng Biao did not look at Chen Jianguo. His eyes went slowly over Chen Xuan, from his plain sneakers to his neat hair, and stayed there with thin contempt.

"So you're the logistics clerk," he said.

"Good morning, Director Cheng."

"Give them their notice and clear my lobby, Wei." Cheng Biao took the toothpick out. "And tell them the visiting room is closed this weekend. Pipe repairs."

"Director Cheng," Chen Xuan asked politely, "is the lawyer window open on Saturdays?"

Hmm?

Cheng Biao's lip curled. "Have you hired a lawyer?"

"Not yet."

"Then I suggest you find a very good one."

He turned and walked back through the steel door. It clanged shut behind him.

Inside, Cheng Biao's smile was gone.

A clerk who stayed this calm was not an ordinary clerk!

He must have a lawyer friend, or a cousin on the force, someone who had taught him the rules.

Why ask about the lawyer window with no lawyer hired? Who was behind him?

Cheng Biao would find out before Monday.

A moment later, one stamped sheet slid out through the slot under the glass.

Wei Kun stared at the last line on it and forgot to breathe.

Would the nephew read that far?
