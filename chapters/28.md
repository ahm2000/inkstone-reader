# Chapter 28: Just an Old Float

The reservoir water lay flat and green. Nothing stirred on the bank except the straw hat over Chen Xuan's sleeping face.

His grandfather, Chen Fugen, thumped the ground with his fishing rod. "Chen Xuan. Your Second Uncle spent forty hours in a cell, and you come to my reservoir to nap? Look how thin you are. Don't they feed you at work?"

"He came out at 6:20 this morning, Grandpa." Chen Xuan's voice was soft and drowsy. "I couldn't sleep all night. I sat on the station steps and waited for news. That's why I'm worn out."

"It was pure luck, that's all. And here you are, sleeping through it."

Chen Fugen flicked the hat off his grandson's face with the tip of his rod. Chen Xuan caught it without opening his eyes.

"Don't be mad, Grandpa. I'm just glad he's home."

Beside them sat a lean old man in a gray cap and a fishing vest with too many pockets. His back was straight as a ruler.

His name was Zhao Dehai. The villagers thought he was a quiet retiree, maybe an old clerk from some county office, who had fished this bank for three summers. Nobody ever asked more.

Chen Xuan tipped his hat up an inch. "Morning, Old Zhao."

"It's eleven o'clock." Zhao Dehai did not turn his head.

"Then it's a late morning. Those are the best kind."

"Forty hours in a cell for a fight he never started," Zhao Dehai said.

"A crooked director locked him up, and an honest director let him out." Chen Fugen scratched his head. "And one decent young policeman passed him hot water through the bars. Officer Wei something."

"Wei Kun." Chen Xuan's eyes softened as he looked out over the water. "He didn't even know us. He gave a stranger a cup of hot water."

Zhao Dehai said the name twice, slowly, like a man putting it in his pocket. "Wei Kun."

Then he looked at Chen Xuan's line. "There is no worm on your hook."

"No."

"Why not?"

"If a fish bites, it wakes me up. I don't want to be woken up."

"Lazy," Zhao Dehai snapped. "I worked fifty years, and I never met a young man like you."

Chen Xuan grinned. "I work five days a week in the city, Old Zhao. Sunday is my lazy day. It's in my contract."

Zhao Dehai snorted, though the corner of his mouth twitched before he caught it.

"I need bait, and I left my wallet at the guesthouse. Lend me ten yuan."

Chen Xuan dug a bill out of his jacket and held it out between two fingers. "A loan. I'm writing it down. The interest is one worm."

"I'll bring worms." Zhao Dehai marched off, his steps brisk.

At the bait stall, he held up two fingers. "Eight yuan." The vendor asked for ten. "Eight."

Zhao Dehai, the Retired Governor of Lanshui Province, had once signed budgets of forty billion yuan. Now he haggled over two yuan as if he were guarding the whole province.

The vendor muttered, "City folks. Every last one is a miser."

Zhao Dehai took his worms and walked back slowly. Two nights ago, at the guesthouse, his secure line had gone dead. The village phones still worked, but no plane had shown a light as it crossed the stars over the reservoir.

He had stood at his window until they passed. He had asked nobody a question. He had been a governor too long not to know which questions to leave alone.

* * *

Thirty meters behind the fishermen, an unremarkable man in a gray jacket sat under a willow tree, reading a newspaper.

He was Number Nine of the Black Pine Guard, the secret unit that watched over Chen Xuan. He had been in position since dawn.

Then he noticed a man selling cabbages nearby.

The vendor wore a farmer's straw hat, yet his back was the straightest in the village, and his cabbages did not have one wilted leaf.

Out on the water, two men sat in a small wooden boat. Their fishing lines had never once been cast.

Number Nine understood at once. A bodyguard always knows another bodyguard. The vendor understood in the same second. Neither of them moved.

The vendor said in a rough country drawl, "Nice weather."

"Good for fishing," Number Nine said.

Slowly, under the fold of his newspaper, Number Nine opened his palm. A small black badge carved with a pine branch lay there for one breath. It was the Black Pine badge.

The vendor drew a green stone token from his belt, no bigger than a domino, and showed it just as slowly. It was the mark of a retired senior leader's personal guard.

"Ren Gang," he murmured. "Colonel. Forty men. Eleven years with the old gentleman."

"Number Nine."

"Black Pine." Ren Gang looked at the badge again, and his back straightened even more. "There are only thirty of those badges in the whole country. My principal must not know I'm here."

"Mine doesn't know I'm here either."

"He thinks he's alone?"

"He thinks he's eating a steamed bun."

Ren Gang's moustache twitched. "My old gentleman thinks he's a retired clerk on holiday."

Number Nine glanced at the reservoir. "Your boat hasn't cast a line all day."

"Your bakery van is parked in the middle of a cabbage field."

Ren Gang was quiet for a moment. Then he looked up at the empty sky.

"The night before last, black planes with no lights went over this water. The old gentleman's secure line died for an hour, though the village phones worked. He stood at his window until the planes passed."

"Did he ask you about it?"

"No. And I told him nothing."

Number Nine kept his eyes on his newspaper. "I saw nothing either."

Neither of them spoke of the red line on Number Nine's phone. It said that one more team stood near this bank, a team he could not see and could not touch.

They did not shake hands. They stayed seated like strangers, two professionals doing their jobs while the two men they guarded chatted like neighbors at a bus stop.
