# Chapter 6: The Empty Chair

Gong's Noodles was the last light on Old Street, and it held a small mystery.

By the window stood a round table with four chairs.

Three were used every night.

The fourth was always empty, with a clean bowl and a pair of chopsticks set in front of it.

Nobody who walked in knew why.

Gong Yi knew.

He was a thin man in glasses and a flour-dusted apron, and he looked like a quiet noodle seller who had never raised his voice in his life.

His real title was Number Twenty-three of the Detail, and the Detail's whole watch over Yunxi ran from the back of his kitchen.

"Eleven ways," said the delivery rider, slumping onto a stool with his helmet in his lap. "I counted eleven ways to break that man's hand tonight. I held my handlebars so hard I bent them."

"Twelve," said Auntie Ma, setting down a tray. "You forgot the ladle."

"And the Boss ate a bun!" cried the cook, whose nickname was Big Pot. "His father gets slapped on the lane, and he eats a bun!"

"He eats when he's thinking," Gong Yi said, wiping a cup.

"Does he think?" the rider asked.

"Mostly he just eats."

The three of them laughed, quietly, and for a moment the kitchen was warm.

Then Gong Yi raised one finger.

Through the open window, from the barbecue stall across the little square, came a man's loud, happy voice.

"Drink, Officer Lei! Tonight, all on Hongrui!"

Brother Hu sat there with his shirt open and a towel on his neck, and across the table sat Lei Bo, the young officer from the front hall, with his hair still gelled.

Beside them lounged Big Tooth and Thin Monkey, gnawing skewers.

"Brother Hu, you are a hero," Lei Bo said, raising his glass. "Slapping an old man in front of the whole lane! The street will talk for a year."

"I did it politely. Only one hand." Brother Hu held it up and flexed it. "Besides, Director Cheng said no arrests, so I made no arrests. I only put up a little paper and paint."

"Ha! Paper only!"

Brother Hu tossed a red envelope across the table.

"Five thousand. For your trouble, brother, and for your bad memory about Brother Hu's name."

Lei Bo pocketed it faster than a rabbit ducks into its hole.

"Memory? What name?"

"Good boy. And this one is ten thousand, for Director Cheng's tea." Brother Hu slid out a second envelope, thicker than the first. "He says no arrests. Fine. I don't need arrests. I need the police blind."

"The station is blind in both eyes, Brother Hu," Lei Bo said.

"I have a code, you know." Brother Hu thumped his own chest. "Never touch a woman, never touch a child."

"Code!" Thin Monkey cackled. "Last week you cried over a cat."

"It had a limp!"

The stall shook with laughter, and a drunk customer at the next table clapped.

Brother Hu stopped laughing first.

"But the old man's family, that is different," he said, and he picked his teeth with a skewer. "The sister teaches at the junior high. Tomorrow a man will ask her principal for her file. The mother climbs six floors every day, and the stairwell bulb is already out. Stairs are long, brothers."

In the noodle shop, Big Pot dropped his ladle, and nobody picked it up.

"Another thing." Brother Hu leaned in. "That clerk. The nephew with the bun. I looked at him, and for one second I thought, hmm. Then I laughed. What can a man with a lunch box do?"

"He came to the station with a blanket!" Lei Bo said. "A blanket! The Director nearly died laughing."

The whole table roared.

In the noodle shop, the rider's knuckles went white on his helmet.

"May I at least put soy sauce in his beer?"

"No," said Gong Yi.

"May I accidentally drop a pot lid on his head?"

"No," said Gong Yi, and he almost smiled.

"A big pot lid?" Big Pot asked hopefully.

"No." Gong Yi tapped a small recorder next to the cash register, whose red light glowed. "Every word is saved. Five thousand yuan, one officer, one envelope. That is about two weeks of a clerk's pay, and it will be in the file."

"And when does the file open?"

"When the Boss says so. For now he has told us only to record."

Auntie Ma sighed.

"Then pray he gets hungry for justice."

* * *

In the underground garage of Hongrui Real Estate, Dong Haisheng sat in the back seat of his black sedan with the engine off, and the dark was so quiet that he could hear his own watch.

He had made a joke at dinner, and he had laughed at it.

That was an hour ago.

Now his phone lit up on his knee, with a name he had never saved and always recognized.

He answered with both hands.

"Chairman Huang."

"Boss Dong." The voice was soft, almost kind, like an uncle's at a New Year table. "Have you eaten?"

"I have, thank you. I."

"I heard the Secretary spoke tonight. No more arrests." A small pause. "That must be hard on you."

Dong Haisheng's mouth was dry.

"My three hundred million costs two percent a month. Do you know how much that is each day, Boss Dong?"

"Two hundred thousand, Chairman."

"Two hundred thousand. Your driver needs three years to earn that. You lose it before breakfast." The voice stayed gentle. "On the thirtieth, Huaxin Bank reviews your 1.1 billion yuan loan. If you haven't broken ground, they take everything. Then I take what's left of you."

"The corner will be cleared by Monday."

"Is the corner cleared yet, Boss Dong?"

"Not yet."

"Mm. I hear about a clerk with a bun. A clerk is small. But small things trip big men on dark stairs." A soft laugh. "Your girl is in Grade Four at the river school, I hear. A good school. Such a long road home. Remove the clerk, Boss Dong, or I will send someone to help you count the days."

A drop of sweat ran down Dong Haisheng's neck and into his collar.

"No need, Chairman! I will handle it!"

The line clicked.

Dong Haisheng stared at his hand. It trembled so hard that the phone rattled against the window.

He wiped his face with his sleeve, and then he dialed another number.

"Hu. Tomorrow, no arrests. But the tailor and the cobbler, make them tired. No marks on faces."

"Boss Dong, you got it!"

He hung up and said nothing for a long time.

Why, with all his money, did he feel like the smallest man in the car?

* * *

By three in the morning, the last customer had gone.

Gong Yi stood at the window table and looked at the fourth chair.

A fresh bowl of noodles had gone on it at midnight and was cold by now.

He took it away, washed it, and laid down a clean one.

"Same tomorrow?" Auntie Ma asked from the doorway.

"The same."

"Three years, and he has never sat in that chair. He doesn't even know the shop is ours."

"He will." Gong Yi straightened the chopsticks until they lay perfectly parallel. "A chair that stays empty is a promise. When the Boss finally comes and sits, I want the bowl to be hot."

Auntie Ma smiled.

On the wall behind him, the small coded screen showed one green dot, and it was not on Fuxing New Village.

It was on the corner of Old Street, beside a crooked shutter.

"He is up?" Auntie Ma whispered.

The delivery rider leaned out of the back door and squinted down the dark lane.

"He has a screwdriver," he reported. "He is putting the shutter back on its bolt. He is humming. Big Tooth shouted from the van, 'Fixing a wall that is coming down, clerk?' He said, 'Evening. It hangs better straight,' and went on humming. Now he is fixing the lock, with his tongue out, like a boy."

"Does he look angry?"

"He looks like a man who wants to go back to bed."

They watched the dot for a while, and nobody laughed.

Under it on the screen, one line of text glowed.

*8:30 a.m.: goes to Old Street Station.*

"A bench," Gong Yi said, shaking his head. "I wonder what the station will do when it finds out who is sitting on it."
