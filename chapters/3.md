# Chapter 3: The Booking Desk

Behind the booking desk at Old Street Police Station, Wei Kun sat and stared down at a blank intake form.

"Name," he said.

"Chen Mingde."

"Occupation."

"Hardware shop owner," came the quiet answer.

"Do you understand why you are here?"

"No. I never touched that man."

"Then say so in your statement," Wei Kun muttered, and looked away.

The man in front of him was thin and gray-haired, and he kept his eyes on the floor.

He was not going to make trouble.

Under Attitude, Wei Kun wrote one word: Calm.

In twelve years, he had booked more than three hundred suspects, and he could tell the troublemakers by their shoulders.

Wei Kun was alone with this file for a simple reason. The station had thirty-six names on its roster, but only ten were real police officers, and he was one of them. The other twenty-six were auxiliary staff on one-year contracts, who carried batons instead of guns and never got promoted.

Two weeks earlier, the Political Instructor and the deputy chief had both been sent away on a "training course." The Political Instructor, Qiu Guoqiang, was the station's second in command, and the man who actually ran it by the book.

How convenient!

It left Cheng Biao's keys and his dirtiest files sitting on Wei Kun's desk.

Then he flipped to the attached evidence, and his pen stopped dead!

Clipped to the file was a private clinic's injury note for Boss Dong's driver.

It was stamped ten minutes to two!

Wei Kun's neck went ice-cold.

The arrest had been at five past two, yet the doctor had signed the injury note fifteen minutes before. The "assault" had not even happened when somebody wrote down the wound!

The note was fake! It had been written in advance!

And he knew exactly whose signature sat on the detention order.

Should he say something? Who in this building would listen, and what would it cost him?

On paper he held a senior-sounding title, "deputy principal staff member," with nobody under him and no power to sign an order.

Cheng Biao, the Deputy Director of the whole county bureau, was the man who really ran this station. He stood only one real step above that empty title, but it was the only step that mattered.

And Wei Kun still owed a mortgage of twenty-eight hundred yuan every month.

His wife was seven months pregnant and could barely bend down to tie her shoes.

That morning, Cheng Biao had said it plainly at the meeting: "You fill in the forms, Wei. Thinking is my job."

Wei Kun swallowed hard and kept writing until he reached the box marked Time of Decision. The reason for that box was simple. Police must record the exact moment they legally decide to detain a suspect, and every later deadline counts from it.

His fingers would not move.

He left the box blank.

"Don't fill that in yet."

Cheng Biao had strolled in without a sound. He wore a black leather jacket over his uniform trousers, and a wooden toothpick rolled between his teeth.

"Is the rest finished, Wei?"

"Yes, Director Cheng," Wei Kun said stiffly. "Only the time box is empty."

"Good. We will write the time in later."

In a government office, a lower officer always drops the word "deputy" and calls his boss by the highest title he can. This was doubly true for a man whose signature could lock anyone in Yunxi behind bars for fifteen days.

Cheng Biao perched on the corner of the desk.

He smiled down at Chen Mingde like a friendly uncle at a family dinner.

"Old Chen, let's be honest," he said softly. "Assaulting a man in public means ten days in detention, maybe fifteen, plus a police record. Your daughter is in college, isn't she? Think how she will have to explain her father's record at every job interview. It does not have to be ugly. Sign the buyout contract, Hongrui drops the complaint, and you are home for dinner in ten minutes!"

Silence fell over the desk.

Not a pen scratched. Not a phone rang.

"Director Cheng," Chen Mingde said quietly, still not looking up. "I want to see the written detention order first."

"What?" Cheng Biao blinked.

"The official paper that says why you are holding me, and when it was signed. I want to see it first."

"Paper?" Cheng Biao repeated slowly.

"Yes, Director Cheng. That is all I ask."

The toothpick stopped moving!

Eleven Old Street owners had sat in that chair over the past two months, and all eleven had broken down and signed. It had worked every single time!

So why was this twelfth man, a nobody with a tiny shop and no connections, calmly asking for official paperwork? This was not in the plan!

Was he stubborn? Was he stupid? Or did he know somebody important?

Unease crept down the back of Cheng Biao's neck, and he hated it!

Too much was riding on this one corner shop. His brother-in-law held a thirty-eight million yuan demolition contract. His wife wanted a villa. And next Wednesday, the provincial inspection team would arrive in Yunxi County!

To put it simply, those were anti-corruption investigators sent down from the provincial capital. One dirty land grab shown to them could end a local official's career in a single afternoon.

Wednesday sat on Cheng Biao's chest like a heavy stone.

"Smart handling, Director," a junior officer said as he passed the desk.

He had seen none of it, and he understood even less.

"Thank you," Cheng Biao said, without looking up, but he filed the man's name away anyway. Flattery was cheap, but it was never completely worthless.

"Fine, Old Chen," Cheng Biao snapped, and the friendly mask was gone. "Wait for your paper, then. Lock him up, Wei. A cold night in the cell will clear his head!"

"Yes, Director!"

Wei Kun stood up and unlocked the cell door, and his knees felt weak.

"Officer," Chen Mingde said quietly as he stepped inside, "will the paper come tomorrow?"

Wei Kun could not answer.

On the desk, a blank box on the form still waited for a time.

What time would Cheng Biao want in it, and would Wei Kun be the one to write it?
