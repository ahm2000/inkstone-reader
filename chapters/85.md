# Chapter 85: Elder Song's Afternoon Tea

Long shadows stretched across the grey stone yard in the Western Hills outside Beijing. Crisp autumn wind moved through the old pines, carrying the dry scent of fallen needles and damp earth. Along the outer wall stood ten motionless figures in plain black gear, dark masks covering their faces, rifles hanging still at their sides. Not even the compound's own security chief stepped inside the outer edge around the wooden pavilion.

Inside the pavilion, ninety-year-old founding elder Elder Song poured dark Pu'er tea from a small clay pot into two white cups. His spotted hands moved slowly and steadily, never spilling a single drop onto the carved elm table.

Retired General Elder Qin Zhengguo sat across from him on a wooden stool, his wool jacket unbuttoned at the collar. He had spent twelve straight hours shouting down agency heads, clearing emergency arrest orders, and locking down provincial command lines. Yet in front of Elder Song, his broad shoulders carried a quiet, heavy weight.

"Drink while it is hot, Qin Zhengguo," Elder Song said in a voice dry as pine bark. "You look like you have been chewing iron since sunrise."

Elder Qin Zhengguo lifted his cup, took a slow sip, and set it down with a soft click. He glanced once toward the outer stone wall where the ten black figures stood without shifting an inch.

"Old Chief," Qin Zhengguo said quietly, "I have served the state for fifty years. I have seen ministers fall and generals stripped of their stars. Still, I have never seen Beijing freeze solid over a single phone call from a quiet county."

Elder Song smiled faintly and turned his own cup between his fingers. "Are you asking why the Premier bowed his head, or why I did not stop the Black Order from moving?"

"Both," Qin Zhengguo admitted. "The younger men in the central offices are terrified. They keep asking how one private citizen can hold a token that outranks standing cabinet ministers and shuts down the White Guard across the whole country without firing one bullet. They think the state has lost its balance."

Elder Song looked out past the pavilion eaves toward the red autumn ridges of the Western Hills. For a long moment, only the rustle of wind through the pine branches filled the yard.

"The younger men read history out of clean textbooks," Elder Song said softly. "They only see the tall towers in Beijing and the paved highways running across every province. They never saw the winter when our grain barns were empty, the treasury held less than a week of silver, and foreign ships blocked every port along the coast."

Qin Zhengguo sat straighter. He had been a young company commander back then, old enough to remember hungry winters, though too junior to know what happened behind the locked doors of the founding council.

"In that winter," Elder Song continued, "three entire provinces were one bad frost away from collapse. Our factories had no steel, our hospitals had no medicine, and our railways had no coal. The founding council sat in a cold room for four nights with nothing left to pledge."

He poured a thin stream of fresh tea into Qin Zhengguo's cup.

"Then an anonymous benefactor reached out through a single silent courier," Elder Song said. "Over the next forty years, that lineage opened hidden overseas credit lines, shipped grain through blockaded waters, delivered industrial blueprints we could not buy at any price, and backed our early sovereign reserves when foreign banks tried to crush our currency. Measured in today's scale, the wealth they poured into keeping this nation alive exceeds 1.2 trillion."

Qin Zhengguo stared into his tea. His fingers tightened slightly around the porcelain rim. "And they never asked for a seat in the Great Hall."

"Not one seat," Elder Song said. "Not one medal, not one statue, not one street named after them. When the founding council tried to offer them governorships and hereditary honors, their patriarch refused every single paper. He asked for only two things."

"What two things?" Qin Zhengguo asked.

"First, that their family name be erased from every public ledger so their descendants could live simple, peaceful lives away from politics," Elder Song replied. "Second, a blood-signed Black Order pledged by the founding elders themselves. If corrupt officials or greedy power brokers ever pushed their household to the wall, the holder of the Black Order could strike once, and the state would honor the debt without question."

Qin Zhengguo let out a long, slow breath. The tension in his shoulders eased into something much deeper, closer to awe.

"So the Black Order was never a gift we handed down," Qin Zhengguo murmured. "It is an unpaid debt we owe."

"An unpayable debt," Elder Song corrected gently. "You cannot pay back the hand that kept you breathing when you were starving. You can only honor your word when that hand finally knocks on the door."

Qin Zhengguo shook his head with a bitter laugh. "And out of all the doors in this country, the fools in Handong Province chose to kick open the one household they should never have touched. Deputy Provincial Secretary Gao Yuliang and Shanshui Group head Zhao Ruilong thought their Handa Faction was untouchable."

"Greedy men always think the sky belongs to them until it falls," Elder Song said. He rested his thin fingers against the warm clay teapot. "The holder of the Black Order does not want Beijing. He does not want ministries, armies, or headlines. If he had wanted power, he would not have lived quietly in Yunxi County for all these years. He only wants his family left in peace."

"Provincial Party Secretary Sha Ruijin and Anti-Corruption Bureau Chief Hou Liangping are moving fast in Jingzhou City," Qin Zhengguo reported. "Gao Yuliang and Zhao Ruilong are already locked away, and Public Security Director Qi Tongwei's local network is falling apart. Still, Premier Gu Chengshan wants a full vote tonight in the Great Hall to clear out their protectors in the central ministries."

Elder Song nodded once. "Good. Let the young ministers see that the old rules still bite. Tonight we clean the house properly, not just for the holder of the Black Order, but so the people across every province remember that the state still has a spine."

Outside the pavilion, the ten black figures stood silent against the fading golden light, watching the mountain road as the autumn chill settled over the Western Hills.
