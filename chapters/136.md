# Chapter 136: Zurich Strikes Back

Late Sunday night in Zurich, cold rain tapped against the tall windows of a wood-paneled boardroom overlooking the dark lake.

Inside, the air was thick with cigar smoke and quiet fury.

Henrik Lindholm, senior partner of the Northern Ledger, stood at the head of the oak table with his palms pressed flat on the wood. A printed news report from Lanshui Province lay before him, showing a grainy photograph of Li Weiming being led out of a hotel lobby in handcuffs by Chinese police.

"Two years of work," Henrik Lindholm said, his voice tight and cold. "More than 400 million yuan in quiet fees and local favors. Gone in forty minutes."

Across the table, Thomas Bauer tapped ash from his cigarette into a crystal tray. His eyes stayed on the second photograph clipped to the report. It showed the Lanshui Convention Center and Qin Yuhan standing at the front microphone with her blue folder held high.

"Li Weiming was careless," Thomas Bauer said flatly. "He let a local clerk read page forty-eight out loud in front of three hundred chairmen and six television cameras. Once the crowd heard Zurich, Deputy Governor Wu Zhenbang ran for the door like a scalded rabbit."

"Li Weiming is finished," Henrik Lindholm snapped. "He is in a Chinese detention cell, and our lawyers cannot get past the gate. Forget him. What matters is what the other coastal provinces see tomorrow morning."

A younger director cleared his throat. "They will see Northern Ledger got slapped in public and walked away without throwing a punch."

"Exactly," Henrik Lindholm said softly. He lit a fresh cigarette and took one slow drag. "If twenty port directors think they can tear up our contracts on live television and sleep peacefully, our business in Asia is dead before summer."

"Then who do we hit?" Thomas Bauer asked, narrowing his eyes. "We still do not know who that clerk in the white shirt was. Our people in Qingyan checked Hengxin Logistics. It is a tiny freight office on the twelfth floor of an old building, with twelve trucks and a nervous manager named Sun Haoran. Hitting them is like stepping on an ant."

Henrik Lindholm's finger came down hard on Qin Yuhan's photograph.

"We do not step on an ant," he said coldly. "We cut down the tallest tree in the harbor."

The room went silent.

"Haiyue Group," Thomas Bauer murmured, leaning forward. "Qin Yuhan was the first chairwoman to stand up and back that clerk's questions. She even handed her customs records to the reporters."

"Yes," Henrik Lindholm said with a cold smile. "Haiyue Group owns three of the cleanest deep-water docks in Qingyan. Half the shipping firms in Lanshui look to Qin Yuhan before signing a paper. If Haiyue Group is bleeding on the floor by Wednesday, every other port chairman will learn the price of crossing us."

He pressed a brass button beside his elbow.

Thirty seconds later, the door opened. A sharp-featured man in a charcoal suit walked in, carrying a slim briefcase and wearing the easy grin of someone who enjoyed bad news.

Marcus Vance was managing director of Aegis Meridian, an overseas hedge fund that worked with Northern Ledger whenever a public company needed to be broken.

"Evening, gentlemen," Marcus Vance said, taking a chair without waiting to be asked. He glanced at Qin Yuhan's photo. "Rough weekend in Lanshui."

"We did not call you for sympathy, Marcus," Henrik Lindholm said briskly. "We called you to hunt."

Marcus Vance's grin widened. "Haiyue Group?"

"Haiyue Group," Thomas Bauer confirmed, sliding a folder across the table. "They are publicly listed, built on port terminals, and carrying 6 billion yuan in short-term bank loans that renew next quarter. Their cash reserves are thin after buying new cranes last autumn."

Marcus Vance scanned the loan schedule, his eyes lighting up.

"6 billion yuan in bank loans tied to their share price," Marcus Vance muttered, tapping the page. "A glass jaw. If the stock drops fifteen percent in a day, those nervous local banks will scream for their money back before the ink dries."

"We want blood in the water by Monday noon," Henrik Lindholm said coldly, "and we want them begging for buyers by Wednesday."

"How much ammunition are we putting behind this?" Marcus Vance asked.

"Aegis Meridian already holds borrowing lines for huge blocks of Haiyue shares," Henrik Lindholm replied. "Northern Ledger will back you with an extra 10 billion yuan in credit tonight. Dump borrowed shares the second Monday's market opens. Hit them hard in the first two hours so retail buyers panic."

Marcus Vance nodded slowly. "Selling alone drops them eight or ten percent. To push them off a cliff, the market needs an ugly story so the banks freeze their credit lines immediately."

Thomas Bauer smiled faintly and tapped a second sheet inside the folder.

"Already written," Thomas Bauer said. "At nine o'clock Monday morning, thirty minutes before the opening bell, four overseas financial wires and a dozen paid market chats will run the exact same rumor. They will claim customs inspectors opened a secret smuggling probe into Haiyue's third dock and questioned two senior managers over the weekend."

Marcus Vance let out a low whistle. "A port-smuggling probe? Even if Qin Yuhan proves it is fake three days later, no bank manager waits three days when a headline like that hits his desk. They freeze her accounts first and ask questions next month."

"That is the point," Henrik Lindholm said, stubbing out his cigarette. "While she fights angry banks and scared directors, her stock keeps falling. Tuesday morning, drop a second wave of 15 billion yuan in short orders right at the open. That snaps their spine. By Thursday, our partner firms buy Haiyue's docks for thirty cents on the dollar."

Marcus Vance closed the folder and stood up, buttoning his jacket with crisp satisfaction.

"Consider it done," Marcus Vance said smoothly. "My trading floor is already on the night shift. By tomorrow's lunch break, Chairwoman Qin Yuhan will wish she had stayed quiet in Lanshui."

"No mistakes, Marcus," Henrik Lindholm warned. "This is a lesson."

"I love teaching lessons," Marcus Vance replied with a thin smile. "Watch the screens at nine thirty."

When the door clicked shut, Thomas Bauer poured two fingers of whiskey.

"What about the plain clerk from Qingyan?" Thomas Bauer asked casually. "Chen Xuan?"

Henrik Lindholm scoffed softly, waving a hand.

"He makes five thousand yuan a month and carries a dented thermos," Henrik Lindholm said. "Once Haiyue Group collapses, his little office closes by winter. We do not waste a single euro on a nobody."

Outside, cold rain poured down the Zurich glass, while six thousand miles away, Monday morning was already rising over Qingyan Harbor.
