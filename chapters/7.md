# Chapter 7: Hot Water

Old Street was dark, and every shutter was pulled tight except two.

At the far end, under one bare bulb, Old Yu sat with a trouser cuff on his knee.

He had hemmed cuffs on this street for forty-four years.

Tonight his fingers were not really sewing.

They were shaking.

"Old Yu, it's late," his wife called softly from the back room. "Come to bed."

"Soon," he answered. "One more cuff."

"You have not eaten tonight."

"I am not hungry."

"Then at least drink some water," she said.

There was no more cuff to finish, but he could not make himself stand up.

He could not forget the sight of Chen Mingde in handcuffs. Old Yu had stood silent in his doorway while Boss Dong smiled at him, and now he felt disgusted with himself!

Eleven families had signed!

Out of the last three, the toughest one sat in a police cell, and that left only two!

"If Old Chen signs, I have no chance!" Old Yu muttered.

"If he doesn't, they will come for me next. Then who pays my granddaughter's tuition, or my wife's heart medicine?"

He watched the steel needle tremble in his hand.

"Tomorrow," he told himself. "If Old Chen is still in that cell tomorrow night, I will sign."

"I am an old man. Nobody will blame me!"

Was that true?

He did not know.

Directly across the dark street, one other faint yellow light was burning.

Inside the shuttered steamed-bun stall, Meng Xiaofang sat beside her sleeping four-year-old daughter. Her eyes were swollen red.

In her dented tin box lay eighty-three crumpled yuan, all she had for rent, flour and milk.

"Sleep, little one," she whispered. "Mama is here."

The little girl did not wake.

"One more day," Meng Xiaofang said to the tin box. "Please, just one more day."

Meng Xiaofang knew the plain math: if Old Yu signed, she would be the very last holdout!

And the last one standing was always the easiest to crush.

Neither neighbor knew what the other had decided. Yet behind two dim windows across the silent lane, two tired people made the exact same quiet vow.

If Chen Mingde was not home by tomorrow night, they would give up!

---

Inside Old Street Police Station, the holding cell was small and smelled faintly of bleach and damp concrete.

It held a hard bench, a tiny barred window high near the ceiling, and a bare bulb that never switched off.

Chen Mingde sat with his back against the wall and his rough hands between his knees.

Nobody had given him a blanket, and the autumn chill crept up through the concrete and into his bones.

Across from him, a heavyset man brought in for a bar fight lay snoring on the other bench, and this was not his first night in this room.

"The food is terrible," the man had told Chen Mingde, rolling over. "The blankets are a myth. The smartest thing to do is sleep."

"You should have just paid up!" he added, before the snoring began.

"Me, I decked a guy over fifty yuan, and I'm out in three days. You're in here over some shop. Rich folks' problems!"

"Maybe so," Chen Mingde said quietly.

The man did not hear him, because he was already snoring again.

He had said it as if Chen Mingde had chosen this. Some people always find a way to make the smaller man the fool for not giving in.

Chen Mingde could not sleep.

He thought of his wife, Wu Lanying, sitting stiffly on his brother's sofa. She was probably holding a cup of water she would not drink.

He thought of his daughter, Xiaoqing, laughing with her roommates in her Wuhan dormitory, and he had not let anyone call her.

"Let the girl have one more peaceful night," he whispered.

What would Lanying say, if she could speak to him now?

"Come home," she would say. "Nothing else matters."

And against his will, he thought about the four hundred fifty-six thousand yuan.

To be honest, wasn't that a pile of money? He had never held so much cash in his life!

If he signed tomorrow morning, he would walk out the front door, and his wife would cry with relief. Who could blame an aging hardware seller for wanting to go home?

Yet that corner shop was twenty-two years of his life!

It was the shutter he rolled up at seven every morning and locked at nine every night. It was every hand-labeled screw drawer, and the red-and-white sign he had painted himself.

It was every neighbor on Old Street saying, "Go ask Old Chen, he'll have it."

If he sold it for less than half its worth, what would he be?

Not a hardware man. Not anything!

"Don't pull back when the fish pulls," he whispered to the blank wall. "Wait."

Tap, tap.

Slow, heavy footsteps echoed down the concrete hallway.

They stopped right outside the iron bars. Chen Mingde looked up and saw a round-faced police officer with tired red eyes, holding a steaming paper cup.

For several seconds Wei Kun just stood there, as if he had forgotten why he had come down at all.

"Uncle," he said at last, keeping his voice low enough that the hallway camera could not pick it up. "Would you like some hot water?"

Chen Mingde reached through the bars and took the cup with both hands.

It was only plain boiled water, yet the moment the warmth reached his palms, his fingers stopped shaking.

"Thank you, young man."

"Drink it slowly," Wei Kun said. "It's hot."

"What time is it? The light never goes out in here."

"Eleven o'clock, Uncle. It's still Friday."

"Then tomorrow has not started yet," Chen Mingde said, and he gave a small smile.

"Is my family still outside, young man?"

"I cannot say, Uncle."

Wei Kun had nothing more to give. He gave one stiff nod and walked quickly back down the hall, like a man who had just broken a rule.

Silence.

Chen Mingde pressed the warm cup against his chest and closed his eyes.

He could not explain why, but a quiet certainty settled over him that somebody was already on the way.

His older brother, of course. And right behind his brother, somewhere out in the night, was the boy who had learned never to yank too hard on a fishing line.
