# Chapter 7: Hot Water

Near midnight, in the duty room of Old Street Police Station, duty officer Wei Kun's hands were still warm.

He sat at the duty desk and stared at them as if they belonged to somebody else.

An old man in a cell had taken a paper cup through the bars with both hands and said, "Thank you, young man."

Nobody in this building had thanked Wei Kun for anything in three years.

So why did he feel like a thief?

"Brother Wei!"

Lei Bo leaned in at the door, chewing sunflower seeds and grinning.

"I saw you on the hallway screen. Bringing room service to the guest, eh?"

"He's cold." Wei Kun kept his voice low. "It was a cup of water."

"Of course, of course." Lei Bo shut the door and sat on the desk. "But Director Cheng says no comforts for the Old Street holdouts. Cold nights make warm signatures. You know that."

"I know."

"I would hate for him to hear about it." Lei Bo rubbed his thumb against his fingers. "Silence is cheap, Brother Wei. A carton of good cigarettes, eight hundred yuan, and my memory goes bad. Or it stays sharp, and I tell Director Cheng before breakfast. Your wife is seven months along, isn't she? One salary, a mortgage, a baby. A bad review costs more than cigarettes."

"I have a mortgage."

"Everybody has something. My cousin paid two hundred thousand for a seat on the traffic team. He is very happy now." In the station, a "seat" was a good post that could be bought. "Me, I'm saving up. Or I could skip the saving and do Director Cheng a favor, like telling him about a soft-hearted officer." Lei Bo smiled. "Think about it."

Two hundred thousand yuan was forty-three months of Wei Kun's pay.

Then slow, heavy boots sounded in the corridor, and Lei Bo's smile vanished as if it had never been there.

Cheng Biao, the deputy chief who ran the station, walked in with a toothpick in his teeth and liquor on his breath.

He had come straight from the banquet at Fuyuan Garden, where Fan Weiguo, the County Party Secretary, had warned him in front of everyone. Fan Weiguo never shouted. He had once told Cheng Biao, "I keep a list, and a chair is easy to replace."

Cheng Biao had been looking for somebody to blame ever since.

"Director Cheng!" Lei Bo cried. "You came yourself! I was just about to report a serious mistake!"

"Is that so?"

"Yes, Director! Brother Wei carried hot water to the Old Street guest! I told him no!"

Wei Kun stood and kept his hands at his sides. "Director Cheng."

"I hear somebody is running a hotel in my cell."

"Director, it was boiled water. The man is fifty-two, and the floor is concrete."

"Did I ask what he was?"

"No, Director."

"Then why are you answering?"

Silence.

Lei Bo added, "He's always soft, Director. Last month he gave a drunk his own bun."

"Lei Bo."

"Yes, Director?"

"Did you say no to him, or did you only think it?"

Lei Bo's mouth opened and shut.

"In my heart, Director." He went red to the ears.

Cheng Biao stepped close to Wei Kun, and the toothpick rolled to the corner of his mouth.

Then his hand came up, and crack.

Wei Kun's head turned with the blow. His cap fell and rolled across the floor.

His cheek burned, and something worse burned behind it. He was thirty-six years old, with twelve years on the force, and he had just been slapped in front of a boy who laughed at everything.

Shame rose hot up his neck. He did not touch his face.

Cheng Biao spoke quietly. "Kindness costs money, Wei. Who pays for yours? You are a deputy principal staff member. That fancy title has nothing under it."

"Yes, Director."

"I can have you guarding the county landfill gate by Monday, and nobody would ask why. Do you know how many men would pay for your seat?"

"No, Director."

"More than you can afford. Your wife is due in the spring, I hear. She is booked at the county maternity hospital. Its vice president eats dinner with me twice a month, and beds there go to families with clean files." Cheng Biao took out the toothpick and studied it. "Yours will have a note in it by Monday."

Wei Kun's mouth went dry.

"Tomorrow the nephew comes back to my lobby. At 7:40 you bring me the detention paper, the one with the empty time box. It will say what I tell you to write. Understood?"

"Understood, Director."

"Clean up."

He walked out, and Lei Bo trotted after him, laughing at nothing.

The door shut.

Wei Kun did not move. Then the door opened a crack, and a thin boy slipped in.

It was Zhou Pingan, the nineteen-year-old auxiliary whose cap Cheng Biao had knocked off only a few hours before.

He picked up Wei Kun's cap, brushed it clean on his sleeve, and held it out with both hands.

He said nothing. His eyes were wet, but he stood straight. Then he hurried away.

Wei Kun looked at the cap for a long time.

His phone buzzed, and he knew who it was before he looked.

"Kun? You are still up?"

His wife, Su Min, sounded soft and sleepy. Her voice filled the cold room like a lamp.

"The baby kicked twice just now. I think she knows your voice. Was it a good day?"

Every night she asked, and every night he said yes.

He looked at the red mark on his cheek in the dark window.

"A good day," he said.

"You are a bad liar, you know." She laughed gently. "Did you eat?"

"I ate."

"The mortgage is due on the fifth, twenty-eight hundred. I moved the money from the baby fund. There are still six thousand one hundred left, so don't worry."

"I will worry anyway."

"Kun." Her voice dropped. "Whatever it is, don't be a hero. Be a father. Come home to us."

His throat closed.

"I will," he said.

"Promise?"

"Promise."

After she hung up, Wei Kun sat very still, holding a cap he did not want to put on.

* * *

At 7:40 the next morning, Cheng Biao stood over the desk with a bun in one hand and a blank notice in the other.

"Friday, two-thirty p.m., right after the arrest. Write it."

Cheng Biao wanted the paper to say the decision to hold Chen Mingde came the moment he was arrested. Then the long night in the cell would look legal. The truth was worse. Nobody had decided anything on paper until this morning, and the old man had sat in a cold cell for eighteen hours.

Lei Bo waited at his elbow with the red stamp ready.

Wei Kun took the pen. His fingers were cold. He remembered everything at once: the paper cup, Su Min's voice, two hundred thousand yuan, the crack of a slap.

Don't be a hero, she had said.

He was not. He was just a man whose hand would not write a lie for an old man's family to read.

He did not write Friday. He wrote the true time.

*Time of Decision: Saturday, 7:50 a.m.*

The ink was still wet when Cheng Biao's phone rang.

"Boss Dong!" Cheng Biao's back bent a little as he turned toward the window. "Good morning! Yes, yes, the nephew comes today. I have it in hand!"

"Director, the time box," Wei Kun began. He was ready to say what he had written.

"Fine, fine." Cheng Biao waved a hand without looking.

Lei Bo snatched the page and stamped it with a thump, without reading one word.

At the next desk, Zhong Hai looked up from his lunch box. He and Wei Kun had shared one pot of tea every day for ten years.

He read the time upside down, and then he looked quickly at his own shoes.

His phone lay face down beside the rice. It had buzzed twice since Lei Bo came in, and he had not dared to look.

Then Lei Bo ran the page down to the records room.

Ding Shouyi sat there with a stapler in his hand and a face nobody ever remembered.

He glanced at the notice, and his stapler stopped. A true hour on paper was proof that Chen Mingde had been held for eighteen hours with no decision at all. For the length of one breath he smiled.

Then he logged it, filed it, and typed one line into a phone no bigger than a matchbox: *A man wrote the true hour.*

Upstairs, Wei Kun's palms were wet.

He had done the smallest thing in the world, and he was already sure it would cost him everything.

Who, in a building like this one, would ever read that one small line?
