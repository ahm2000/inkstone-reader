# Chapter 7: Hot Water

Near midnight, in the duty room of Old Street Police Station, Wei Kun's hands were still warm.

He sat down at the duty desk and stared at them as if they belonged to somebody else.

An old man in a cell had taken a paper cup through the bars with both hands and said, "Thank you, young man."

Nobody had thanked Wei Kun for anything in this building in three years.

So why did he feel like a thief?

"Brother Wei!"

Lei Bo leaned in at the door, chewing sunflower seeds and grinning.

He was a young officer with gelled hair. His whole career rested on one skill: laughing one second before Cheng Biao did.

"I saw you on the hallway screen. Taking room service to the guest, hm?"

"He is cold," Wei Kun said. "It was a cup of water."

"Of course, of course." Lei Bo shut the door and perched on the desk. "But Director Cheng says no comforts for the Old Street holdouts. Cold nights make warm signatures. You know that."

"I know."

"I would hate for him to hear. I would never be the one to tell him." Lei Bo rubbed his thumb against his fingers. "Silence is cheap, Brother Wei. A carton of good cigarettes, eight hundred yuan, and my memory goes bad. Or it stays sharp, and the road home is long and dark. Your wife walks it to meet you sometimes, doesn't she?"

"I have a mortgage."

"Everybody has something. My cousin paid two hundred thousand for a seat on the traffic team. Happy as a pig. Me, I'm saving up. Or I could skip the saving and do Director Cheng a favor." Lei Bo smiled. "Think about it."

Two hundred thousand yuan was forty-three months of Wei Kun's pay.

Then slow, heavy boots sounded in the corridor, and Lei Bo's smile vanished as if it had never been there.

He was off the desk before the door opened.

Cheng Biao walked in with a toothpick in his teeth and the smell of liquor on his jacket.

He had come straight from the banquet at Fuyuan Garden, where Party Secretary Fan had spoken to him very softly for ten minutes.

"I never shout, Biao," Fan had said. "I only count. A chair is easy to replace." Cheng's toothpick had snapped in two.

He had been looking for somebody to blame ever since.

"Director Cheng!" Lei Bo cried. "You came yourself! I was just about to report a serious mistake!"

"Is that so?"

"Yes, Director! Brother Wei carried hot water to the Old Street guest! And I told him no! I said it plainly!"

Wei Kun stood and kept his hands at his sides.

"Director Cheng."

"I hear somebody is running a hotel in my cell."

"Director, it was boiled water. The man is fifty-two, the floor is concrete, and he has no blanket."

"Did I ask what he was?"

"No, Director."

"Then why are you answering?"

Silence.

"He is always soft, Director," Lei Bo added. "Last month he gave a drunk his own bun."

"Lei Bo."

"Yes, Director?"

"Did you say no to him, or did you only think it?"

Lei Bo's mouth opened and shut.

"In my heart, Director," he said, and went red to the ears.

Cheng Biao stepped close to Wei Kun, and the toothpick rolled to the corner of his mouth.

Then his hand came up, and crack.

Wei Kun's head turned with the blow, and his cap fell and rolled across the floor.

His cheek burned, and something worse burned behind it.

He was thirty-six years old, with twelve years on the force, and he had just been slapped in front of a boy who laughed at everything.

He felt humiliated, hot and thick, all the way up his neck.

He did not touch his face.

"Kindness is for people who can pay for it," Cheng Biao said quietly. "Do you pay Hongrui's bills, Wei? Do you pay my brother-in-law's? You're a deputy principal staff member. That's a title with nothing under it."

"Yes, Director."

"I can have you guarding the county landfill gate by Monday, and nobody would ask why. Do you know how many men would pay for your desk?"

"No, Director."

"More than you can afford. Your wife is due in the spring, I hear. Maternity wards have long lists, Wei, and I eat dinner with the men who keep the lists." Cheng Biao took out the toothpick and studied it. "Be careful on that road home."

Wei Kun's mouth went dry, and a drop of cold sweat slid under his collar.

"Tomorrow the nephew comes back to my lobby. At 7:40 you bring me the detention paper. It will say what I tell you to write. Understood?"

"Understood, Director."

"Clean up."

He walked out, and Lei Bo trotted after him, laughing at nothing.

The door shut.

Wei Kun did not move.

Then the door opened a crack, and a thin boy slipped in.

It was Zhou Pingan, the nineteen-year-old auxiliary whose cap Cheng Biao had knocked off only a few hours before.

He picked up Wei Kun's cap, brushed it clean on his sleeve, and held it out with both hands.

He said nothing.

His eyes were wet, but he stood straight, and then he hurried away.

Wei Kun looked at the cap for a long time.

His phone buzzed, and he knew who it was before he looked.

"Kun? You are still up?"

Su Min's voice was soft and sleepy, and it filled the cold room like a lamp.

"The baby kicked twice just now. I think she knows your voice. Was it a good day?"

Every night at the door she asked it.

Every night he said yes.

He looked at the red mark on his cheek in the dark window glass.

"A good day," he said.

"You are a bad liar, you know." She laughed gently. "Did you eat?"

"I ate."

"The mortgage is due on the fifth, twenty-eight hundred. I moved the money from the baby fund. There are still six thousand one hundred left, so don't worry."

"I will worry anyway."

"Kun." Her voice dropped. "Whatever it is, don't be a hero. Be a father. Come home to us."

His throat closed.

"I will," he said.

"Promise?"

"Promise."

After she hung up, Wei Kun sat very still, holding a cap he did not want to put on.

* * *

At 7:40 the next morning, Cheng Biao stood over the desk with a bun in one hand and a blank notice in the other.

"Friday, two-thirty p.m., right after the arrest. Write it."

Lei Bo hovered at his elbow with the red stamp ready.

Wei Kun took the pen.

His fingers were cold, and he remembered everything at once: the paper cup, Su Min's voice, two hundred thousand yuan, the crack of a slap.

Don't be a hero, she had said.

He was not.

He was just a man whose hand would not write a lie on a paper an old man's family would hold.

*Time of Decision: Saturday, 7:50 a.m.*

The ink was still wet when Cheng Biao's phone rang.

"Boss Dong!" Cheng Biao said, and his back bent a little as he turned toward the window.

"Good morning! Yes, yes, the nephew comes today, I have it in hand!"

"Director, the time box," Wei Kun began.

"Fine, fine." Cheng Biao waved a hand, not looking.

Lei Bo snatched the page and stamped it, thump, without reading one word.

At the next desk, Zhong Hai looked up from his lunch box.

He and Wei Kun had split one pot of tea every day for ten years.

He read the time upside down, and then he looked quickly at his own shoes.

His phone lay face-down beside the rice, and it had buzzed twice with a name he did not want to see.

Then Lei Bo ran the page down to the records room.

An old clerk named Old Ding sat there with a stapler in his hand and a face nobody ever remembered.

He glanced at the notice, and his stapler stopped.

For the length of one breath, he smiled.

Then he logged it, filed it, and typed five words into a phone no bigger than a matchbox: *A man wrote the true hour.*

Upstairs, Wei Kun's palms were wet.

He had done the smallest thing in the world, and he was already sure it would cost him everything.

Who, in a building like this one, would ever read that line?
