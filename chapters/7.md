# Chapter 7: Hot Water

Yunxi County, Old Street. Friday, 10:30 p.m.

The narrow lane lay dark, every storefront shutter pulled tight except two.

At the far end, under one bare bulb beside an ancient sewing machine, Old Yu sat hemming a trouser cuff by hand after forty-four years on this street.

Tonight, his fingers weren't really sewing. They were shaking.

He couldn't get the sight of Chen Mingde in handcuffs out of his head. He'd stood silent in his doorway while Boss Dong smiled at him, and he felt disgusted with himself! Eleven families had signed. Out of the last three, the toughest one sat in a police cell. Only two remained.

If Old Chen signs, Old Yu thought bitterly, I have no chance. If he doesn't, they'll come for me next, and who pays my granddaughter's tuition or my wife's heart medicine?

He sighed, watching the steel needle tremble. Tomorrow, he promised himself. If Old Chen is still in that cell tomorrow night, I'll sign. I'm an old man. Nobody will blame me.

Directly across the dark street, one other faint yellow light burned.

Inside the shuttered steamed-bun stall, Meng Xiaofang sat beside her sleeping four-year-old daughter, her eyes swollen red. In her dented tin box lay eighty-three crumpled yuan for rent, flour, and milk.

If Old Yu signed, she'd be the very last holdout left, and the last one standing was always the easiest to crush.

Neither neighbor knew what the other had decided. Yet behind two dim windows across the silent lane, two exhausted people made the exact same quiet vow: if Chen Mingde wasn't home by tomorrow night, they were giving up!

Yunxi County, Old Street Police Station, holding cell. Friday, 11:00 p.m.

The holding cell was small and smelled faintly of bleach and damp concrete. Inside sat a hard concrete bench, a tiny barred window high near the ceiling, and a bare bulb that never switched off.

Chen Mingde sat on the bench with his back pressed against the wall and his rough hands tucked between his knees. Nobody had given him a blanket. The autumn chill crept straight up through the concrete into his bones.

Across from him, a heavyset man brought in for a bar fight snored loudly on the opposite bench. He'd spent two nights in this room before, and before rolling over he'd given Chen Mingde some blunt advice: the food was terrible, the blankets were a myth, and the smartest thing to do was sleep.

"You should've just paid up," the man had added, before his snoring started. "Me, I decked a guy over fifty yuan and I'm out in three days. You're in here over some shop. Rich folks' problems." He said it like Chen Mingde had chosen this, the way some people always find a way to make the smaller man the fool for not giving in.

Chen Mingde couldn't sleep.

He thought of his wife, Wu Lanying, probably sitting stiffly on his brother's sofa clutching a cup of water she wouldn't drink. He thought of his daughter, Xiaoqing, laughing with her roommates in her Wuhan dormitory, completely unaware. He hadn't let anyone call her. Let the girl have one more peaceful night.

And against his will, he thought about the 456,000 yuan.

To be honest, wasn't that a pile of money? He'd never held that much cash in his entire life. If he signed tomorrow morning, he'd walk straight out the front door, and his wife would burst into tears of relief. Who could blame an aging hardware seller for wanting to go home?

Yet that little corner shop was twenty-two years of his life! It was opening the rolling shutter at seven every morning and locking it at nine every night. It was every hand-labeled screw drawer, the red-and-white sign he'd painted himself, and every neighbor on Old Street saying, "Go ask Old Chen, he'll have it." If he sold it for less than half its worth, what would he be? Not a hardware man. Not anything.

"Don't pull back when the fish pulls," he whispered softly to the blank wall. "Wait."

Slow, heavy footsteps echoed down the concrete hallway.

They stopped right outside the iron bars. Chen Mingde looked up and saw a round-faced police officer with red, tired eyes standing there, holding a steaming paper cup.

Wei Kun stood silently for several seconds, almost as if he'd forgotten why he walked down here.

"Uncle," he said at last, keeping his voice low enough that the hallway camera couldn't pick it up. "Would you like some hot water?"

Chen Mingde reached through the bars and took the cup with both hands. It was only plain boiled water, yet the moment the warmth hit his palms, his fingers stopped shaking.

"Thank you, young man."

Wei Kun gave one stiff nod without another word and walked briskly back down the hall, moving like a man who'd just broken a rule.

Chen Mingde pressed the warm cup against his chest and closed his eyes. He couldn't explain why, yet a quiet certainty settled over him. Somebody was already on the way. His older brother, of course. And right behind his brother, somewhere out there in the night, was the boy who had learned never to yank too hard on a fishing line.
