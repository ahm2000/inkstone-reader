# Chapter 52: The Empty Chairs

By five thirty on Monday morning, the outer waiting hall of the Central Compound was thick with cigarette smoke and quiet fear.

More than sixty senior officials sat on dark benches or stood near brass ashtrays along the wall. Every personal phone had been taken at the inner gate. Without screens or aides to hide behind, men who gave orders to millions of workers were left staring across the room at one another.

What froze the air was not who had shown up. It was who had not.

Along the inner seating table arranged by rank, more than a dozen chairs stood completely empty. Some still had untouched white teacups in front of them. Others had no nameplates at all, leaving pale empty spots on the dark wood where aides had removed them minutes earlier.

Near the second ash stand, Deputy Finance Minister Pei Rongsheng tapped ash from a cigarette that had burned halfway down after only two drags. He kept his left hand buried in his coat pocket to hide a slight shake.

"Count the second row again," Pei Rongsheng murmured without moving his lips much, his eyes fixed across the hall. "Tell me my eyes are playing tricks on me, Han Qingshan."

Beside him, Supreme People's Court Vice President Han Qingshan rolled an unlit cigarette between his fingers. His face stayed calm, though a deep line formed between his eyebrows.

"Your eyes are fine, Pei Rongsheng," Han Qingshan said quietly. "Four seats from Public Security. Three from State Security. Two from the Supreme Procuratorate. Two from banking and treasury. And three military chairs on the east side."

"Fourteen," Pei Rongsheng whispered, his throat bobbing as he swallowed. "Fourteen vice-minister and bureau chairs empty at a Top Red Emergency summons. Nobody sleeps through a red-phone call."

"Maybe they are already inside the main hall," Han Qingshan said smoothly, though his eyes flicked sideways to study Pei Rongsheng's face. "You look awful pale, Old Pei. Did the Treasury's southern city bond review run into trouble last week?"

Pei Rongsheng whipped his head around, his eyes flashing. "Do not point that judge's finger at me, Han Qingshan. My signatures on the southern bond deals passed three reviews. If you want to look for trouble, why is your own Third Criminal Division chief missing from the court bench?"

Before Han Qingshan could fire back, a low, dry laugh cut between them.

Theater Political Commissar Major General Du Zhenping stepped closer, blowing blue smoke toward the ceiling. His olive uniform was neatly pressed, yet dark circles under his eyes showed he had not slept.

"Save your breath, both of you," Du Zhenping said with a blunt military edge. "Ten military offices do not get dragged through the West Gate at five in the morning over a city bond review or a court clerk taking bribes."

Pei Rongsheng stiffened. "Then tell us, Commissar Du. You have ears inside Joint Staff. What happened between midnight and five o'clock?"

Du Zhenping looked down at his glowing cigarette, then looked slowly at both men. "At two in the morning, three air-defense zones around Beijing went into quiet lockdown for forty minutes. No drills were on the schedule. No foreign planes were near the area. That only happens when something moves inside the capital that does not answer to regional army command."

Han Qingshan stopped rolling his cigarette. "Moves inside the capital," he repeated softly. "A night arrest operation?"

"Not a normal one," Du Zhenping corrected, lowering his voice. "When Public Security or Discipline Inspection grabs a sitting director, the department head gets a polite call before sunrise. Look over there."

Du Zhenping nodded toward the far corner. Vice Minister of Public Security Shen Weiguo stood alone by a pillar, lighting a fresh cigarette right off his old one. His jaw was locked tight as he stared at the empty spot where Director Ma Zhenyu's seat belonged.

"Shen Weiguo has no idea where his own superiors are," Du Zhenping said flatly. "Nobody in this room knew a thing until five o'clock."

Silence settled over the three men. Across the hall, quiet talk stopped whenever polished shoes stepped past in the hallway outside. Two Organization Department officials sat stiffly with hands folded on their knees, refusing to meet anyone's eyes.

Pei Rongsheng took a hard drag until the glowing tip nearly touched his filter, then jammed it into the brass tray. His calm finally cracked.

"I tried calling He Guoliang at five oh eight," Pei Rongsheng admitted in a tight whisper, glancing around nervously. "Just to ask if the southern committee had a plan for today. The line was dead. Not busy. Cut off at the phone exchange."

Han Qingshan's thin half-smile vanished. He stepped half a pace back, as if Pei Rongsheng's coat might stain his own. "Why on earth would you call He Guoliang after a red-level summons?"

"I work with his office every three months!" Pei Rongsheng hissed back, sweat forming on his forehead. "You signed two joint court memos with Lin Zhengde last month, Han Qingshan. Do not pretend you never sat at their table."

"Official memos are signed in daylight through the office," Han Qingshan said icily, his knuckles white around his unlit cigarette. "Private calls at five in the morning are your own business."

"Both of you quiet down," Du Zhenping warned softly, his back snapping straight. "Look at the table."

An aide from the Central Compound office had stepped quietly out of the side hallway. Without saying a word or looking at anyone, the young man walked to the second row, picked up a dark wooden nameplate with a Deputy Minister of Justice's name on it, turned it face down against his chest, and walked back out.

Nobody coughed. Nobody clicked a lighter. Thin lines of smoke rose straight toward the ceiling in dead silence.

Thirteen empty spaces now stared back at them. No, fourteen including the one just cleared, and who knew how many more were coming.

Pei Rongsheng's fingers twitched inside his coat pocket as he watched Han Qingshan's mouth for any sign of nerves. Han Qingshan watched Du Zhenping, trying to guess if the general knew whose names were next. Du Zhenping kept his face hard as stone, though a vein pulsed steadily at his temple.

Every older official in the hall had spent thirty years surviving political storms. None of them had ever waited outside a room where the storm was already over before the doors even opened.

At five forty-five, the brass locks on the Main Security Hall double doors clicked open with a sharp metallic sound.

Central Commission for Discipline Inspection Secretary Gao Zhengyi stepped into the doorway. His eyes were bloodshot, his cheeks were gray, and his collar was crooked from a long, sleepless night. He did not say hello.

"Leave your coats and cigarettes outside," Gao Zhengyi ordered, his voice rough. "Every department head and senior deputy inside the Main Security Hall. Now."

Chairs scraped harshly across the floor. Nobody spoke another word as sixty of the country's highest officials walked grimly inside.
