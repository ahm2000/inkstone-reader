# Chapter 52: The Empty Chairs

By five thirty on Monday morning, the outer waiting hall of the Central Compound was thick with cigarette smoke and unspoken dread.

Over sixty senior officials sat on dark benches or stood clustered near brass ashtrays along the wall. Every personal phone had been surrendered at the inner gate. Without screens or aides to shield them, men who commanded millions of subordinates were left staring across the hall at one another.

What froze the air was not who had arrived. It was who had not.

Along the inner protocol table set in strict rank order, more than a dozen seats stood starkly empty. Some held untouched porcelain teacups. Others had no nameplates at all, leaving faint pale rectangles where aides had removed them minutes earlier.

Near the second ash stand, Deputy Finance Minister Pei Rongsheng tapped ash from a cigarette that had burned halfway down after only two drags. His left hand stayed buried in his coat pocket to hide a faint tremor.

"Count the second row again," Pei Rongsheng murmured without moving his lips much, his eyes fixed across the hall. "Tell me my eyesight is failing, Han Qingshan."

Beside him, Supreme People's Court Vice President Han Qingshan rolled an unlit cigarette between his fingers. His expression stayed composed, though a tight crease sat between his eyebrows.

"You are counting fine, Pei Rongsheng," Han Qingshan said quietly. "Four seats from Public Security. Three from State Security. Two from the Supreme Procuratorate. Two from banking and treasury regulation. And three military chairs on the east side."

"Fourteen," Pei Rongsheng whispered, his throat bobbing. "Fourteen vice-ministerial and bureau seats empty at a Grade-One Red Mobilization. Nobody sleeps through a red-line summons."

"Perhaps they are already inside the main hall," Han Qingshan suggested smoothly, though his eyes flicked sideways to measure Pei Rongsheng's face. "You look unusually pale, Old Pei. Did the Treasury's southern municipal bond audit run into trouble last week?"

Pei Rongsheng snapped his head around, eyes flashing defensively. "Do not point that judicial finger at me, Han Qingshan. My signatures on the southern bond tranches passed three internal reviews. If you want trouble, why is your own Third Criminal Division chief missing from the judicial bench?"

Before Han Qingshan could retort, a low, dry chuckle cut between them.

Theater Political Commissar Major General Du Zhenping stepped closer, exhaling blue smoke toward the ceiling. His olive uniform was razor-pressed, yet dark hollows beneath his eyes betrayed a sleepless night.

"Save your breath, both of you," Du Zhenping said with blunt military edge. "Ten military directorates do not get dragged through the West Gate at five in the morning over a municipal bond audit or a court clerk taking bribes."

Pei Rongsheng stiffened. "Then enlighten us, Commissar Du. You have ears inside Joint Staff. What happened between midnight and five o'clock?"

Du Zhenping studied his glowing cigarette, then looked slowly at both men. "At two in the morning, three air-defense corridors around the capital shifted to silent lockdown for forty minutes. No drills logged. No foreign aircraft near the zone. That only happens when something moves inside the capital that does not answer to theater command."

Han Qingshan stopped rolling his cigarette. "Moves inside the capital," he repeated softly. "An internal arrest operation?"

"Not an ordinary one," Du Zhenping corrected, dropping his voice. "When Public Security or Discipline Inspection grabs a sitting director, the department head gets a courtesy call before sunrise. Look over there."

Du Zhenping jerked his chin toward the far corner. Vice Minister of Public Security Shen Weiguo stood alone by a pillar, lighting a fresh cigarette directly off his previous butt, his jaw locked as he stared at the blank space where Director Ma Zhenyu's seat belonged.

"Shen Weiguo does not know where his own superiors are," Du Zhenping said flatly. "Nobody in this room knew anything until five o'clock."

Silence settled over the three men. Across the hall, murmurs died whenever polished shoes crossed the corridor outside. Two Organization Department liaisons sat stiffly with hands folded on their knees, avoiding every glance.

Pei Rongsheng dragged hard until the ember nearly touched his filter, then jammed it into the brass tray. His composure cracked.

"I tried calling He Guoliang at five oh eight," Pei Rongsheng admitted in a tight whisper, glancing around nervously. "Just to ask if the southern committee had an agenda. The line was dead. Not busy. Disconnected at the exchange."

Han Qingshan's forced half-smile vanished. He stepped half a pace back, as if Pei Rongsheng's sleeve carried soot. "Why on earth would you dial He Guoliang after a red-level summons?"

"I work with his office every quarter!" Pei Rongsheng hissed back, sweat beading across his forehead. "You signed two joint judicial memos with Lin Zhengde last month, Han Qingshan. Do not pretend you never sat at their table."

"Official memos are signed in daylight through the secretariat," Han Qingshan said icily, knuckles white around his unlit cigarette. "Private calls at five in the morning are your own affair."

"Both of you quiet down," Du Zhenping warned softly, his posture snapping rigid. "Look at the table."

An aide from the Central Compound Secretariat had stepped silently from the side corridor. Without speaking or meeting a single gaze, the young man walked to the second row, lifted a dark wooden nameplate bearing a Deputy Minister of Justice's name, turned it face down against his chest, and walked back out.

Nobody coughed. Nobody clicked a lighter. Thin ribbons of smoke rose straight toward the ceiling in dead stillness.

Thirteen empty spaces now stared back at them.

Pei Rongsheng's fingers twitched inside his pocket as he searched Han Qingshan's mouth for any tremor. Han Qingshan watched Du Zhenping, trying to guess whether the military man knew whose names were next. Du Zhenping kept his face carved like granite, though the vein at his temple pulsed visibly.

Every veteran in the hall had spent thirty years surviving political storms. None had ever waited outside a room where the storm had already finished before the doors opened.

At five forty-five, the brass latches on the Main Security Hall double doors clicked open with a sharp metallic echo.

Central Commission for Discipline Inspection Secretary Gao Zhengyi stepped across the threshold, eyes bloodshot, cheeks gray, collar crooked from a sleepless night. He offered no greeting.

"Leave your coats and cigarettes outside," Gao Zhengyi ordered, his voice raw. "Every department head and ranking deputy inside the Main Security Hall. Now."

Chairs scraped harshly across the floor. No one spoke another word as sixty of the country's highest officials filed grimly inside.
