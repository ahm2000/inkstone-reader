# Chapter 18: The Recount

The next morning, five minutes before the recount, Zhang Pengfei stood beside a stack of printouts three inches thick. He had dark circles under his eyes from checking numbers until two in the morning.

"Watch," he whispered to Sister Pan. "He leaves at five every day, so his overtime is zero. When General Manager Sun reads my nineteen nights, first place comes back to me."

The glass doors swung open. Manager Hu Dewei held one door, sweating, and General Manager Sun Haoran walked through.

Thirty chairs scraped, and thirty people stood up.

"Good morning, General Manager Sun!"

"Sit, please." Sun waved one hand, and nobody sat.

He did not look at the whiteboard or the paper tower. He looked at a scratched green thermos next to Chen Xuan's keyboard, the way a man looks at a safe.

"General Manager!" Zhang Pengfei carried his stack forward in both arms. "The full recount! Page one shows nineteen late nights for me, and zero overtime for Supervisor Chen."

Sun turned a page, then another, then let the stack lean against Hu's arm.

"Pengfei, who touched the port invoices before Supervisor Chen?"

"Four of us, sir. But the audit was his."

"Then read me the initials on the second desk. Blue ink, on the second container."

Zhang looked down, and the blood drained out of his face. Two blue letters sat on that page: Z.P.

"It passed your desk, and you signed it," said Sun, quite gently. "On Friday morning, Supervisor Chen found the double storage fee. Yesterday the port wired fourteen thousand yuan back to us."

"I didn't know about the bonus rule!" Zhang burst out, turning to his boss. "Manager Hu, you never told me!"

"I told you to read page twelve!" Hu Dewei snapped. "Three times! You said you knew it by heart!"

"You said it was nothing!"

"Enough. Company rule: recover more than ten thousand yuan, and the finder gets 1.5 bonus points. Manager Hu, the board."

Hu wiped out 93.6 and wrote: Chen Xuan, 95.1.

That was 95.1 against 93.4. The gap was now eight times bigger.

Zhang swayed on his feet. He had stayed up all night to pull a mask off Chen Xuan, and he had signed his own name on the proof.

"I only read page twelve," said Chen Xuan, looking up from his bun. "Anyone can."

It was flat and calm, and it landed on Zhang like a slap.

Sun walked to the corner desk. The man who fired people twice a year bent his back a little, and a clerk in the second row whispered, "Is he going to bow?"

"Supervisor Chen. Manager Hu says your tea is very fragrant. Half a cup, if you can spare it."

"Morning, General Manager. Sure." Chen Xuan poured half a paper cup.

Sun held it in both hands, smelled the steam, and drank. For three long seconds he did not move.

He had smelled this once, from a tiny jar his old mentor brought from the provincial compound, where only twenty jin were picked each spring. And here it sat in a ten-yuan thermos beside a half-eaten bun.

"Who is this Uncle Hao?" Sun said softly. "A man who sells that for thirty yuan has either no sense of price or a very great sense of people."

"He's a nice man," said Chen Xuan. "His shop never has customers." He dug in his canvas bag and set a paper packet on the desk. "A spare. Thirty yuan. That's what he charged me."

Sun's heart thumped. He would have paid a month's salary for that packet, and Chen Xuan offered it like a spare umbrella. How deep did this young man go?

Sun took a crisp bill out of his wallet and handed it over with both hands, the way you hand a gift to an elder. Chen Xuan said, "Thanks," and took it like a man selling vegetables.

"Everyone!" Sun turned around, beaming. "Supervisor Chen came first, and he saved us fourteen thousand. This Friday, the company takes the whole Operations Department to dinner at the Yunhai Grand Hotel!"

The floor exploded.

"One table is over twenty thousand yuan. That's almost two months of Chen's pay!"

"Thank you, General Manager Sun! Thank you, Supervisor Chen!"

Chen Xuan lifted a hand to say it was nothing, and put it over his face when nobody saw.

Then he noticed that his chair, which had squeaked for two years, made no sound at all.

By the mop bucket, Auntie Lu hummed and did not look at him.

"Auntie Lu. Did you oil this?"

"A tea egg is worth a little oil," she said. "Sit, sit."

"Thanks. Really."

A man had paid him thirty yuan for a bag of leftovers, and the whole room was clapping as though he had cured a disease.

At his desk, Zhang Pengfei pressed his fingernails into his palm.

"You'll remember that dinner longer than the tea, Chen," he whispered to his own cuff. He had a cousin, the cousin had a friend, and the friend had a card.

He texted under his desk.

*Brother Fang, it's Pengfei. Can I borrow your Yunhai Silver card Friday night? Company dinner. I'll take care of it.*

The answer came in half a minute.

*Fang Zihao: Silver costs 80,000 a year to hold. That's almost seven months of your pay. Scratch it and my father will look at your wife's uncle's permits. Tell the front desk it's mine. Maybe I'll drop by to watch.*

Zhang smiled for the first time that morning, and it was not a nice smile.

* * *

At lunch, Chen Xuan was holding a twelve-yuan tray when his phone rang.

"Xuan!" Chen Mingde's voice shook with joy, so hard that the phone crackled. "The provincial people came to Old Street yesterday. They froze all eleven shop contracts on the spot! If any owner still wants to sell, the county must pay the full eight thousand per square meter, not 3,800!"

"Good news, Second Uncle."

"My shop is sixty square meters, Xuan. That gap is two hundred fifty-two thousand yuan! I sat down on a box of nails and your aunt had to fan me!"

That was twenty-one months of Chen Xuan's pay, pulled out of a thief's pocket and handed to a hardware man.

"And Wei Kun!" Chen Mingde could hardly breathe. "The one who brought me hot water! The county named him Acting Deputy Chief of Old Street Station this morning. Instructor Qiu Guoqiang is back and runs the station again. Wei Kun came by for a box of nails, glowing, and I wouldn't take his money!"

Chen Xuan put down his chopsticks. His throat was so tight that he looked at the canteen's back door so no one could see his eyes.

"I'm glad," he said at last. "Really glad."

"Heaven has eyes, Xuan! Pure luck, that they came a day early!"

"Hmm," said Chen Xuan, rubbing his forehead.

Luck, again. He had asked for a receipt and a front door, and half of Yunxi had decided heaven did the rest.

He hung up and found the tofu had gone cold. He ate it anyway.

* * *

That night in the quiet courtyard, Chief An Zhiyuan kept two ledgers in a locked drawer.

One listed what the Boss's people owed him, a column of names, each with a score that had never once fallen below one hundred.

The second listed what the Boss was owed. Its page was empty, because the Boss never kept score.

"Chief," said Captain Tang, bringing in a cup. "People's Hospital reports. Tiantian has the fourth-floor bed, and the operation is Thursday, paid by a 'charity fund' that doesn't exist."

"And the father?"

"On Sunday he walked to Wei Kun's window and said everything himself, before anyone asked. Wei Kun didn't forgive him. He only said: Go sit with your son."

Chief An wrote one line, and under it a second: *The law will weigh the help of a witness. The bed stays.*

"One thing more," said Deputy Pei. "Fourteen oiled a chair for a tea egg. And the Boss's company has a Friday dinner booked for twenty. At the Yunhai Grand Hotel."

Chief An's pen stopped.

Then, for the first time all week, the old man smiled, a small, dry smile, like a man whose old bet had just come due.

"The Yunhai," he said. "Of all the hotels in the city. Pull Friday's guest list, and let nobody tell him where he is eating."

"Chief?"

"Three years ago the holding company's trustee told the manager there: One day a young man will put this card on your counter. Serve him, ask nothing, tell nobody."

Captain Tang frowned.

"And the manager?"

"Is about to have the worst and best Friday of his life," said Chief An, and shut the drawer.
